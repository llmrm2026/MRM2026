# Management of Chronic Kidney Disease in Adults (2nd Edition, 2018): 50 MCQs

**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia (MaHTAS), *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Chronic Kidney Disease in Adults*, Second Edition (2018).
**Format:** 50 single-best-answer questions, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style, written with layered reasoning (recognise, interpret, apply, exclude). Each question has the key, an explanation of the key and the distractors, a teaching point and a CPG reference.
**Scope:** Risk factors and screening, proteinuria and haematuria, renal function and classification, blood pressure and RAS blockade, glycaemic and dietary measures, CV risk, nephrotoxins, pregnancy, referral, anaemia and CKD-MBD, drug dosing.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from the CPG text. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

## Section 1: Risk Factors and Screening

### Q1
**Topic:** Screening interval in hypertension

A 52-year-old woman has had essential hypertension for 6 years, controlled on amlodipine. She has no diabetes. Her eGFR last year was 88 ml/min/1.73 m2 and urine dipstick was negative. She asks how often her kidneys need to be checked.

**How often should she be screened for chronic kidney disease?**

A. Every 3 years
B. Every 6 months
C. Only if she develops diabetes
D. At least yearly

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Recommendation 1 states that patients with diabetes mellitus and/or hypertension should be screened at least yearly for CKD. Hypertension may be a cause or consequence of CKD and may accelerate progression to ESRD. A lengthens the interval, C ignores hypertension as an independent indication, and B is more frequent than the CPG requires for a patient with normal results.

**Teaching point:** Diabetes and hypertension are the two conditions that mandate at least yearly CKD screening.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2(B); Recommendation 1; Algorithm 2.

### Q2
**Topic:** When to start screening in type 1 diabetes

A 24-year-old man was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes 3 years ago. He does not smoke, is normotensive and has a normal lipid profile. He has had no urine testing for kidney disease.

**According to the CPG algorithm, when should urine screening for diabetic kidney disease begin?**

A. Immediately, at this visit
B. After 10 years of diabetes
C. Only once he develops hypertension
D. After 5 years of diabetes, or earlier if other cardiovascular risk factors are present

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Algorithm 1 advises urine dipstick screening in type 1 diabetes after 5 years of diabetes, or earlier in the presence of other cardiovascular risk factors. In type 2 diabetes, screening starts at the time of diagnosis, which is why A is the trap (it applies the type 2 rule). B is too late and C waits for a complication.

**Teaching point:** Type 2 diabetes: screen at diagnosis. Type 1 diabetes: screen after 5 years, or earlier with CV risk factors.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 1.

### Q3
**Topic:** Proton pump inhibitors as a risk factor

A 61-year-old man with gastro-oesophageal reflux has taken omeprazole 40 mg daily for 8 years without review. His eGFR has fallen from 84 to 66 ml/min/1.73 m2 over this time. He also takes amlodipine.

**Which statement about his acid-suppression therapy best reflects the CPG?**

A. Long-term PPI use is associated with developing and progressing CKD, and should be avoided unless clearly indicated
B. PPIs are renally safe, and the decline must be due to amlodipine
C. H2-receptor antagonists carry the same dose-dependent CKD risk as PPIs
D. The PPI dose should simply be doubled because reflux causes CKD

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** PPI use significantly increases the risk of developing CKD (RR/OR 1.10 to 1.50) and of CKD progression (HR 1.26 to 1.32), and the risk correlates with cumulative dose. The CPG advises avoiding long-term PPI use unless there is a clear indication. B is wrong because PPIs are a recognised risk. C is wrong because the association was not evident with cumulative H2-blocker dose. D has no basis.

**Teaching point:** Review long-term PPIs in patients at risk of CKD; the risk is dose-related and not seen with H2-blockers.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2(G); Section 4.7(iii).

### Q4
**Topic:** Risk factors for which screening may be considered

A primary care team audits which non-diabetic, non-hypertensive patients should be offered CKD screening.

**Which patient is listed in the CPG as one for whom screening may be considered?**

A. A 30-year-old woman with migraine using occasional paracetamol
B. A 45-year-old man with well-controlled asthma
C. A 35-year-old woman with iron-deficiency anaemia from menorrhagia
D. A 58-year-old man with gout

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Screening may be considered in patients with age >65, obesity, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, nephrotoxic drugs or long-term PPIs/analgesics, family history of CKD, gout, multisystem disease such as SLE, structural renal tract disease, calculi or prostatic hypertrophy, and incidental haematuria or proteinuria. Gout (and asymptomatic hyperuricaemia) is associated with CKD. A, B and C are not listed indications.

**Teaching point:** Gout is one of the listed triggers for CKD screening in patients without diabetes or hypertension.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2(I); Recommendation 1.

## Section 2: Proteinuria and Haematuria

### Q5
**Topic:** Screening test in diabetes when dipstick is negative

A 49-year-old woman has had type 2 diabetes for 4 years. She is on metformin. A urine dipstick for protein is negative.

**What is the most appropriate next screening test for kidney disease?**

A. Albumin:creatinine ratio on an early morning spot urine sample
B. 24-hour urine total protein
C. Repeat urine dipstick in 5 years
D. Renal ultrasound

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Recommendation 2 states that in patients with diabetes, an albumin:creatinine ratio on an early morning spot urine should be performed to screen for microalbuminuria if the dipstick for protein is negative. The early morning sample minimises the effect of posture and exercise. B is used for quantifying established proteinuria. C misses early albuminuria. D is not a screening test for albuminuria.

**Teaching point:** In diabetes, a negative dipstick is not the end of screening: follow it with an early morning urine ACR.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1.1; Recommendation 2; Algorithm 1.

### Q6
**Topic:** Confirming microalbuminuria

A 57-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has an early morning urine ACR of 6 mg/mmol. He has no symptoms of urinary infection and no heart failure.

**How should the diagnosis of diabetic kidney disease be established?**

A. The single result is diagnostic; start ACE inhibitor and stop monitoring
B. Retest twice over 3 to 6 months; diabetic kidney disease is established if 2 of 3 tests are positive
C. Arrange a renal biopsy to confirm diabetic nephropathy
D. Repeat the ACR only if his eGFR falls below 60 ml/min/1.73 m2

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Algorithm 1 advises that a positive microalbuminuria screen is retested twice in 3 to 6 months after excluding other causes such as UTI and heart failure. If 2 of 3 tests are positive, diabetic kidney disease is established, then microalbuminuria is quantified and followed 3- to 6-monthly. A ignores variability in albumin excretion. C is unnecessary. D ignores albuminuria as a marker of kidney damage regardless of eGFR.

**Teaching point:** Albuminuria is confirmed on 2 of 3 samples over 3 to 6 months.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 1; Section 3.1.1.

### Q7
**Topic:** Transient proteinuria

A 33-year-old woman attends a well-person check while menstruating. She ran a half-marathon the previous day. Urine dipstick shows protein 1+ and blood 1+. She has no history of kidney disease and her blood pressure is 118/74 mmHg.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Start an ACE inhibitor for proteinuria
B. Refer urgently to a nephrologist
C. Repeat the urinalysis at a later date, away from menstruation and strenuous exercise
D. Arrange a 24-hour urine protein collection the same day

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Strenuous exercise and menstruation both increase urinary protein excretion (Table 1), and menstruation and vigorous exercise are listed causes of haematuria in Algorithm 3. Proteinuria should be confirmed by a repeat test, and a positive dipstick for blood on two of three occasions is needed before full microscopy. A and B act on a result that is likely transient. D would be confounded by the same factors.

**Teaching point:** Exclude transient causes (exercise, menstruation, fever, UTI) before labelling proteinuria or haematuria.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1.1, Table 1; Section 3.1.2; Algorithm 3.

### Q8
**Topic:** Drugs that lower measured proteinuria

A 63-year-old man with non-diabetic CKD had a 24-hour urine protein of 1.4 g/day before treatment. He was started on ramipril and also takes naproxen for knee pain. Repeat 24-hour protein is 0.6 g/day.

**Which statement best explains why this reduction must be interpreted with caution?**

A. Naproxen increases protein excretion, so the true value is higher still
B. Ramipril has no effect on protein excretion
C. A 24-hour collection overestimates protein in men
D. Both ACE inhibitors and NSAIDs decrease urinary protein excretion

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Table 1 lists ACEi/ARB and NSAIDs as factors that decrease urinary protein excretion. The fall reflects the intended ACEi effect, but the NSAID also lowers the reading while being nephrotoxic, and NSAIDs should be avoided in CKD. A reverses the direction for NSAIDs. B is wrong because ACEi/ARB reduce proteinuria. C has no basis.

**Teaching point:** ACEi/ARB and NSAIDs both reduce measured protein excretion; only one of them is renoprotective.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1.1, Table 1; Section 4.7(ii).

### Q9
**Topic:** Albuminuria category

A 46-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes has a confirmed early morning urine ACR of 42 mg/mmol on repeated testing.

**Which KDIGO albuminuria category does she belong to?**

A. A1, normal to mildly increased
B. A2, moderately increased
C. A3, severely increased
D. Cannot be classified without a 24-hour collection

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** In Table 4, A1 is ACR <3 mg/mmol, A2 is 3 to 30 mg/mmol and A3 is >30 mg/mmol (AER >300 mg/24 hours). An ACR of 42 mg/mmol is A3. D is wrong because ACR on a spot sample is an accepted basis for categorisation. The CPG discourages the term "microalbuminuria" in favour of A1-A3.

**Teaching point:** ACR thresholds: <3 (A1), 3-30 (A2), >30 mg/mmol (A3).

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.4(c), Table 4.

### Q10
**Topic:** Quantifying proteinuria

A 38-year-old man has 2+ protein on dipstick on two separate occasions. Renal function is normal. The clinician wants to quantify his proteinuria.

**Which test does the CPG recommend for protein quantification?**

A. 24-hour urine collection, with early morning urine protein:creatinine ratio as an alternative
B. Repeat dipstick reading graded from trace to 3+
C. Serum albumin level
D. Urine microscopy for casts

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG states that a 24-hour urine collection should be used for protein quantification, and an early morning uPCR can be used as an alternative. A dipstick only screens. Serum albumin and microscopy do not quantify urinary protein.

**Teaching point:** Dipstick screens; 24-hour collection (or uPCR) quantifies.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1.1, Table 2.

### Q11
**Topic:** Microscopic haematuria with infection

A 44-year-old woman has dysuria and frequency. Dipstick shows blood 2+ and leucocytes 2+. Urine culture grows E. coli and she is treated with an appropriate antibiotic.

**When should microscopic haematuria be re-assessed?**

A. Immediately after the first antibiotic dose
B. With follow-up urinalysis six weeks after completion of therapy
C. Only if she develops visible haematuria
D. After 12 months

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Algorithm 3 states that when haematuria is accompanied by signs and symptoms of UTI, the infection is treated and resolution of microscopic haematuria is confirmed with follow-up urinalysis six weeks after completion of therapy. If haematuria resolves, she can be discharged. A is too early, and C and D could miss persistent pathology.

**Teaching point:** Recheck haematuria six weeks after treating a UTI before deciding whether it needs investigation.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 3; Section 3.1.2.

### Q12
**Topic:** Glomerular versus non-glomerular haematuria

A 62-year-old male former smoker has persistent microscopic haematuria on three occasions with no infection. Urine microscopy shows isomorphic red cells and no casts. There is no proteinuria and his creatinine is normal.

**Where should he be referred?**

A. Nephrologist
B. No referral; repeat in one year
C. Urologist
D. Dietitian

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Algorithm 3 directs haematuria with evidence of glomerular disease (proteinuria, red cell casts, dysmorphic red cells, elevated creatinine) to a nephrologist, and haematuria without these to a urologist. Non-glomerular haematuria warrants urological evaluation, which matters here given possible malignancy. A is for glomerular features. B delays investigation of persistent haematuria.

**Teaching point:** Dysmorphic cells, casts, proteinuria or raised creatinine point to the nephrologist; otherwise the urologist.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1.2; Algorithm 3.

## Section 3: Renal Function and Classification

### Q13
**Topic:** Limits of serum creatinine

A 72-year-old woman weighing 45 kg has a serum creatinine of 95 umol/L, within the laboratory reference range. Her family is reassured that her kidneys are normal.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. A normal creatinine excludes CKD in older women
B. Creatinine is more sensitive than eGFR for early CKD
C. Creatinine is unaffected by muscle mass or age
D. Creatinine rises only after about 50% decline in GFR, so it should not be used alone; eGFR should be calculated

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Serum creatinine is not sensitive for early CKD because it is affected by age, gender, ethnicity, muscle mass and diet, and only rises after a 50% decline of eGFR. Recommendation 3 states that creatinine should not be used alone. A small, older woman with low muscle mass can have a "normal" creatinine with a markedly reduced GFR. A, B and C contradict this.

**Teaching point:** A "normal" creatinine can hide a halved GFR, especially in small, elderly women.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2, Figure 1; Recommendation 3.

### Q14
**Topic:** Preferred eGFR equation

A laboratory is updating how it reports renal function for adult outpatients.

**Which equation does the CPG recommend for estimating GFR?**

A. MDRD equation
B. Cockcroft-Gault creatinine clearance
C. 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation
D. Serum urea-based estimate

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Recommendation 3 recommends the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. It is superior to MDRD especially at eGFR >60, and a Malaysian study showed better accuracy than MDRD below 60 and at or above 90 ml/min/1.73 m2. Cockcroft-Gault has traditionally been used for drug dosing rather than staging. Urea is not used to estimate GFR.

**Teaching point:** Stage CKD with CKD-EPI 2009; Cockcroft-Gault is the traditional drug-dosing formula.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2; Recommendation 3.

### Q15
**Topic:** Role of cystatin C

A 41-year-old male bodybuilder has an eGFR of 52 ml/min/1.73 m2 on two occasions 4 months apart. Urine ACR is normal, urine microscopy is bland and renal ultrasound is normal. He takes creatine supplements.

**Which test is most appropriate to confirm or refute CKD?**

A. Renal biopsy
B. Serum cystatin C to estimate GFR
C. Repeat creatinine after a high-protein meal
D. Isotope renogram

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Cystatin C is independent of muscle mass, age, sex and diet, and may be used to confirm CKD in adults with eGFR 45-59 ml/min/1.73 m2 and no other markers of kidney damage. Combining creatinine and cystatin C is more accurate than either alone. High muscle mass and creatine intake raise creatinine. A is invasive and not indicated. C would worsen the confounding. D is not suggested for this purpose.

**Teaching point:** Use cystatin C to confirm CKD when eGFR is 45-59 with no other kidney damage marker.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2.

### Q16
**Topic:** Definition of CKD and staging

A 66-year-old woman has an eGFR of 38 ml/min/1.73 m2 on two occasions 5 months apart. Her urine ACR is 1.8 mg/mmol on repeated testing.

**How should her CKD be categorised?**

A. G3a A1
B. G3b A2
C. G4 A1
D. G3b A1

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** CKD is defined as eGFR <60 for more than 3 months, which she meets. In Table 3, G3a is 45-59, G3b is 30-44 and G4 is 15-29, so 38 is G3b. An ACR <3 mg/mmol is A1. A uses the wrong GFR band, B the wrong albuminuria band, and C confuses G3b with G4.

**Teaching point:** G3a 45-59, G3b 30-44, G4 15-29; classify by Cause, GFR and Albuminuria (CGA).

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.4, Tables 3 and 4; Recommendation 4.

### Q17
**Topic:** Chronicity criterion

A 54-year-old man has an eGFR of 54 ml/min/1.73 m2 measured 3 days after a diarrhoeal illness. A test 6 months earlier showed 92 ml/min/1.73 m2. Urinalysis is normal.

**Which is the most appropriate interpretation?**

A. CKD cannot be diagnosed yet; the reduced eGFR must persist for more than 3 months
B. He has CKD stage G3a
C. He has CKD stage G2
D. He has end-stage kidney disease

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** CKD requires eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m2, or a marker of kidney damage, present for more than 3 months. A single low value shortly after volume depletion may reflect acute kidney injury. B labels CKD too early. C misreads the number. D has no basis.

**Teaching point:** The 3-month rule separates CKD from a transient or acute fall in GFR.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.4.

### Q18
**Topic:** Markers of kidney damage

A 29-year-old woman has an eGFR of 104 ml/min/1.73 m2. Over 6 months, several findings are recorded.

**Which finding, if persistent for more than 3 months, alone establishes CKD?**

A. Serum uric acid of 420 umol/L
B. Urine ACR of 8 mg/mmol
C. Body mass index of 33 kg/m2
D. Fasting glucose of 6.4 mmol/L

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Markers of kidney damage include albuminuria (ACR ≥3 mg/mmol or AER ≥30 mg/24 h), urine sediment abnormalities, tubular disorders, histological or imaging abnormalities and a history of transplantation. Persistent albuminuria defines CKD even with a normal eGFR (stage G1). A, C and D are risk factors, not markers of kidney damage.

**Teaching point:** CKD can be present with a normal eGFR if a damage marker, such as ACR ≥3 mg/mmol, persists.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.4.

### Q19
**Topic:** Indications for renal ultrasound

A 50-year-old woman has stable CKD G3a. Her eGFR has fallen from 58 to 51 ml/min/1.73 m2 over 12 months.

**Which feature is an indication for renal ultrasound according to the CPG?**

A. CKD G3a alone
B. Age over 50 years
C. Loss of eGFR of more than 5 ml/min/1.73 m2 within one year
D. Controlled hypertension

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Ultrasound indications include rapid deterioration of renal function (loss of eGFR >5 ml/min/1.73 m2 within one year, or 10 within five years), haematuria, symptoms of obstruction, a family history of polycystic kidney disease with age over 20 years, and planned biopsy. Her fall of 7 meets the rapid-decline criterion. A, B and D are not listed indications.

**Teaching point:** Rapid decline (>5 in 1 year or >10 in 5 years) triggers both ultrasound and nephrology referral.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.3; Recommendation 12.

### Q20
**Topic:** Ultrasound in family history of polycystic kidney disease

A 23-year-old man's father and aunt have autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. He is normotensive with a normal eGFR and urinalysis.

**What is the most appropriate imaging recommendation?**

A. Renal ultrasound, as he has a family history of polycystic kidney disease and is over 20
B. No imaging until he develops haematuria
C. Contrast CT of the abdomen
D. MRI with gadolinium as first-line

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** A family history of polycystic kidney disease with age over 20 years is a listed indication for renal ultrasound, the first-line renal imaging test (renal size, cysts, obstruction). B waits for a complication. C and D expose him to contrast without need when ultrasound is the recommended first-line test.

**Teaching point:** Ultrasound is the first-line renal imaging test; family history of PKD over age 20 is an indication.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.3.

## Section 4: Blood Pressure and RAS Blockade

### Q21
**Topic:** BP target in diabetic kidney disease

A 59-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes has CKD G3a with urine protein of 0.4 g/day. Clinic BP on two visits is 136/82 mmHg on lisinopril.

**What is the CPG blood pressure target for her?**

A. ≤150/90 mmHg
B. ≤140/90 mmHg
C. ≤130/80 mmHg (SBP 120 to 130 mmHg)
D. ≤120/70 mmHg

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Recommendation 5 sets ≤130/80 mmHg (SBP 120-130 mmHg) for diabetic kidney disease regardless of whether proteinuria is above or below 1 g/day. Her BP is above target. B is the target for non-DKD with proteinuria <1 g/day, which is the trap. A and D are not CPG targets.

**Teaching point:** In DKD, the target is ≤130/80 whatever the level of proteinuria.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1; Recommendation 5.

### Q22
**Topic:** BP target in non-diabetic CKD with low proteinuria

A 70-year-old man with hypertensive nephrosclerosis has CKD G3b and urine protein of 0.3 g/day. He has no diabetes, and BP is 138/84 mmHg on amlodipine and indapamide. He has postural dizziness when standing quickly.

**What is the most appropriate blood pressure target?**

A. ≤140/90 mmHg, individualised for co-morbidity and age
B. ≤130/80 mmHg
C. ≤120/80 mmHg
D. ≤110/70 mmHg

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** For non-DKD with proteinuria <1 g/day, the CPG target is ≤140/90 mmHg (SBP 120-140 mmHg), individualised to co-morbidity and age. He is at target, and his postural symptoms argue against intensifying. B applies to DKD or non-DKD with proteinuria ≥1 g/day. C and D risk hypotension and AKI, which the CPG warns about with intensive lowering.

**Teaching point:** Non-DKD with proteinuria <1 g/day: ≤140/90 mmHg; individualise in the elderly.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1; Recommendation 5.

### Q23
**Topic:** BP target in non-diabetic CKD with heavy proteinuria

A 42-year-old woman with IgA nephropathy has urine protein of 1.6 g/day despite ramipril. Her BP is 134/86 mmHg. She has no diabetes.

**What is her CPG blood pressure target?**

A. ≤140/90 mmHg
B. ≤130/80 mmHg (SBP 120 to 130 mmHg)
C. ≤150/90 mmHg
D. No specific target; treat proteinuria only

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** For non-DKD with proteinuria ≥1 g/day, the target is ≤130/80 mmHg (SBP 120-130 mmHg). Proteinuria is an independent predictor of progression, and its reduction correlates with slower decline. A applies when proteinuria is <1 g/day. C is too lenient. D ignores the BP target.

**Teaching point:** Proteinuria ≥1 g/day lowers the non-DKD target to ≤130/80 mmHg.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1; Recommendation 5.

### Q24
**Topic:** Intensive targets from SPRINT

A 64-year-old man with non-diabetic CKD G3a, proteinuria 0.2 g/day and previous myocardial infarction tolerates his antihypertensives well. BP is 132/78 mmHg.

**Which statement about lowering his systolic BP further is most consistent with the CPG?**

A. Intensive lowering is contraindicated in all CKD patients
B. Lowering SBP towards 120 mmHg improved renal outcomes in the SPRINT CKD subgroup
C. SBP should be kept above 140 mmHg to protect renal perfusion
D. Lowering SBP towards 120 mmHg can be considered because he has high CV risk and tolerates treatment, with watch for hypotension and AKI

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Based on SPRINT, lowering SBP towards 120 mmHg can be considered in non-DKD patients with high CV risk in whom BP lowering is well tolerated. In the CKD subgroup, intensive lowering reduced all-cause mortality but not CV or renal outcomes, which is why B is wrong. Physicians must watch for symptomatic hypotension and AKI. A and C contradict this.

**Teaching point:** SPRINT supports SBP ~120 in high-risk non-DKD CKD for mortality, not renal, benefit.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1; Recommendation 5.

### Q25
**Topic:** Choice of antihypertensive without significant proteinuria

A 57-year-old woman with non-diabetic CKD G3a has hypertension (148/92 mmHg) and urine protein of 0.2 g/day. She has stable angina with a resting heart rate of 88/min.

**Which statement about drug choice is most appropriate?**

A. An ACE inhibitor is mandatory because she has CKD
B. Dual RAS blockade is first line
C. Any antihypertensive class may be used; choose according to co-morbidity, so a beta-blocker or calcium channel blocker is reasonable
D. Loop diuretic is the only acceptable agent

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Recommendation 6 states that any class of antihypertensive may be used in CKD without proteinuria, the choice depending on co-morbidity. ACEi/ARB become first line in non-DKD with hypertension only when protein excretion is ≥0.5 g/day. Her angina makes a rate-limiting or anti-anginal agent sensible. A over-applies the ACEi indication. B is not routine. D has no basis.

**Teaching point:** Without significant proteinuria, there is no preferred class in CKD: treat to target and to co-morbidity.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2; Recommendation 6; Algorithm 4.

### Q26
**Topic:** ACEi/ARB thresholds in non-diabetic CKD

A 48-year-old man with chronic glomerulonephritis is normotensive (122/76 mmHg) and has urine protein of 1.3 g/day on repeated testing.

**What is the most appropriate management of his proteinuria?**

A. ACE inhibitor (or ARB) as first-line, because protein excretion is ≥1.0 g/day
B. No treatment, because he is normotensive
C. Dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker
D. Thiazide diuretic

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Recommendation 6 lists ACEi/ARB as first line in non-DKD when protein excretion is ≥1.0 g/day (regardless of BP), and in non-DKD with hypertension when it is ≥0.5 g/day. B ignores the proteinuria indication. C and D lower BP but lack the established anti-proteinuric, renoprotective effect.

**Teaching point:** Non-DKD: ACEi/ARB for proteinuria ≥1 g/day, or ≥0.5 g/day if hypertensive.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2; Recommendation 6.

### Q27
**Topic:** ACEi before ARB

A 55-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and ACR 12 mg/mmol needs RAS blockade. She has no previous ACE inhibitor intolerance.

**Which is the most appropriate first choice according to the CPG?**

A. An ARB, because it is more renoprotective
B. Combined ACE inhibitor and ARB
C. Aliskiren
D. An ACE inhibitor, switching to an ARB only for a non-renal adverse reaction such as cough

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** There is no evidence that ARB is superior to ACEi, and health economic evidence favours ACEi for cost-effectiveness. The CPG advises prescribing ACEi first and changing to an ARB only if there is a non-renal adverse drug reaction. A overstates ARB benefit. B is not routine and carries more adverse events. C added no benefit in trials.

**Teaching point:** ACEi first; ARB only for non-renal intolerance.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(a).

### Q28
**Topic:** Monitoring after starting ACEi

A 68-year-old man with diabetic kidney disease (eGFR 46 ml/min/1.73 m2) starts perindopril 4 mg.

**When should his renal profile be rechecked?**

A. Within two weeks of starting
B. After six months
C. Only if he develops oedema
D. At his annual review

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Recommendation 6 and Section 4.1.2(a) state that renal profile should be reassessed within two weeks of initiation or dose escalation of ACEi/ARB, the interval depending on baseline renal function. B, C and D could miss hyperkalaemia or a significant creatinine rise.

**Teaching point:** Recheck creatinine and potassium within 2 weeks of starting or increasing ACEi/ARB.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(a); Recommendation 6.

### Q29
**Topic:** Acceptable creatinine rise on ACEi

A 61-year-old woman with DKD starts ramipril. Baseline creatinine is 110 umol/L. Two weeks later creatinine is 128 umol/L and potassium is 5.1 mmol/L. She is well and BP is above target.

**What is the most appropriate action?**

A. Stop ramipril
B. Switch to an ARB
C. Halve the dose permanently
D. Continue and titrate ramipril towards the maximum recommended dose

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The rise is about 16%. ACEi/ARB should be titrated to the maximum recommended dose to achieve BP and anti-proteinuric targets provided creatinine stays <30% above baseline (eGFR reduction <25%) and potassium is <5.6 mmol/L. A and C abandon proven benefit. B is not indicated: an ARB has the same renal haemodynamic effect.

**Teaching point:** A creatinine rise under 30% with potassium under 5.6 is expected; keep titrating.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(a).

### Q30
**Topic:** When to reduce or stop ACEi

A 72-year-old man with CKD G3b started losartan 6 weeks ago. Creatinine has risen from 150 to 205 umol/L and potassium is 5.8 mmol/L. He has not been unwell, takes no NSAIDs and is euvolaemic.

**What is the most appropriate action?**

A. Increase the losartan dose
B. Add spironolactone
C. Continue losartan unchanged and recheck in 6 months
D. Reduce or discontinue losartan

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Creatinine has risen by about 37% and potassium is ≥5.6 mmol/L. The CPG advises considering reduction or discontinuation within two months of starting when creatinine stays ≥30% above baseline (or eGFR falls ≥25%) or potassium is ≥5.6 mmol/L, after excluding other precipitating factors such as volume depletion or NSAIDs. A and B would worsen hyperkalaemia. C ignores the threshold.

**Teaching point:** Creatinine rise ≥30% or potassium ≥5.6 within 2 months: reduce or stop ACEi/ARB.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(a).

### Q31
**Topic:** Dual RAS blockade

A 51-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes has ACR 85 mg/mmol despite maximum-dose ramipril and BP of 128/78 mmHg. Her GP proposes adding valsartan.

**What is the most appropriate response?**

A. Add valsartan, as dual blockade is first line in DKD
B. Avoid routine dual RAS blockade; it does not reduce mortality, raises hyperkalaemia, hypotension and AKI, and is reserved for selected non-DKD patients under a nephrologist
C. Add aliskiren instead
D. Stop ramipril and use valsartan alone at double dose

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Dual RAS blockade reduces proteinuria but not mortality, has mixed effects on hard renal endpoints, and causes more hyperkalaemia, hypotension and AKI. It should not be prescribed routinely and may be considered only in carefully selected non-DKD patients with persistent proteinuria >0.5 g/day and normal potassium, under nephrologist supervision. She has DKD, so A is wrong. C (direct renin inhibitor) added no benefit with more adverse events. D has no evidence base.

**Teaching point:** Dual RAS blockade is not routine, and in this CPG is limited to selected non-DKD patients under nephrology.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(b); Recommendation 6.

### Q32
**Topic:** Direct renin inhibitors

A 66-year-old man with DKD on losartan asks whether adding aliskiren would protect his kidneys further.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Added to standard RAS blockade it gave no extra benefit and caused more adverse events
B. It halves the risk of end-stage renal disease
C. It is preferred over ACE inhibitors in diabetic kidney disease
D. It is the recommended add-on when potassium is above 5.6 mmol/L

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In RCTs including VA NEPHRON-D and ALTITUDE, adding a direct renin inhibitor to standard RAS blockade did not confer additional benefit and was associated with more adverse events. B and C have no support. D is dangerous, as RAS blockade raises potassium further.

**Teaching point:** Aliskiren on top of ACEi/ARB: no benefit, more harm.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(e).

### Q33
**Topic:** Calcium channel blockers and proteinuria

A 54-year-old woman with non-diabetic proteinuric CKD remains above BP target on maximum-dose ACE inhibitor. An add-on agent is needed that may also reduce proteinuria.

**Which calcium channel blocker class has the greater anti-proteinuric effect according to the CPG?**

A. Short-acting nifedipine
B. Standard L-type dihydropyridines such as amlodipine
C. Non-dihydropyridines such as diltiazem or verapamil
D. No calcium channel blocker reduces proteinuria

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Meta-analysis showed that non-dihydropyridine CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem) had a greater anti-proteinuric effect than dihydropyridines in both DKD and non-DKD, although long-term renal outcome evidence is lacking. Some L/T- and L/N-type DHPs (e.g. lercanidipine, cilnidipine) may also help as add-ons. CCBs as add-on to RAS blockers are reasonable and safe. A and B are less anti-proteinuric, and D is wrong.

**Teaching point:** NDHP CCBs reduce proteinuria more than standard DHPs; renal outcome benefit is unproven.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.2(c); Algorithm 4.

## Section 5: Glycaemia, Diet and Other Measures

### Q34
**Topic:** HbA1c target and reliability in CKD

A 63-year-old man with DKD and CKD G4 has an HbA1c of 6.1%, but his home capillary glucose readings are frequently 12-15 mmol/L. His haemoglobin is 9.8 g/dL.

**What is the most likely explanation and the best way to assess control?**

A. HbA1c is falsely high in CKD; rely on fasting glucose only
B. His control is excellent; no change needed
C. HbA1c may be falsely low because of anaemia in CKD; use regular blood glucose measurements
D. Home meters are always inaccurate in CKD

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG notes that HbA1c may be falsely low in CKD due to anaemia, so regular blood glucose measurements are advised for more accurate assessment. The target HbA1c is ≤7%, individualised to co-morbidity and age. A reverses the direction. B accepts a misleading value. D has no basis.

**Teaching point:** In anaemic CKD patients, a "good" HbA1c may be falsely low: check glucose readings.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2; Recommendation 7.

### Q35
**Topic:** SGLT2 inhibitor evidence

A 60-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes, established cardiovascular disease and eGFR 52 ml/min/1.73 m2 is considering an SGLT2 inhibitor.

**Which trial finding does the CPG cite for renal benefit?**

A. Canagliflozin reduced amputation risk
B. Empagliflozin reduced incident or worsening nephropathy by 39% in patients with eGFR ≥30
C. SGLT2 inhibitors are contraindicated in all CKD
D. Empagliflozin increased progression to dialysis

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In EMPA-REG OUTCOME (T2DM, high CV risk, eGFR ≥30), empagliflozin reduced incident or worsening nephropathy by 39% at four years (HR 0.61). Canagliflozin in CANVAS reduced albuminuria progression and a composite renal outcome but increased amputation risk (HR 1.97), so A reverses the finding. C and D contradict the data.

**Teaching point:** SGLT2 inhibitors reduce CV outcomes and may be renoprotective; canagliflozin carried an amputation signal.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.

### Q36
**Topic:** Dietary protein in CKD

A 58-year-old man with non-diabetic CKD G4 asks about diet. He is well nourished with a BMI of 24 kg/m2.

**Which dietary prescription is most consistent with the CPG?**

A. High-protein diet of 1.5 g/kg/day to preserve muscle
B. Protein 0.6-0.8 g/kg/day with energy 30-35 kcal/kg/day, supervised by a dietitian
C. Protein-free diet until dialysis
D. Energy restriction to 20 kcal/kg/day with unrestricted protein

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Recommendation 8 states that a low-protein diet (0.6-0.8 g/kg/day) with adequate energy (30-35 kcal/kg/day) may be given in CKD stage 3-5, supervised by a dietitian, weighing benefit against protein-calorie malnutrition. A contradicts avoiding excessive protein. C risks malnutrition. D inverts the energy advice.

**Teaching point:** Low protein must be paired with adequate calories and dietitian supervision.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.3; Recommendation 8.

### Q37
**Topic:** Protein restriction in overt diabetic kidney disease

A 62-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and overt nephropathy (urine protein 2 g/day, CKD G3b) is referred to the dietitian.

**What protein intake is recommended for her?**

A. 0.3 g/kg/day with keto-acid supplements
B. 0.6 g/kg/day
C. 0.8 g/kg/day
D. Unrestricted protein

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG recommends 0.8 g/kg/day in DKD (versus 0.6-0.8 in non-DKD). A low-protein diet of 0.6 g/kg/day is not advised in overt DKD because of the risk of malnutrition, which makes B the trap. A (very low protein with keto-acids) is for non-DKD stage 4-5. D ignores the advice to avoid excessive protein.

**Teaching point:** DKD: 0.8 g/kg/day; avoid 0.6 g/kg/day in overt DKD.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.3; Recommendation 8.

### Q38
**Topic:** Very low protein diet with keto-acids

A 52-year-old man with non-diabetic CKD stage 5 (eGFR 14 ml/min/1.73 m2), not yet on dialysis, weighs 60 kg. He is motivated to delay dialysis and is adherent to dietary advice.

**Which statement about keto-acid supplementation is most accurate?**

A. Keto-acids benefit any patient regardless of diet
B. They are indicated mainly in diabetic kidney disease
C. They should be given at a fixed dose of 2 tablets daily
D. A vegetarian very low protein diet with keto-acids can defer dialysis in non-DKD, at 1 tablet per 5 kg body weight per day, supervised by a dietitian

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** In non-DKD with eGFR <20, a vegetarian VLPD supplemented with keto-acids deferred dialysis (RRT needed in 11% vs 30%) and was nutritionally safe. Only patients who adhere to the VLPD benefit, at 1 tablet for every 5 kg body weight per day (12 tablets for him), under dietitian supervision. A ignores adherence. B is wrong because the evidence is in non-DKD. C is an incorrect dose.

**Teaching point:** Keto-acids: non-DKD stage 4-5, adherent to VLPD, 1 tablet per 5 kg per day.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.3.

### Q39
**Topic:** Uric acid and miscellaneous renoprotective agents

A 47-year-old man with CKD G3a has asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (uric acid 520 umol/L) and no gout. He asks whether allopurinol, pentoxifylline or herbal Cordyceps will protect his kidneys.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Allopurinol is proven to prevent ESRD and is mandatory
B. Pentoxifylline is first-line renoprotection
C. Evidence for uric acid lowering is emerging but needs more RCTs, and evidence for pentoxifylline, vitamin D analogues and traditional medicines is inconclusive
D. Cordyceps is recommended as adjuvant renoprotection

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG describes uric acid reduction as a potential strategy with emerging evidence that needs more RCTs. There is no conclusive evidence for pentoxifylline, vitamin D analogues or traditional medications; studies of Cordyceps and puerarin were of low methodological quality. A, B and D overstate weak evidence.

**Teaching point:** ACEi/ARB, BP and glycaemic control are proven; urate lowering and herbal agents are not.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 4.5 and 4.6.

## Section 6: Cardiovascular Risk and Nephrotoxins

### Q40
**Topic:** Statins in CKD

A 67-year-old woman with CKD G3b, no known cardiovascular disease and LDL-C of 3.4 mmol/L asks whether a statin is worthwhile.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Statins should be offered for primary prevention of cardiovascular events; SHARP showed a 17% reduction in major atherosclerotic events with ezetimibe/simvastatin
B. Statins are mainly indicated to slow CKD progression
C. Statins are contraindicated below eGFR 45
D. Statins are only for secondary prevention in CKD

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Recommendation 9 states that statins should be offered for primary and secondary prevention of CV events in CKD. In SHARP (CKD stages 3-5), ezetimibe/simvastatin reduced major atherosclerotic events by 17%, without increased adverse events. B is wrong because evidence for slowing progression is insufficient. C is wrong (many statins need no adjustment). D omits primary prevention.

**Teaching point:** Statins in CKD are for CV prevention, not for kidney protection.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 4.4 and 5.1; Recommendation 9.

### Q41
**Topic:** Aspirin for primary prevention

A 58-year-old man with CKD G3a and hypertension but no cardiovascular disease asks whether he should take daily aspirin.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Start aspirin 100 mg daily for primary prevention
B. Start aspirin plus clopidogrel
C. Aspirin is indicated once eGFR falls below 30
D. Aspirin should not be used for primary prevention in CKD

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Recommendation 10 states that aspirin should not be used for primary prevention of CVD in CKD. A meta-analysis found no clear benefit and no reduction in mortality, with a likely increase in major bleeding. Aspirin is recommended for secondary prevention. B is wrong because the combination should be avoided unless there is a compelling indication. C has no basis.

**Teaching point:** In CKD: aspirin for secondary prevention only.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2; Recommendation 10.

### Q42
**Topic:** Dual antiplatelet therapy in CKD

A 64-year-old woman with DKD and a TIA 3 years ago takes aspirin. Her neurologist suggests adding clopidogrel long term "for extra protection". She has had no recent coronary event or stent.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Add clopidogrel, as the combination reduces mortality in DKD
B. Avoid adding clopidogrel unless there is a compelling indication; in DKD the combination was associated with higher mortality
C. Stop aspirin and use clopidogrel plus warfarin
D. Double the aspirin dose instead

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Recommendation 10 states that combination clopidogrel and aspirin should be avoided in CKD unless compelling indications are present. A post-hoc CHARISMA analysis showed a 60% increase in overall mortality with the combination versus aspirin alone in DKD, and CKD patients have a higher bleeding risk. A reverses the evidence. C and D are not supported.

**Teaching point:** Dual antiplatelet therapy in CKD needs a compelling indication.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2; Recommendation 10.

### Q43
**Topic:** Contrast-induced AKI prevention

A 69-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and eGFR 41 ml/min/1.73 m2 needs a contrast-enhanced CT for suspected pancreatic malignancy, and no alternative imaging will answer the question.

**Which measure is recommended by the CPG?**

A. Oral N-acetylcysteine as the main prophylaxis
B. High-osmolar ionic contrast at the usual dose
C. Isotonic saline peri-procedure with the lowest dose of low- or iso-osmolar contrast, avoiding repeat studies within 48 hours
D. Intravenous sodium bicarbonate as the proven prophylaxis

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** He is high risk (eGFR <60 and diabetes). Measures include isotonic saline peri-procedure, non-ionic low-osmolar (e.g. iopamidol) or iso-osmolar (e.g. iodixanol) contrast, the lowest possible dose and no repeat studies within 48 hours. The CPG states there is insufficient evidence for N-acetylcysteine and sodium bicarbonate, which makes A and D traps. B uses the wrong contrast.

**Teaching point:** Contrast AKI prevention: isotonic saline and minimal low/iso-osmolar contrast; NAC and bicarbonate are unproven.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.7(iv).

## Section 7: Pregnancy and Referral

### Q46
**Topic:** Pregnancy in CKD

A 30-year-old woman with IgA nephropathy wants to conceive. Her creatinine is 98 umol/L, BP is 124/78 mmHg on lisinopril and urine protein is 0.4 g/day. She has no active systemic disease.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Pregnancy should be avoided because she has CKD
B. Pregnancy may be considered with pre-pregnancy care, stopping lisinopril before conception, and multidisciplinary co-management
C. Continue lisinopril throughout pregnancy because it protects the kidneys
D. Pregnancy is safe and needs no specialist input

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Pregnancy may be considered in women with mild impairment (creatinine <124 umol/L), well-controlled BP and proteinuria <1 g/day, all of which she meets. All women of reproductive age with CKD should receive pre-pregnancy care, ACEi/ARB should be avoided in pregnancy, and pregnant women with CKD should be co-managed by a multidisciplinary team. A is too restrictive. C ignores that ACEi must be avoided. D ignores the increased maternal-foetal risks that begin even at stage 1.

**Teaching point:** Creatinine <124, controlled BP and proteinuria <1 g/day: pregnancy may be considered, without ACEi/ARB.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6; Recommendation 11.

### Q47
**Topic:** Referral criteria

A primary care doctor reviews four patients with CKD.

**Which patient meets the CPG criteria for referral to a nephrologist?**

A. A 68-year-old woman with eGFR 52 ml/min/1.73 m2, stable for 3 years, and proteinuria 0.3 g/day
B. A 74-year-old man with eGFR 58 ml/min/1.73 m2 and controlled BP on two agents
C. A 55-year-old man with uPCR 130 mg/mmol despite optimal treatment
D. A 49-year-old woman with eGFR 70 ml/min/1.73 m2 and ACR 4 mg/mmol on ramipril

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Referral criteria include persistent heavy proteinuria (≥1 g/day or uPCR ≥100 mg/mmol) despite optimal treatment, isolated microscopic haematuria after excluding urogynaecological causes, haematuria with proteinuria ≥0.5 g/day, rapid decline, eGFR <30, resistant hypertension, suspected renal artery stenosis or hereditary disease, pregnancy, persistent potassium abnormalities and unexplained CKD. Patients A, B and D are stable without a referral criterion.

**Teaching point:** uPCR ≥100 mg/mmol (≈1 g/day) despite treatment is a referral trigger.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7; Recommendation 12.

### Q48
**Topic:** Resistant hypertension as a referral criterion

A 60-year-old woman with CKD G3a has a clinic BP of 156/94 mmHg, confirmed with home readings and good adherence, despite amlodipine 10 mg, perindopril 8 mg and indapamide 1.5 mg.

**Why does she meet the CPG criteria for nephrology referral?**

A. She has failed to reach target BP despite three antihypertensive agents including a diuretic
B. She is over 60 years old
C. She takes a calcium channel blocker
D. Her CKD is stage G3a

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Resistant hypertension, defined as failure to achieve target BP despite three antihypertensive agents including a diuretic, is a referral criterion in Recommendation 12. Age, the drug class and G3a CKD alone are not referral criteria (G4-G5, eGFR <30, is).

**Teaching point:** Three drugs including a diuretic without reaching target: refer.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7; Recommendation 12.

### Q49
**Topic:** Immediate discussion with a nephrologist

A 66-year-old man with known CKD G3b presents with vomiting after a week of ibuprofen for back pain. Creatinine has risen from 160 to 420 umol/L and potassium is 7.1 mmol/L with peaked T waves.

**What is the most appropriate referral pathway?**

A. Routine outpatient nephrology referral within 3 months
B. Primary care follow-up in one week
C. Immediate discussion with a nephrologist, alongside emergency treatment of hyperkalaemia
D. Urology referral

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Immediate discussion with a nephrologist is indicated for AKI superimposed on CKD, newly detected ESRD, accelerated or malignant hypertension, life-threatening hyperkalaemia and suspected rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. He has both AKI on CKD (NSAID-related) and life-threatening hyperkalaemia. A and B are dangerously slow. D is for renal outflow obstruction.

**Teaching point:** AKI on CKD or life-threatening hyperkalaemia needs immediate nephrology discussion.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.

### Q50
**Topic:** CKD in the elderly

An 81-year-old woman has eGFR 52 ml/min/1.73 m2, stable over 4 years, urine protein 0.2 g/day, and well-controlled BP and diabetes.

**What is the most appropriate setting for her care?**

A. Urgent nephrology referral for renal replacement therapy planning
B. Primary care, in collaboration with a nephrologist
C. Discharge from all kidney follow-up because the eGFR is age-appropriate

**Teaching point:** Stable, low-risk elderly CKD patients are managed in primary care with nephrology collaboration.
