# Management of Obesity (2nd Edition, 2023): 50 MCQs

**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia and partner societies, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Obesity*, 2nd Edition (2023).
**Format:** 50 single-best-answer questions, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style. At least 20 have long multilayer stems that need clinical reasoning. Several stems reproduce CPG tables as text, with the table number cited. Every wrong answer is a near-miss.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from the CPG text. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

## Section 1: Assessment and Diagnosis

### Q1
**Topic:** Asian BMI categories and the next step

A 41-year-old Malaysian-Chinese man attends an insurance medical. He weighs 80 kg and is 1.70 m tall. Waist circumference is 94 cm, BP 126/78 mmHg and fasting glucose 5.3 mmol/l. He has no symptoms and takes no medication.

Adapted from CPG Table 2-1 (BMI classification for Asian adults):

| Class | BMI (kg/m2) |
|---|---|
| Normal | 18.5-22.9 |
| Pre-obese | 23.0-27.4 |
| Obese I | 27.5-32.4 |
| Obese II | 32.5-37.4 |
| Obese III | ≥37.5 |

**Which is the correct classification and most appropriate next step?**

A. Pre-obese; reassure and rescreen in 5 years
B. Obese I; start orlistat
C. Obese I; lifestyle intervention and assess for associated conditions
D. Obese II; refer for bariatric assessment

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** BMI = 80 / 1.70² = 80 / 2.89 = 27.7 kg/m2, which is 27.5 or above, so he is Obese I by the Asian bands. Pharmacotherapy requires BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with a comorbidity, and he has no comorbidity, so orlistat is not indicated. Bariatric surgery needs BMI ≥37.5, or ≥32.5 with metabolic syndrome. Pre-obese would be the answer on WHO Caucasian cut-offs (25-29.9 would be overweight, not obese). Anyone with BMI of 23 or more needs assessment for associated conditions.

**Teaching point:** Obesity in Asians starts at 27.5 kg/m2. Obese I is managed with lifestyle intervention plus screening for associated conditions.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.2, Table 2-1; Section 2.3.

### Q2
**Topic:** Waist circumference at high BMI

A 36-year-old woman with BMI 38 kg/m2 is being assessed. Her GP wants to record her waist circumference to stratify cardiometabolic risk and measures it as 112 cm.

**What is the most accurate statement about waist circumference in her?**

A. It should be measured at the umbilicus, not midway between ribs and iliac crest
B. It is essential and above the Asian threshold of 80 cm
C. It adds little, because it loses predictive value above BMI 35
D. It must be repeated with bioelectrical impedance

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Waist circumference is most useful in the normal and pre-obese BMI categories. For BMI above 35 kg/m2 it is unnecessary because it loses its predictive value. The Asian thresholds are 90 cm (men) and 80 cm (women), but the question is whether to measure at all. The CPG technique is midway between the lowest palpable rib and the iliac crest, not the umbilicus. Impedance is an alternative body-fat method, not a requirement.

**Teaching point:** Measure waist circumference in people with normal or pre-obese BMI. Above BMI 35 it adds nothing.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.2 (Classification by waist circumference).

### Q3
**Topic:** BMI misclassification in the elderly

A 74-year-old woman has BMI 22 kg/m2 but has lost muscle bulk over several years. She has diabetes, hypertension and a waist circumference of 92 cm. Her grandson says she cannot be "overweight" as her BMI is normal.

**What is the most appropriate interpretation?**

A. Normal; BMI classification is reliable in her age group
B. Normal; waist circumference applies only below age 60
C. Obese; BMI cut-offs fall by 5 units after age 70
D. Body fat may be underestimated; use waist circumference and fat measures

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Older people tend to have a higher body-fat proportion because of sarcopenia, so a normal BMI can be misclassified as healthy. BMI also cannot separate muscle from fat or show fat distribution. Waist circumference (92 cm exceeds the Asian 80 cm female threshold) and body-fat measures give a better risk estimate. The CPG does not adjust BMI cut-offs by age.

**Teaching point:** BMI under-reads adiposity in the elderly. Add waist circumference or a body-fat measure.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.2 (Limitations of BMI).

### Q4
**Topic:** Drug review in a patient with weight gain (long clinical stem)

A 47-year-old man with bipolar disorder, hypertension, type 2 diabetes and osteoarthritis of the knee has gained 14 kg in 18 months. BMI is now 31 kg/m2. His medications are lithium, olanzapine, mirtazapine (added for low mood 10 months ago), atenolol, gliclazide, metformin and a 2-week course of prednisolone he finished last month. He is keen to avoid weight gain.

**Which single change would be the most appropriate first step?**

A. Stop gliclazide, as sulphonylureas are the main cause
B. Add phentermine to the current regimen
C. Discuss alternatives to the contributing psychotropics with his psychiatrist
D. Stop atenolol, as beta-blockers cause the largest weight gain

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG tables the drug classes that contribute to weight gain: antidepressants (mirtazapine), antiepileptics and mood stabilisers (lithium), antipsychotics (olanzapine, quetiapine, clozapine, risperidone), beta-blockers (atenolol), glucocorticoids and some glucose-lowering drugs (insulin, sulphonylureas, TZDs). He has several contributors, but the psychotropics (olanzapine, mirtazapine, lithium) are the main ones, and changing them needs psychiatric input to avoid relapse. Stopping one drug alone is unlikely to be enough, and adding phentermine (contraindicated with uncontrolled hypertension, and a sympathomimetic) is not the first step. Medication history is part of the obesity assessment.

**Teaching point:** Review obesogenic medicines at assessment. Antipsychotics, mood stabilisers, antidepressants, beta-blockers, steroids and sulphonylureas all contribute.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.3, Table 2-2.

### Q5
**Topic:** Weight-loss targets by comorbidity

A 52-year-old woman with BMI 36 kg/m2 has T2DM of 2 years' duration (HbA1c 7.6%), moderate obstructive sleep apnoea, and an ultrasound showing fatty liver with raised ALT. She asks what weight loss she should aim for and what each comorbidity needs.

Adapted from CPG Table 2-3 (treatment targets by diagnosis):

| Diagnosis | Weight loss target (%) |
|---|---|
| Metabolic syndrome | 10 |
| T2DM | 5-15 |
| Hypertension | 5-15 |
| Sleep apnoea | 7-11 |
| MAFLD | 10-40 |

**What is the best single weight-loss target to cover all three of her conditions?**

A. At least 5%
B. 7-8%
C. 10-15%
D. Over 40%

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** T2DM needs 5-15%, sleep apnoea 7-11% and MAFLD 10-40%. The one target that covers all three is at least 10%, and 10-15% is the usual range. A 5% loss helps glucose but is too little for apnoea and fatty liver. The CPG also notes that 10-15% loss is needed for OSA, osteoarthritis and steatotic hepatitis. Over 40% is not a goal. The 7-8% figure is the asthma target.

**Teaching point:** Match the target to the comorbidity. 5-10% improves BP, HDL and glucose, but OSA, OA and fatty liver need 10-15%.

**Reference:** CPG Section 1.4; Section 2.4, Table 2-3.

### Q6
**Topic:** Realistic weight-loss goals

A 45-year-old man with BMI 33 kg/m2, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia sets himself a goal of losing 20 kg in 8 weeks. He weighs 98 kg.

**Which goal does the CPG describe as realistic for someone with CV risk factors?**

A. Up to 1 kg per week, with 3-5% of baseline weight as an initial target
B. 2.5 kg per week until BMI is below 25 kg/m2
C. 15% of baseline weight in 8 weeks
D. 10 kg per month, using VLCD from the outset

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Realistic goals are up to 1 kg per week and up to 10% of baseline weight, with 3-5% in the presence of CV risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and hyperglycaemia. 20 kg in 8 weeks (2.5 kg/week) is unrealistic and unsafe. A VLCD (below 800 kcal/day) needs medical supervision with a dietitian and is not the starting point.

**Teaching point:** Aim for up to 1 kg/week and 3-5% initially in people with CV risk factors, up to 10% overall.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Realistic goals).

### Q7
**Topic:** Screening frequency

A practice nurse asks how often adult patients should be screened for overweight and obesity and which measurements to use.

**Which is the correct approach?**

A. BMI every 5 years; waist circumference only if BMI is above 30
B. Weight alone every 2 years
C. BMI and waist circumference yearly
D. Body fat percentage at every visit

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** All adults should be screened yearly with BMI and waist circumference, with a BMI of 23 kg/m2 or above (or waist 90 cm in men, 80 cm in women) triggering further evaluation. Weight alone ignores height and fat distribution. Body-fat percentage is an alternative, not the routine standard. Waist circumference is most useful at lower BMI categories.

**Teaching point:** Screen yearly with BMI plus waist circumference. Evaluate further at BMI ≥23 kg/m2.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.1; Recommendations 1.

### Q8
**Topic:** WHO versus Asian waist thresholds

A 38-year-old Malay woman has BMI 24.8 kg/m2 and waist circumference 86 cm. A colleague points out that the WHO cut-off for women is 88 cm and says she is "fine".

**Which is the most appropriate response?**

A. She is fine; WHO cut-offs apply to all ethnic groups
B. Waist circumference should be ignored when BMI is below 25
C. She is fine unless waist exceeds 102 cm
D. Asian thresholds are lower, so she is above the cut-off and needs assessment

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Asian studies show a high prevalence of T2DM and CVD at lower cut-offs, 90 cm in men and 80 cm in women, versus the WHO 102 cm and 88 cm. Her 86 cm exceeds the 80 cm Asian threshold, and her BMI of 24.8 is already pre-obese (23-27.4). Waist circumference is most useful in people with normal or pre-obese BMI.

**Teaching point:** Use Asian waist cut-offs: 90 cm (men) and 80 cm (women).

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.2.

## Section 2: Lifestyle, Diet and Behaviour

### Q9
**Topic:** Calorie prescription (long clinical stem)

A 39-year-old woman with BMI 31 kg/m2 and a sedentary desk job wants to lose weight. A friend lost weight on 800 kcal/day for 6 weeks. She has no medical conditions and eats about 2300 kcal/day. She asks her dietitian for the fastest plan.

**Which prescription best matches the CPG for starting treatment?**

A. 800 kcal/day, unsupervised
B. 1800-2000 kcal/day with no exercise
C. 1200-1500 kcal/day, or a 500-750 kcal/day deficit
D. Fasting every second day with no calorie counting

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For an initial weight loss of 0.5-1 kg/week, the CPG suggests 1200-1500 kcal/day for women (1500-1800 for men), or reducing usual energy intake by 500-750 kcal/day. VLCD (below 800 kcal/day) must be prescribed under the supervision of a medical team that includes a dietitian. Alternate-day fasting is an option only with dietitian input, and no method has proven superior.

**Teaching point:** Women: 1200-1500 kcal/day. Men: 1500-1800 kcal/day. Or cut 500-750 kcal/day.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Energy restriction).

### Q10
**Topic:** Macronutrient choice

A 50-year-old man with BMI 34 kg/m2 and T2DM has tried a ketogenic-style, low-carbohydrate diet. His wife prefers a balanced isocaloric plan with 50% carbohydrate. He asks which is better for weight loss.

**What does the CPG evidence show?**

A. Low-carbohydrate diets produce clearly more weight loss at 1-2 years
B. Low-carbohydrate diets are contraindicated in T2DM
C. Balanced diets produce 5 kg more weight loss at 6 months
D. No significant difference in weight loss or CV risk factors at 3-6 months or 1-2 years

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Studies comparing low-carbohydrate diets (carbohydrate below 45% of energy) with balanced isocaloric diets (40-65% carbohydrate) in people with or without T2DM showed no difference in weight loss at 3-6 months (-0.74 kg) or 1-2 years (-0.48 kg), and no difference in CV risk factors. The CPG recommends calorie restriction combined with either low carbohydrate or low fat intake. Low-carbohydrate diets are not contraindicated.

**Teaching point:** Calorie restriction works. Low carbohydrate or low fat are both acceptable. Choose by preference.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Macronutrient modification).

### Q11
**Topic:** Intermittent fasting

A 44-year-old woman asks whether time-restricted eating is better than a daily calorie target. She has read that the fasting window itself drives weight loss.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Time-restricted eating is proven superior and should be first line
B. Alternate-day fasting is contraindicated in obesity
C. Weight loss depends on cumulative caloric restriction, and effectiveness cannot be concluded
D. Fasting duration, not calories, predicts weight loss

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG states that the degree of weight loss depends on cumulative caloric restriction, not the length of the fast, and that a conclusion on effectiveness cannot be reached because studies differ. A 12-month trial found alternate-day fasting (6.0% loss) and daily energy restriction (5.3%) did not differ in weight loss, maintenance, adherence or CV risk. It may be used as an alternative method.

**Teaching point:** Intermittent fasting works through calorie reduction. It is not proven superior to daily restriction.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Intermittent fasting).

### Q12
**Topic:** Physical activity prescription

A 55-year-old man with BMI 33 kg/m2 and hypertension walks 20 minutes twice a week. He asks what exercise he needs to lose weight rather than merely stay healthy.

**Which prescription fits the CPG?**

A. 150 minutes/week moderate intensity, progressing to 300 minutes/week, or 75-150 minutes/week of vigorous intensity
B. 60 minutes/week of light activity
C. 150 minutes/week only, with no progression, and avoid resistance work
D. Vigorous activity daily for 90 minutes

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Physical activity is encouraged in all people with obesity: at least 150 minutes/week (30 minutes/day) progressing to 300 minutes/week (60 minutes/day) of moderate intensity, or at least 75-150 minutes/week of vigorous intensity for weight loss. Combining moderate and vigorous activity helps maintain weight loss, and both aerobic and resistance exercise should be emphasised.

**Teaching point:** Moderate: 150 → 300 min/week. Vigorous: 75-150 min/week. Include resistance training.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1 (Physical activity); Key Recommendations.

### Q13
**Topic:** Combining diet and exercise

A 42-year-old woman with obesity has lost 4 kg on a 1500 kcal/day plan with no exercise. A colleague claims that exercise adds nothing to diet.

**Which CPG finding best answers the colleague?**

A. Calorie restriction alone was as effective as diet plus activity and behaviour change
B. Exercise reduces weight only in men
C. Exercise only helps once BMI is below 25
D. Diet alone was less effective than diet with 30-60 minutes/day of moderate exercise and behavioural modification

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** In an RCT, calorie restriction alone (1500 ± 200 kcal/day) was not as effective as the same diet combined with 30-60 minutes/day of moderate-intensity exercise and behavioural modification for reducing BMI, waist circumference and body fat and increasing fat-free mass in obese women.

**Teaching point:** Diet plus exercise plus behavioural modification outperforms diet alone.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Energy restriction).

### Q14
**Topic:** Over-restriction and weight regain

A 40-year-old woman who usually eats about 2400 kcal/day was prescribed a 1000 kcal/day diet in a trial and lost more weight than a comparison group at 6 months. Over months 7-12, however, she regained weight.

**Which CPG finding explains this?**

A. People with baseline intake of 2000 kcal/day or more given 1000 kcal/day regained more than those given 1500
B. Weight regain happens only if exercise is stopped
C. 1000 kcal/day diets give lower weight loss at 6 months than 1500
D. Regain was due to orlistat withdrawal

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In the trial of 1000 versus 1500 kcal/day (with increased walking), weight loss was greater at 6 months with 1000 kcal/day, but participants whose baseline intake was 2000 kcal/day or more and who were assigned 1000 kcal/day were significantly more prone to regain weight in months 7-12 than those given 1500 kcal/day. Very severe restriction in people with high baseline intake is therefore not sustainable.

**Teaching point:** Greater early loss on a very low target does not mean better maintenance, especially in high-baseline eaters.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Energy restriction).

### Q15
**Topic:** Psychological therapy

A 33-year-old woman with obesity has binge-eating episodes, low self-esteem and has repeatedly regained weight after short diets. She feels judged by previous clinicians.

**Which approach best matches the CPG?**

A. Dietary advice alone, with weight as the only outcome measure
B. Antidepressant therapy as the sole treatment
C. Multicomponent psychological and behavioural therapy, including communication that avoids stigma
D. Bariatric surgery before any psychological work

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG key recommendation is to integrate multicomponent psychological and behavioural therapy: enhancing communication and avoiding stigmatisation, psychoeducation, motivational interviewing and behavioural strategies, and psychological interventions. Dietary advice alone, a drug alone, or skipping to surgery ignore the behavioural drivers. Fighting stigma is itself a stated goal of obesity management.

**Teaching point:** Obesity care includes psychological and behavioural therapy and active avoidance of stigma.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendations (Psychological and behavioural therapy); Section 2.4.

### Q16
**Topic:** VLCD supervision

A dietitian is considering a very low calorie diet (500 kcal/day) for a 48-year-old man with BMI 41 kg/m2 and fatty liver who is about to enter a weight-loss programme before bariatric assessment.

**What does the CPG require?**

A. It may be started at home with no supervision
B. It is contraindicated before surgery
C. It is the first-line treatment for all BMI above 30
D. It should be used only as part of a comprehensive plan supervised by a medical team that includes a dietitian

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** VLCD (below 800 kcal/day) should be prescribed as part of a comprehensive treatment plan under the supervision of a medical team that includes a dietitian. It is not first line for everyone. Preoperative weight loss is encouraged: a 4-week 1200 kcal/day low-calorie diet with at least 8% excess weight loss led to better post-operative results.

**Teaching point:** VLCD (<800 kcal/day) only with medical team and dietitian supervision.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1.1 (Energy restriction); Section 4.3.2.

### Q17
**Topic:** Maintaining weight loss

A 46-year-old woman lost 12 kg over 6 months and now wants to avoid regaining it. She has been exercising at moderate intensity for 150 minutes/week.

**Which advice best supports maintenance?**

A. Stop exercise once the target weight is reached
B. Combine moderate and vigorous activity through the week
C. Switch to vigorous activity only, 30 minutes/week
D. Replace all exercise with calorie counting

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A combination of both moderate and vigorous intensity activity throughout the week should be encouraged to maintain weight loss, with both resistance and aerobic exercise emphasised to improve physical function and well-being. Stopping activity invites regain. Pharmacotherapy short courses (6 months or less) show rebound weight gain when stopped, so obesity is managed as a chronic disease.

**Teaching point:** Maintain weight loss with a mix of moderate and vigorous activity, plus resistance work.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendations (Physical activity); Section 4.2.

## Section 3: Pharmacotherapy

### Q18
**Topic:** Indication for anti-obesity drugs

A 49-year-old man with BMI 28 kg/m2, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and T2DM has completed 6 months of structured diet and exercise with a 3% weight loss. His BP is controlled. He asks whether medication is allowed.

**What is the most accurate answer?**

A. Not indicated; BMI must be 35 or above
B. Indicated only after bariatric surgery
C. Indicated as monotherapy without lifestyle change
D. Indicated as an adjunct, because BMI is ≥27 with comorbidity

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Pharmacotherapy is indicated at BMI ≥30 without comorbidity, or BMI ≥27 with comorbidity such as T2DM, hypertension, dyslipidaemia or OSA. He has BMI 28 with three comorbidities. It must be used only as an adjunct to diet, exercise and behavioural modification, never alone, and with medical supervision and careful monitoring.

**Teaching point:** Drugs: BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with comorbidity, only as an adjunct to lifestyle.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2; Table 4-4; Recommendations 5.

### Q19
**Topic:** Stopping rule

A 44-year-old woman started a high-dose GLP1 receptor agonist 3 months ago for obesity with hypertension. She has lost 3.1% of her starting weight. She tolerates the drug well.

**What does the CPG advise?**

A. Continue unchanged for a further year
B. Stop and consider alternative medicines or approaches, as the response is below 5%
C. Double the dose without review
D. Stop all lifestyle advice

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** If a patient does not achieve more than 5% weight loss after 3 months of treatment, or if there are significant safety or tolerability problems, the drug should be discontinued and an alternative approach considered. A weight loss of 5% or more is a clinically important outcome for BP, glucose and lipids. Review should be monthly for the first 3 months, then at least every 3 months.

**Teaching point:** No more than 5% loss at 3 months means stop and change the approach.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.

### Q20
**Topic:** Phentermine and cardiovascular disease

A 56-year-old man with BMI 34 kg/m2 and poorly controlled hypertension (BP 168/98 mmHg on two drugs) asks for phentermine because a friend lost weight quickly on it. He had a stroke 3 years ago.

**What is the most appropriate response?**

A. Prescribe phentermine 30 mg daily for 12 months
B. Prescribe phentermine only on alternate months, to prevent tolerance
C. Prescribe phentermine with a beta-blocker to offset the BP rise
D. Phentermine is contraindicated with uncontrolled hypertension and previous CVD

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Phentermine is a sympathomimetic amine whose contraindications include uncontrolled hypertension, history of CVD (CAD, stroke, arrhythmia, heart failure), seizures, hyperthyroidism, pregnancy and breastfeeding. Long-term CV outcome studies are lacking. It is licensed in Malaysia only for 3 months, or cyclically, and is not approved long-term by the USFDA.

**Teaching point:** Phentermine: short-term (3 months) or cyclical only, and not with uncontrolled hypertension or CVD.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Table 4-5.

### Q21
**Topic:** Phentermine-topiramate in a woman of childbearing age (long clinical stem)

A 31-year-old woman with BMI 36 kg/m2 and PCOS has failed to lose weight with lifestyle change. She is not on contraception and her last period was 5 weeks ago. She is interested in phentermine/topiramate. She has a past history of depression, currently well.

**Which is the most appropriate management?**

A. Start 3.75/23 mg and check weight in 3 months
B. Exclude pregnancy first, use effective contraception, and monitor mood
C. Start 15/92 mg for maximal weight loss
D. Avoid because phentermine/topiramate has no effect on BP

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Phentermine/topiramate is Pregnancy Category D: topiramate is teratogenic (cleft lip and palate). The CPG requires a negative urine pregnancy test before starting and monthly thereafter, with effective contraception. Mood-related effects (depression, anxiety) occur at higher doses, so monitoring is needed with a history of depression. Starting without excluding pregnancy, or beginning with the highest dose, is unsafe. The combination does not worsen BP at the higher dose; it has been shown to reduce BP.

**Teaching point:** Phentermine/topiramate: negative pregnancy test before and monthly, with contraception. Watch mood.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Table 4-5.

### Q22
**Topic:** Naltrexone-bupropion safety

A 38-year-old man with BMI 33 kg/m2 and a history of a single seizure during alcohol withdrawal 5 years ago, now abstinent, is considering naltrexone/bupropion. He takes oxycodone daily for chronic back pain.

**What is the most appropriate decision?**

A. Prescribe; both conditions are irrelevant to the drug
B. Prescribe at double the dose to overcome naltrexone antagonism
C. Prescribe with a high-fat meal to improve absorption
D. Avoid, because of seizure risk and chronic opioid use

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Contraindications to naltrexone/bupropion include seizure disorders, drug or alcohol withdrawal, chronic opioid use (naltrexone is an opioid antagonist), uncontrolled hypertension, anorexia/bulimia, glaucoma and MAO inhibitors. There is a seizure risk, so doses must be adhered to and co-administration with high-fat meals avoided. The boxed warning concerns suicidal behaviour and ideation.

**Teaching point:** Naltrexone/bupropion: avoid with seizures or withdrawal, chronic opioid use, uncontrolled hypertension or eating disorders.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Table 4-5.

### Q23
**Topic:** GLP1-RA contraindication

A 45-year-old woman with BMI 35 kg/m2 and T2DM requests high-dose semaglutide. Her sister had medullary thyroid carcinoma and carries a RET mutation (MEN2A). She is not pregnant.

**What is the most appropriate decision?**

A. Prescribe; family history is irrelevant
B. Avoid, as a family history of MTC or MEN2 is a contraindication
C. Prescribe at half dose with thyroid ultrasound
D. Prescribe liraglutide instead, as it is safe in MEN2

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Liraglutide 3 mg and semaglutide 2.4 mg both carry a boxed warning for thyroid C-cell tumours and are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2. A half dose does not remove the risk, and liraglutide has the same warning. Other options are a non-GLP1 drug such as orlistat, or a different approach.

**Teaching point:** All obesity GLP1-RAs are contraindicated with a personal or family history of MTC or MEN2.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Table 4-5.

### Q24
**Topic:** Semaglutide and retinopathy

A 56-year-old man with T2DM of 14 years, HbA1c 9.4%, BMI 33 kg/m2 and background diabetic retinopathy is to start semaglutide. He has not seen an ophthalmologist for 18 months.

**What is the most appropriate step?**

A. Start; retinopathy is not relevant to semaglutide
B. Start at 2.4 mg weekly to achieve faster glycaemic control
C. Contraindicated; semaglutide always worsens retinopathy
D. Co-manage with an ophthalmologist and monitor closely

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Rapid improvement in glycaemia in T2DM patients with baseline retinopathy resulted in early transient worsening of retinopathy with semaglutide. Patients with obesity and T2DM with retinopathy treated with semaglutide should be monitored closely and co-managed with an ophthalmologist. It is not an absolute contraindication, and a faster start is the wrong direction. Dose escalation starts at 0.25 mg weekly, increasing every 4 weeks over 16 weeks.

**Teaching point:** Semaglutide with retinopathy: slow titration and ophthalmology review.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Table 4-5.

### Q25
**Topic:** Orlistat adverse effects and interactions (long clinical stem)

A 50-year-old woman with hypothyroidism on levothyroxine, BMI 32 kg/m2, and recurrent calcium oxalate kidney stones is offered orlistat 120 mg three times daily. She asks about side effects and drug interactions.

**Which statement is the most accurate for her?**

A. Orlistat is systemically absorbed and raises BP
B. Orlistat can reduce levothyroxine absorption and increase urinary oxalate, so caution is needed
C. Orlistat is contraindicated only in pregnancy
D. Orlistat reduces absorption of water-soluble vitamins only

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Orlistat is a non-systemic pancreatic lipase inhibitor. About 30% of dietary triglyceride is excreted. Side effects include oily stool, faecal urgency and incontinence, flatulence, reduced absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and increased urinary oxalate, which may cause acute oxalate nephropathy. It affects absorption of levothyroxine, cyclosporine, anticonvulsants and antiretrovirals. It is contraindicated in malabsorption and cholestasis. With her stones and levothyroxine, a different agent or close monitoring is needed.

**Teaching point:** Orlistat: fat-soluble vitamin loss, oxalate nephropathy and reduced absorption of levothyroxine, ciclosporin and anticonvulsants.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1 (Orlistat); Table 4-5.

### Q26
**Topic:** Choosing between agents by efficacy

A 43-year-old man with BMI 38 kg/m2 and T2DM asks which drug gives the biggest weight loss. His doctor shows him CPG Table 4-5 (mean weight loss in excess of placebo):

| Drug | Weight loss vs placebo |
|---|---|
| Orlistat | 2.63 kg |
| Naltrexone/bupropion | 4.95 kg |
| Liraglutide 3 mg | 5.24 kg |
| Phentermine/topiramate | 8.8 kg |
| Semaglutide 2.4 mg | 12.7 kg |

**Which statement is the most accurate?**

A. Semaglutide 2.4 mg and phentermine/topiramate gave the largest effect, but there are no head-to-head trials between agents
B. Orlistat is the most effective, as it acts locally
C. Liraglutide 3 mg is superior to semaglutide on head-to-head testing
D. Naltrexone/bupropion has been compared head to head with all other agents

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In the absence of head-to-head trials, the most effective drugs by weight loss versus placebo are high-dose semaglutide, phentermine/topiramate and high-dose liraglutide. The only head-to-head RCT is liraglutide 3 mg versus orlistat, in which liraglutide was more effective. Choice should consider efficacy, side effects and cost.

**Teaching point:** The only head-to-head obesity drug RCT is liraglutide 3 mg versus orlistat (liraglutide won).

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Table 4-5.

### Q27
**Topic:** Drug choice with T2DM and fatty liver (long clinical stem)

A 54-year-old man has BMI 35 kg/m2, T2DM (HbA1c 8.3% on metformin), hypertension, and ultrasound evidence of fatty liver with raised ALT. He has already completed 6 months of a structured lifestyle programme with 2% loss. He has no family history of thyroid cancer, no pancreatitis and eGFR 82 ml/min/1.73 m2. He asks for the single drug that would help the most conditions at once.

**Which agent best fits the CPG?**

A. Phentermine alone, 30 mg daily
B. Orlistat alone, 120 mg three times daily
C. A GLP1 receptor agonist (semaglutide or liraglutide)
D. Naltrexone/bupropion

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** GLP1-RAs such as liraglutide and semaglutide have the added benefit of improving glycaemic control, MAFLD and cardiorenal outcomes in people with both T2DM and obesity. Semaglutide 0.5-1.0 mg improved CV and renal outcomes in T2DM, and low-dose semaglutide helped MAFLD. Phentermine raises BP and HR and is short-term only. Orlistat is the weakest in efficacy and offers no cardiorenal benefit. Naltrexone/bupropion attenuates BP benefit through bupropion's sympathomimetic action.

**Teaching point:** In obesity with T2DM and MAFLD, GLP1-RAs treat all three and reduce cardiorenal events.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2.1; Key learning points.

### Q28
**Topic:** Hypoglycaemia risk

A 61-year-old woman with T2DM on glargine insulin and gliclazide is started on liraglutide 3 mg for obesity. Two weeks later she has sweating and shakiness at 4 pm and a capillary glucose of 3.0 mmol/l.

**Which action is most appropriate?**

A. Increase gliclazide, as liraglutide causes insulin resistance
B. Add acarbose to blunt post-prandial glucose
C. Stop liraglutide permanently, as hypoglycaemia is an absolute contraindication
D. Review and reduce the insulin or sulphonylurea dose

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The CPG lists hypoglycaemia as a warning when liraglutide or semaglutide is combined with insulin or secretagogues. GLP1-RAs lower glucose in a glucose-dependent way, so the added hypoglycaemia comes from the insulin and sulphonylurea. The sensible step is to reduce those drugs. It is not an absolute contraindication, and raising the sulphonylurea would worsen the problem.

**Teaching point:** When starting a GLP1-RA for obesity in T2DM, reduce insulin and sulphonylurea doses to prevent hypoglycaemia.

**Reference:** CPG Table 4-5 (Warnings and precautions).

### Q29
**Topic:** Semaglutide titration

A 40-year-old woman with BMI 34 kg/m2, without diabetes, is prescribed semaglutide for obesity. The prescription says "start 2.4 mg weekly".

**What is the correct titration schedule?**

A. Start 2.4 mg weekly with no titration
B. Start 1 mg weekly, increase every week to 2.4 mg
C. Start 0.25 mg weekly, increase every 4 weeks over 16 weeks to 2.4 mg
D. Start 0.5 mg daily, increase every month

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Semaglutide for obesity starts at 0.25 mg weekly and is increased every 4 weeks over 16 weeks until 2.4 mg weekly. **Teaching point:** Obesity semaglutide: 0.25 mg weekly, up-titrated every 4 weeks over 16 weeks to 2.4 mg.

### Q31
**Topic:** Duration of pharmacotherapy

A 47-year-old woman lost 9 kg over 5 months on a GLP1-RA and feels that she has achieved her goal. She wants to stop the drug.

B. Weight is maintained for 5 years after any course

### Q32
**Topic:** Drug choice in a patient with epilepsy (long clinical stem)

A 36-year-old woman with obesity (BMI 37 kg/m2) has epilepsy controlled on carbamazepine. She is not planning pregnancy and has regular contraception. She has no diabetes. She wants a medication in addition to diet.

**Which agent is the least appropriate for her?**

A. Orlistat
B. Naltrexone/bupropion

### Q35
**Topic:** Relative efficacy of procedures

A 39-year-old woman is choosing between sleeve gastrectomy, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and adjustable gastric banding.

Adapted from CPG Section 4.3.1 (percentage weight loss at 1 year and 5 years):

| Procedure | 1-year loss | 5-year loss |
|---|---|---|
| RYGB | 31.2% | 25.5% |
| Sleeve gastrectomy | 25.2% | 18.8% |
| Gastric banding | 13.7% | 11.7% |

**Which statement best reflects the table?**

A. Banding gives the greatest weight loss at both time points
B. RYGB gives the greatest weight loss at 1 and 5 years, and banding the least
C. Sleeve gastrectomy outperforms RYGB at 5 years
D. All three procedures give equal loss at 5 years

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** RYGB is best at both time points (31.2% and 25.5%), sleeve gastrectomy is intermediate (25.2% and 18.8%), and banding is lowest (13.7% and 11.7%). In SM-BOSS there was no significant difference between RYGB and sleeve at 1, 2 and 5 years, but RYGB gave more gastric reflux remission (60.4% versus 25%). **Teaching point:** RYGB > sleeve > banding for weight loss, and RYGB resolves reflux more often than sleeve.

### Q36
**Topic:** Reflux and procedure choice

A 44-year-old man with BMI 42 kg/m2 and T2DM has severe, daily gastro-oesophageal reflux with a hiatus hernia on endoscopy. He is choosing a bariatric operation.

**Which operation is most appropriate for his reflux?**

A. Sleeve gastrectomy
B. Intragastric balloon

**Topic:** Contraindications

### Q38
**Topic:** Preoperative preparation (long clinical stem)

A 47-year-old man with BMI 45 kg/m2, hypertension, T2DM and a STOP-BANG score of 6 is listed for laparoscopic RYGB in 6 weeks. His surgeon asks about preparation.

**Which plan best fits the CPG?**

A. Operate now; weight loss before surgery is pointless
B. Screen for OSA with STOP-BANG, consider perioperative CPAP, and encourage preoperative weight loss of at least 5%
C. Cancel surgery because OSA is present
D. Skip psychological assessment to save time

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Comprehensive pre-operative assessment includes psychological assessment, surgical risk and comorbidities. ### Q39
**Topic:** Post-operative care and complications

A 38-year-old woman had a Roux-en-Y gastric bypass 5 months ago. She presents with epigastric pain that is relieved by food, and a black stool this morning. She takes ibuprofen for headaches and has not been taking her recommended supplements.

**Which complication is most likely, and what prevention was recommended?**

A. Marginal ulcer; PPI prophylaxis
B. Band slippage; antibiotic prophylaxis
C. Balloon migration; early removal
D. Gallbladder rupture; ursodeoxycholic acid

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Marginal ulcer formation is a recognised complication of RYGB (and one-anastomosis gastric bypass), and post-operative care includes proton-pump inhibitors to prevent it, plus micronutrient supplementation, behavioural and nutritional advice and delaying pregnancy until weight stabilises. Band slippage applies to adjustable gastric banding, and balloon migration to intragastric balloons. NSAIDs raise ulcer risk.

**Teaching point:** After gastric bypass: PPI to prevent marginal ulcers, supplements, nutritional follow-up and delay of pregnancy.

### Q40
**Topic:** Intragastric balloon

A 36-year-old woman with BMI 34 kg/m2 who declined surgery has had a fluid-filled intragastric balloon for 6 months. It is due for removal.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Weight loss is permanent after removal
B. Weight regain is expected after removal; benefit over lifestyle changes is lost by 10 years
C. Balloons never cause serious adverse events
D. H. pylori testing is unnecessary before placement

**Topic:** Defining obesity in a child

A 9-year-old boy has BMI 22.6 kg/m2. On the WHO 2007 growth reference his BMI is 2.3 standard deviations above the median.

| Age | Overweight | Obese |
|---|---|---|

**How is he classified?**

B. Overweight
C. Obese

### Q42
**Topic:** Secondary obesity in a child (long clinical stem)

A 7-year-old girl has gained 12 kg in a year. Her height has dropped from the 50th to the 9th centile over 2 years despite continued weight gain. She has fatigue, constipation and dry skin. Her parents are of average height and she eats a normal diet.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Reassure; primary obesity causes accelerated growth, so this is normal
B. Refer for evaluation of an endocrine cause, such as hypothyroidism
C. Start orlistat
D. Refer for bariatric surgery

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** More than 95% of childhood obesity is primary (exogenous) and is usually associated with accelerated linear growth. **Which classification is most appropriate?**
