### Q1
**Topic:** Friedewald equation validity

A 52-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has a non-fasting lipid profile: TC 8.9 mmol/l, HDL-C 0.8 mmol/l and TG 6.2 mmol/l. The laboratory reports "LDL-C: calculated" at 5.3 mmol/l. The GP wants to titrate his statin to an LDL-C target.

Friedewald: LDL-C (mmol/l) = TC - HDL-C - TG/2.2

**What is the most appropriate interpretation and next step?**

A. Equation is valid at any TG; titrate to the reported LDL-C
B. Equation fails above TG 4.5; repeat fasting and use non-HDL-C
C. Equation fails above TG 10; use HDL-C as the target instead
D. Non-fasting samples are never acceptable; stop the statin

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Friedewald equation is not valid when TG exceeds 4.5 mmol/l, so a calculated LDL-C is unreliable here. The CPG says a patient with TG above 4.5 mmol/l should have a repeat lipid panel in the fasting state, and non-HDL-C (TC minus HDL-C, here 8.1 mmol/l) becomes the primary target of therapy. The first answer misapplies the equation, and non-fasting testing is acceptable in general, but fasting is preferred when non-fasting TG is above 4.5 mmol/l (above 2.3 mmol/l in diabetics).

**Teaching point:** TG >4.5 mmol/l: Friedewald LDL-C fails, so repeat fasting and use non-HDL-C as the primary target.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.1-2.4; Key Recommendation 5.

### Q2
**Topic:** Non-HDL-C target

A 58-year-old woman with high cardiovascular risk (target LDL-C ≤1.8 mmol/l) has TG 5.1 mmol/l. Her treating physician wants to use non-HDL-C as the treatment target.

**What non-HDL-C target corresponds to her LDL-C target?**

A. 1.8 mmol/l, the same as the LDL-C target
B. 2.2 mmol/l, which is 0.4 mmol/l above the LDL-C target
C. 3.4 mmol/l, which is 1.6 mmol/l above the LDL-C target
D. 2.6 mmol/l, which is 0.8 mmol/l above the LDL-C target

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** A non-HDL-C target is 0.8 mmol/l higher than the corresponding LDL-C target. For high risk (LDL-C ≤1.8) the non-HDL-C target is ≤2.6 mmol/l, with a reduction of more than 50% from baseline. For very high risk it is ≤2.2 mmol/l (LDL-C ≤1.4), and for moderate risk <3.4 mmol/l (LDL-C <2.6). Non-HDL-C is the primary target when TG exceeds 4.5 mmol/l.

**Teaching point:** Non-HDL-C target = LDL-C target + 0.8 mmol/l. High risk ≤2.6, very high risk ≤2.2.

**Reference:** CPG Section 2.2; Table 4.

### Q3
**Topic:** Risk category and target (long clinical stem)

A 63-year-old man has diabetes for 14 years with no established CVD. He has microalbuminuria (target organ damage) and hypertension. His LDL-C is 2.7 mmol/l on atorvastatin 20 mg.

Adapted from CPG Table 4 (target LDL-C levels):

| Risk category | Examples | Target LDL-C (mmol/l) |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate | 10-20% 10-year risk; diabetes <50 y, <10 y, no risk factors | <2.6 |
| High | >20% 10-year risk; diabetes >10 y without organ damage + 1 risk factor; CKD eGFR 30-<60 | ≤1.8 and >50% reduction |
| Very high | Established CVD; diabetes with CVD or target organ damage or >3 risk factors; CKD eGFR <30 | ≤1.4 and >50% reduction |

**What is his risk category and LDL-C target?**

A. Moderate risk; <2.6 mmol/l
B. High risk; ≤1.8 mmol/l
C. Low risk; clinical judgement
D. Very high risk; ≤1.4 mmol/l

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Diabetes with target organ damage (microalbuminuria) or more than 3 other CV risk factors is very high risk, even without established CVD, so the target is LDL-C ≤1.4 mmol/l with a reduction of more than 50% from baseline. High risk applies to diabetes over 10 years without target organ damage plus one other risk factor, which is the near-miss. His current 2.7 mmol/l is far above target, so statin intensification and ezetimibe will be needed.

**Teaching point:** Diabetes with target organ damage is very high risk: LDL-C ≤1.4 mmol/l.

**Reference:** CPG Table 4; Section 10.3.

### Q4
**Topic:** Very-high-risk recurrent events

A 61-year-old man had an MI 18 months ago. His LDL-C has been 1.3 mmol/l on rosuvastatin 40 mg plus ezetimibe. He now has a second MI.

**According to Table 4, what is the LDL-C goal for recurrent events within 2 years despite achieving <1.4 mmol/l?**

A. <1.8 mmol/l
B. <1.4 mmol/l
C. <1.2 mmol/l
D. <1.0 mmol/l

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** For patients with recurrent CV events within 2 years despite achieving an LDL-C of below 1.4 mmol/l, the CPG's Table 4 suggests a target of below 1.0 mmol/l. All other risk factors should also be treated to target, and a PCSK9 inhibitor would typically be considered if statin plus ezetimibe cannot reach it.

**Teaching point:** Recurrent event within 2 years despite LDL-C <1.4: aim for <1.0 mmol/l.

**Reference:** CPG Table 4 (footnote).

### Q5
**Topic:** Statin intensity classification

A 66-year-old woman with stable angina is prescribed a statin at the following doses by different doctors.

Adapted from CPG Table 12 (statin intensity):

| Intensity | Lowers LDL-C by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| High | ≥50% | Atorvastatin 40-80 mg; rosuvastatin 20-40 mg; simvastatin 20-40 mg |
| Moderate | 30% to <50% | Atorvastatin 10-20 mg; rosuvastatin 5-10 mg; pitavastatin 1 mg |
| Low | <30% | Simvastatin 10 mg; pravastatin 10-20 mg |

**Which of the following is a high-intensity regimen according to the table?**

A. Atorvastatin 20 mg
B. Rosuvastatin 10 mg
C. Pravastatin 20 mg
D. Simvastatin 40 mg

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** In the CPG's Table 12, simvastatin 20-40 mg is listed under high intensity along with atorvastatin 40-80 mg and rosuvastatin 20-40 mg. Atorvastatin 20 mg and rosuvastatin 10 mg are moderate, and pravastatin 20 mg is low intensity. High-intensity statins are intended for very high and high CV risk patients and reduce LDL-C by roughly 50% or more.

**Teaching point:** High intensity: atorvastatin 40-80, rosuvastatin 20-40, simvastatin 20-40 mg (per the CPG table).

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1.1; Table 12.

### Q6
**Topic:** Doubling the statin versus adding ezetimibe

A 59-year-old man post-MI takes atorvastatin 40 mg daily with an LDL-C of 1.9 mmol/l (target ≤1.4 mmol/l). He is adherent. His doctor wants to increase the dose to 80 mg.

**What does the CPG say about the incremental effect of doubling the statin dose versus adding ezetimibe?**

A. Doubling the statin gives about 25% more LDL-C reduction, and ezetimibe gives about 6%
B. Doubling the statin gives about 6% more reduction; adding ezetimibe 10 mg gives about 25% more
C. Both give about 6% additional reduction
D. Doubling the statin gives about 15%, and ezetimibe about 15%

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Adding ezetimibe 10 mg to any statin dose lowers LDL-C by an additional 25%, which is far greater than the 6% obtained when the statin dose is doubled, because ezetimibe adds dual inhibition (liver production and gut absorption). It can be combined with any dose of statin when targets are not achieved with the maximum tolerated statin. For him the gap is 26% (1.9 to 1.4), so ezetimibe is a more efficient step.

**Teaching point:** Doubling a statin adds ~6% LDL-C lowering. Adding ezetimibe adds ~25%.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.2.1.

### Q7
**Topic:** Monitoring after starting a statin

A 55-year-old woman with high risk is started on atorvastatin 40 mg. She asks when her blood tests will be done and how often afterwards.

**Which monitoring plan matches the CPG?**

A. Lipids and ALT every month for the first year, then yearly
B. Lipids and ALT at 1-3 months, then lipids every 6-12 months
C. Lipids at 12 months only, with CK each visit
D. ALT every 3 months for life, with lipids only if symptomatic

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The CPG advises lipid profile and hepatic transaminases at baseline and 1-3 months after starting or changing the dose, then lipids every 6 to 12 months once stable (more often if adherence is a concern). Routine long-term ALT monitoring is no longer recommended, and CK is measured only if myositis is suspected. If targets are met, the same dose is maintained and the drug is not stopped.

**Teaching point:** Statins: lipids and ALT at 1-3 months, then lipids every 6-12 months. Routine long-term ALT is not needed.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1.3; Key Recommendation 7.

### Q8
**Topic:** Raised ALT on a statin

A 62-year-old man with fatty liver has a baseline ALT of 1.5 x ULN. Three months after starting atorvastatin 40 mg, ALT is 2.2 x ULN and he feels well. A colleague says to stop the statin.

**What is the most appropriate action?**

A. Switch to a fibrate because fatty liver is a contraindication
B. Stop permanently, since ALT has doubled from baseline
C. Continue; stop only if ALT exceeds 3 x ULN twice
D. Stop until ALT has been normal for 6 months

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Mild ALT elevation (under 3 times ULN) with statins is common in under 3% and has not been shown to cause true hepatotoxicity. A mildly raised baseline ALT, for example from fatty liver, is not a contraindication, and the statin should continue. The drug is stopped when ALT is above 3 x ULN on two occasions, and cautious reintroduction may be considered once ALT normalises. Progression to liver failure is exceedingly rare.

**Teaching point:** Stop a statin only if ALT >3 x ULN on two occasions. Fatty liver is not a contraindication.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1.3 (Liver function).

### Q9
**Topic:** Timing of statin doses

A 57-year-old man takes simvastatin 40 mg in the morning with coffee and atorvastatin was previously taken in the evening. He asks whether timing matters.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Short half-life statins at night; atorvastatin at any time
B. Statin timing has no effect on any statin
C. All statins must be taken at bedtime
D. Atorvastatin must be taken in the morning only

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Cholesterol is synthesised mostly in the early morning, so statins with shorter half-lives (pravastatin, lovastatin, simvastatin, fluvastatin) should be given in the evening, while long half-life statins (atorvastatin 14 h, rosuvastatin 19 h, pitavastatin 22 h) can be given any time. LDL-C fell 5-8% more when simvastatin was taken in the evening.

**Teaching point:** Short half-life statins (simvastatin, pravastatin, lovastatin) in the evening. Atorvastatin and rosuvastatin any time.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1.2.

### Q10
**Topic:** PCSK9 inhibitors

A 49-year-old man with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia and prior MI has LDL-C 3.0 mmol/l on maximal rosuvastatin plus ezetimibe. His physician plans a PCSK9 inhibitor.

**Which statement is correct?**

A. About 60% lowering; frequent liver failure; avoid in pregnancy
B. About 60% lowering; subcutaneous every 2-4 weeks; avoid in pregnancy
C. About 20% lowering; oral daily; safe in pregnancy
D. About 60% lowering; licensed for children under 8 years

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Alirocumab and evolocumab lower LDL-C by about 60% on average, added to statins or other therapies. They are given subcutaneously (evolocumab 140 mg every 2 weeks or 420 mg monthly; alirocumab 75-150 mg every 2 weeks or 300 mg monthly). Side effects are mainly injection-site itching and flu-like symptoms, with no adverse liver effects (under 2% had LFTs above 3 x ULN). They cross the placenta, so should not be used in pregnancy, and are not licensed in children.

**Teaching point:** PCSK9 mAbs: ~60% LDL-C reduction, subcutaneous, safe for the liver, avoid in pregnancy.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.3.

### Q11
**Topic:** Inclisiran

A 64-year-old woman with CVD and LDL-C 1.9 mmol/l on statin plus ezetimibe is offered inclisiran.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Oral agent, ~20% fall; outcome benefit proven
B. siRNA, ~50% fall; contraindicated in mild CKD
C. Monoclonal antibody, ~60% fall; outcome benefit proven
D. siRNA, ~50% LDL-C fall; outcome benefit not yet proven

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Inclisiran is a long-acting synthetic siRNA that inhibits translation of PCSK9 mRNA, lowering LDL-C by about 50%, given subcutaneously. Its safety profile approximates placebo, with ALT above 3 x ULN in about 1%, and it was safe in mild to moderate CKD. Clinical evidence of reduction in atherosclerotic CV events is still awaited, which explains the IIa,B grade. It is a siRNA, not a monoclonal antibody.

**Teaching point:** Inclisiran: siRNA, ~50% LDL-C lowering, outcome data awaited (IIa,B).

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.5.1.

### Q12
**Topic:** Fibrates and statins

A 54-year-old man with diabetes on maximally tolerated rosuvastatin has an LDL-C at target but TG 3.4 mmol/l and HDL-C 0.85 mmol/l. A fibrate is considered. He also has an eGFR of 48 ml/min/1.73 m2.

**Which approach is most appropriate?**

A. A non-gemfibrozil fibrate with a statin may be considered (IIb)
B. Gemfibrozil with the statin, as the preferred fibrate
C. Fibrates cut CV events in all secondary prevention trials
D. Fibrates are first-line in all diabetic patients

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In diabetics on maximally tolerated statins with low HDL-C (≤0.88) and high TG (≥2.3 mmol/l), fibrates may be considered (IIb,B), though a recent trial (PROMINENT with pemafibrate) was neutral. Gemfibrozil is not recommended in combination with statins because of rhabdomyolysis risk, especially with kidney dysfunction. Doses need adjusting in CKD, and with eGFR below 60 the combination with fibrates should be avoided because muscle symptoms are more likely. Fibrates have not reduced CV events in secondary prevention trials.

**Teaching point:** Avoid gemfibrozil with statins. With eGFR <60, avoid statin-fibrate combinations.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.4; Key Recommendation 14; Table 13.

### Q13
**Topic:** Omega-3 fatty acids and atrial fibrillation

A 67-year-old man with high TG is taking over-the-counter fish oil 4 g/day. He is now found to have palpitations and an irregular pulse.

**Which statement is consistent with the CPG?**

A. REDUCE-IT preparation is available locally and safe
B. 4 g/day nearly doubled AF risk; warn and follow up
C. No AF risk at any dose; continue unchanged
D. 4 g/day halved AF risk; no follow-up is needed

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** There appears to be a dose-related AF risk with omega-3 fatty acids: at 4.0 g/day there is a highly significant, nearly doubled risk, and lower doses (1.8 g/day, 840 mg/day) showed non-significant increases. Patients choosing high doses should be informed and followed for AF. The icosapent ethyl preparation used in REDUCE-IT is not available in Malaysia, and CV benefit of omega-3 remains uncertain (two trials positive, four neutral).

**Teaching point:** High-dose omega-3 (4 g/day) nearly doubles AF risk. CV benefit is uncertain.

**Reference:** CPG Section 11.1.

### Q14
**Topic:** Severe hypertriglyceridaemia (long clinical stem)

A 45-year-old man has no symptoms. A routine fasting TG is 14.2 mmol/l. He drinks 5 beers a night and has a BMI of 32 kg/m2. HbA1c is 6.2%. He has no abdominal pain and amylase is normal.

**Which management best matches the CPG?**

A. Start niacin 3 g/day as the first step
B. Immediate IV insulin infusion and ICU admission
C. Repeat fasting TG in 5-14 days, stop alcohol, low-fat diet, statin
D. Wait for pancreatitis before any treatment

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** In asymptomatic severe hypertriglyceridaemia (above 10 mmol/l), the CPG advises repeating a fasting TG after 5 days but within 2 weeks, reviewing secondary causes (alcohol, diabetes, drugs), seeking specialist advice if it stays above 10 mmol/l, and dietary modification (very low carbohydrate and a very low-fat diet of 10-15% or less of calories, preferably under 5%), plus avoiding alcohol. A statin is the drug of choice, with fibrate, high-dose omega-3 and/or niacin considered for TG above 5.6. IV insulin is for severe hypertriglyceridaemia with uncontrolled diabetes, and niacin is not routinely recommended.

**Teaching point:** Asymptomatic TG >10 mmol/l: repeat fasting in 5-14 days, find secondary causes, very low fat diet, stop alcohol, statin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 11.1(B).

### Q15
**Topic:** Hypertriglyceridaemia with uncontrolled diabetes

A 38-year-old woman with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes in diabetic ketoacidosis has a lipaemic blood sample with TG 22 mmol/l.

**Which treatment does the CPG recommend for severe hypertriglyceridaemia associated with uncontrolled diabetes?**

A. IV insulin infusion, which stimulates lipoprotein lipase
B. High-dose atorvastatin alone to clear TG in hours
C. Nicotinic acid 3 g/day as initial therapy
D. Plasma exchange as the only recommended option

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Severe hypertriglyceridaemia with uncontrolled diabetes warrants an IV insulin infusion, because insulin stimulates intravascular lipoprotein lipase and so clears TG faster. TG improves within 2-5 days but may not normalise. Nicotinic acid is not routinely recommended given its limited benefit and the risk of worsening insulin resistance. If pancreatitis is suspected, a fibrate or nicotinic acid can be used, and omega-3 may be safe in the early phase.

**Teaching point:** Severe TG with uncontrolled diabetes: IV insulin. Niacin is not routine.

**Reference:** CPG Section 11.1(B).

### Q16
**Topic:** LDL-C and TG elevation

A 56-year-old man has TG 2.8 mmol/l, HDL-C 0.9 mmol/l and LDL-C 3.4 mmol/l, with high CV risk.

**What is the primary goal of therapy?**

A. Lowering TG to below 1.7 mmol/l
B. Raising HDL-C above 1.3 mmol/l with niacin
C. Lowering LDL-C to target for his risk category
D. Lowering Lp(a) to below 30 mg/dl

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** In patients with high TG and/or low HDL-C, the primary goal of treatment remains lowering LDL-C to target. TG lowering has not been shown to reduce CV risk, and pharmacological manipulation of HDL-C has not improved CV outcomes. A fasting TG above 1.7 mmol/l is a risk marker but ≤1.7 is not an evidence-based target. Lifestyle changes (weight loss, less simple carbohydrate, exercise, less alcohol) help both TG and HDL-C.

**Teaching point:** High TG or low HDL-C: still treat LDL-C to target first.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 18; Section 11.2.

### Q17
**Topic:** Statin intolerance alternatives

A 60-year-old woman with very high CV risk developed intolerable myalgia on three different statins, with CK normal and symptoms resolving on stopping.

**Which option does the CPG list for her?**

A. High-dose niacin as the first alternative
B. No lipid therapy is needed
C. Ezetimibe, or a PCSK9 inhibitor if very high or high risk
D. Gemfibrozil as the first alternative

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For statin intolerance, ezetimibe monotherapy is an option (IIa,B), and PCSK9 inhibitors are recommended in very high and high risk patients with statin intolerance (I,A). Ezetimibe plus bempedoic acid is also listed for statin intolerant patients (IIa,B). The approach to adverse effects is to reduce the dose or stop temporarily, reintroduce at a lower dose once normalised, and if it recurs, stop and use an alternative.

**Teaching point:** Statin intolerance: ezetimibe ± bempedoic acid, or a PCSK9 inhibitor in high-risk patients.

**Reference:** CPG Table 6; Key Recommendation 7.

### Q18
**Topic:** Statins and pregnancy

A 34-year-old woman with familial hypercholesterolaemia and prior MI asks about her statin because she is planning a pregnancy next year.

**Which statement is correct?**

A. Avoid; if essential use a hydrophilic statin (rosuvastatin)
B. Safe at any dose; use a lipophilic statin
C. Safe throughout pregnancy; start in the third trimester
D. Avoid; if essential use a lipophilic statin (simvastatin)

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Statin therapy should be avoided in pregnancy and lactation unless there is a strong clinical indication, and should not be prescribed to women of childbearing potential without adequate contraception. Earlier teratogenicity concerns have not been consistently shown in human studies, and most malformations occurred with lipophilic statins (atorvastatin, lovastatin, simvastatin) rather than hydrophilic ones (pravastatin, rosuvastatin). If indicated, a hydrophilic statin is preferred. PCSK9 antibodies cross the placenta and are avoided.

**Teaching point:** Avoid statins in pregnancy and lactation. If essential, use a hydrophilic statin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1.2; Section 12.1.1.

### Q19
**Topic:** Heart failure and statins

A 68-year-old man has heart failure with reduced ejection fraction due to dilated cardiomyopathy (normal coronary angiogram) and LDL-C 3.2 mmol/l. A second patient has heart failure due to ischaemic cardiomyopathy.

**Which statement matches the CPG?**

A. No statins in any heart failure
B. Statins if due to CAD; not routine in non-ischaemic HF
C. Statins for non-ischaemic HF only
D. Statins for all heart failure

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** All patients with HF due to CAD should be on statins (IIa,B), whereas routine use of cholesterol-lowering therapy is not recommended in non-ischaemic HF (IIa,B). The first patient has no coronary disease, so routine lipid lowering is not advised unless another indication exists.

**Teaching point:** Heart failure: statins if due to CAD, not routinely for non-ischaemic HF.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 13; Section 10.4.

### Q20
**Topic:** Kidney disease and dialysis (long clinical stem)

Three patients are reviewed. P1 is a 62-year-old man with CKD stage 4 (eGFR 22), not on dialysis, with no CVD. P2 is a 58-year-old man starting haemodialysis, with no prior CVD and no statin. P3 is a 60-year-old woman with previous MI, on atorvastatin, who is starting dialysis.

**Which combination is correct?**

A. P1 no statin; P2 start; P3 continue
B. P1 treat; P2 start; P3 continue
C. P1 treat; P2 start; P3 stop
D. P1 treat; P2 do not start (primary); P3 continue

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Statins or ezetimibe/simvastatin should be started in non-dialysis CKD stages 3-5 for primary and secondary prevention (I,A). Statins should not be started for primary prevention in dialysis patients (III,B), because their CV disease is mostly non-atherosclerotic (medial calcific arteriosclerosis, LVH, arrhythmia) and no benefit has been shown. In patients with established CVD or already on a statin at the start of dialysis, therapy should be continued (IIa,C). CKD eGFR below 30 is very high risk, with a target LDL-C of ≤1.4.

**Teaching point:** Non-dialysis CKD: treat. New dialysis with no CVD: do not start. Already on a statin or has CVD: continue.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 14; Table 4 footnote.

### Q21
**Topic:** Statin dose in CKD

A 70-year-old woman with CKD stage 3a (eGFR 55 ml/min/1.73 m2) and high CV risk is starting a statin. She is also taking a fibrate for TG.

Adapted from CPG Table 15 (KDIGO doses in CKD stage 3a or worse):

| Agent | Dose (mg/day) |
|---|---|
| Atorvastatin | 20 |
| Fluvastatin | 80 |
| Pravastatin | 40 |
| Rosuvastatin | 10 |
| Simvastatin | 40 |
| Simvastatin/ezetimibe | 20/10 |

**Which advice is correct?**

A. Start at a lower dose and avoid fibrate combinations
B. Avoid statins in CKD stage 3
C. Use rosuvastatin 40 mg with gemfibrozil
D. Use atorvastatin 80 mg and add the fibrate

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** With eGFR below 60, the risk of muscle-related symptoms is higher, so statins should be started at a lower dose and combination with fibrates should be avoided (IIa,B). The KDIGO-based doses are atorvastatin 20 mg, rosuvastatin 10 mg, simvastatin 40 mg (or simvastatin/ezetimibe 20/10 mg), and so on. Lipid-lowering therapy lowers the risk of atherosclerotic CVD in CKD, so avoiding statins is wrong.

**Teaching point:** eGFR <60: start statins at lower doses and avoid fibrate combinations.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 14; Table 15.

### Q22
**Topic:** Hypothyroidism

A 55-year-old woman has TC 8.1 mmol/l and LDL-C 5.6 mmol/l. TSH is 38 mU/l with low free T4. Her GP wants to start rosuvastatin 20 mg today.

**What is the most appropriate approach?**

A. Use a fibrate, as statins are contraindicated
B. Start the statin now and treat thyroid later
C. Ignore TSH, as thyroid disease does not alter lipids
D. Treat hypothyroidism, then reassess lipids once euthyroid

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Appropriate treatment of hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism can improve lipid abnormalities (I,A), and the lipid profile should be reassessed once the patient is euthyroid before starting lipid-lowering therapy (I,C). When TC exceeds 7.0 mmol/l, secondary causes such as primary hypothyroidism, nephrosis and cholestatic liver disease should be excluded. Hypothyroidism is more prevalent in the elderly.

**Teaching point:** TC >7 mmol/l: exclude hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome and cholestasis. Reassess lipids when euthyroid.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2; Key Recommendation 15.

### Q23
**Topic:** Hypertension and statins

A 57-year-old man with hypertension, no diabetes and no CVD has LDL-C 3.8 mmol/l. Another hypertensive patient has LDL-C 2.8 mmol/l. Neither has been risk scored.

**Which approach matches the CPG?**

A. No statin unless an event occurs
B. Statin only if BP exceeds 160/100 mmHg
C. Statin if LDL-C >3.4; otherwise risk-score with Framingham

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For hypertensive patients, statins are initiated for primary prevention if LDL-C exceeds 3.4 mmol/l (I,A). In all others, CV risk is assessed with the FRS-General CVD score and the LDL-C target depends on the risk category (Table 4).

**Teaching point:** Hypertension with LDL-C >3.4: statin. Otherwise risk-score with the Framingham score.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 11.

### Q24
**Topic:** Asymptomatic atherosclerosis upgrading risk (long clinical stem)

A 58-year-old man with intermediate Framingham risk (14%) and an LDL-C of 3.1 mmol/l has no symptoms. His ankle-brachial index is 1.45. A carotid ultrasound shows a plaque 1.4 mm thick. His coronary calcium score is 130 Agatston units. His treadmill test is normal.

**What is the most appropriate classification and target?**

A. Established disease: upgrade risk, aim LDL-C <1.4 and >50% fall
B. Low risk: clinical judgement only
C. High risk: aim LDL-C ≤1.8 only if symptoms appear
D. Intermediate risk: aim LDL-C <2.6

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In intermediate-risk patients, features indicating established atherosclerotic CVD include an ABI below 0.9 or above 1.4 (calcified non-compressible vessels), a positive exercise test at ≤6 METS, a calcium score ≥100 Agatston units (or above the 75th percentile), CT angiography plaque with over 50% narrowing, and carotid plaque encroaching at least 50% on the lumen or thicker than 1.2 mm. He has several (ABI 1.45, calcium 130, plaque 1.4 mm), so risk is upgraded and the target is LDL-C <1.4 mmol/l with at least a 50% reduction (I,A).

**Teaching point:** ABI <0.9 or >1.4, calcium ≥100, carotid plaque >1.2 mm: treat as established CVD (LDL-C <1.4).

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 10; Section 10.1.

### Q25
**Topic:** Calcium score decision

A 56-year-old woman with intermediate Framingham risk, non-smoker, no diabetes and no family history of premature CAD, has a coronary artery calcium score of 0 Agatston units.

**What does the CPG suggest?**

A. Repeat calcium score yearly
B. Reassess in 5-10 years if no diabetes, family history or smoking
C. A score of 0 means plaque is present, so start statins
D. Start a high-intensity statin immediately

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A calcium score of 0 suggests low short-term risk: reassessment is advised in 5 to 10 years if diabetes, a family history of premature CAD and cigarette smoking are absent. A score of 1-99 (below the 75th percentile) makes it reasonable to initiate statins in those over 55, and a score of 100 or more (or above the 75th percentile) makes it reasonable to start statins.

**Teaching point:** Calcium 0: reassess in 5-10 years. 1-99: statin if over 55. ≥100: statin.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 10.

### Q26
**Topic:** Secondary prevention timing

A 63-year-old man is admitted with an NSTEMI. His LDL-C on admission is 2.1 mmol/l, which he says is "normal". He has never taken a statin. He is going for PCI tomorrow.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. Statin after discharge if LDL-C exceeds 2.6
B. Delay any statin until 6 weeks after PCI
C. High-intensity statin on admission, whatever the LDL-C
D. Low-intensity statin only, before PCI

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** High-intensity statins should be started on admission in all individuals with ACS, and before PCI and CABG, then continued indefinitely, regardless of baseline cholesterol (I,A). The secondary prevention target is an LDL-C below 1.4 mmol/l with a 50% reduction. All patients with CVD should receive lipid-lowering therapy, and statins should be started after any non-cardioembolic ischaemic stroke or TIA.

**Teaching point:** ACS: high-intensity statin on admission, whatever the baseline LDL-C, and continue indefinitely.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 9.

### Q27
**Topic:** Psychiatric patients

A 44-year-old man with schizophrenia on olanzapine has TC 6.8 mmol/l, LDL-C 4.6 mmol/l and a high CV risk score. His psychiatrist asks which lipid target and drug to prefer.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. LDL-C is the primary target and statins are the drug of first choice
B. TG is the primary target and fibrates are the first choice
C. HDL-C is the primary target and niacin is the first choice
D. Lipid targets do not apply in psychiatric patients

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In patients with psychiatric disorders, LDL-C is the primary target of therapy (I,A) and statins are the drugs of first choice (I,A). The same principle applies in HIV, where LDL-C is the primary goal, drug interactions with antiretroviral therapy are common, and monitoring for adverse effects is important (I,C).

**Teaching point:** In psychiatric illness, as in HIV: LDL-C target and statin first.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendations 16 and 17.

### Q28
**Topic:** Children and familial hypercholesterolaemia

A 12-year-old girl has LDL-C 6.2 mmol/l, tendon xanthomata and a father who had an MI at 38. Three months of lifestyle changes have not lowered her LDL-C below 5.0.

**Which statement is correct?**

A. Fibrate as first-line in children
B. Refer; statin (pravastatin or rosuvastatin from 8 years), target <4.2
C. Niacin as first-line in children
D. Wait until age 18 before any drug

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Children with significantly elevated lipids may have a genetic dyslipidaemia and should be referred to specialists. Targets: LDL-C <4.9 mmol/l for children aged 10 and over, and <4.2 mmol/l for those with a clinical presentation of FH who do not respond to lifestyle change after 3-6 months. Statins are the drug of choice in FH: all statins can be used from age 10 and pravastatin and rosuvastatin from 8 years. Fibrates, niacin and omega-3 lack paediatric safety and efficacy data, and ezetimibe is an alternative adjunct.

**Teaching point:** Paediatric FH: statins (pravastatin or rosuvastatin from 8, others from 10), with LDL-C <4.2 mmol/l if FH.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2.

### Q29
**Topic:** The elderly

A 76-year-old man with a previous MI has LDL-C 3.0 mmol/l and is not on a statin because his daughter says "he is too old". He is independent and cognitively intact.

**Which statement is consistent with the CPG?**

A. Less absolute benefit in secondary prevention in the elderly
B. Statins are for primary prevention only in the elderly
C. Greater absolute benefit in secondary prevention; do not withhold for age
D. Statins are contraindicated over 70

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For secondary prevention, the elderly derive a greater absolute benefit from lipid-lowering therapy and should not be deprived solely on the basis of age, although trial data over 80 years are limited. In primary prevention, results are mixed, with statin therapy considered in older adults under 80 free from CVD, particularly with hypertension, smoking, diabetes and dyslipidaemia. Treatment must be individualised to function, comorbidity and cognition.

**Teaching point:** Do not withhold statins for secondary prevention because of age alone.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.3.

### Q30
**Topic:** Lifestyle targets (long clinical stem)

A 49-year-old man with LDL-C 3.9 mmol/l, BMI 29 kg/m2 and waist 98 cm asks for specific lifestyle targets. He drinks sweetened juices, has a high-salt diet and eats few vegetables.

Adapted from CPG Table 5 (therapeutic lifestyle changes):

| Domain | Target |
|---|---|
| Saturated fat | <10% of calories, replaced by PUFA |
| Trans fat | <1% of calories |
| Sodium | <2000 mg/day (about 5 g salt) |
| Fibre | 20-30 g/day, soluble 7-13 g |
| Weight | BMI <23 kg/m2 or 5-10% loss; waist <90 cm (men), <80 cm (women) |
| Exercise | 150-300 min/week moderate or 75-150 min/week vigorous |

**Which advice matches the CPG?**

A. Sat fat <10%, trans fat <1%, sodium <2000 mg, fibre 20-30 g
B. Sat fat <20%, trans fat <5%, unlimited juicing
C. Sodium <5 g, waist <102 cm, exercise 60 min weekly
D. Sat fat <10%, trans fat <10%, exercise 30 min monthly

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG sets saturated fat below 10% of total calories (replace with PUFA), trans fat below 1%, sodium below 2000 mg daily (equivalent to 5 g of salt, one level teaspoon), fibre 20-30 g (7-13 g soluble), BMI below 23 or 5-10% weight reduction, waist below 90 cm for men and 80 cm for women, and 150-300 minutes of moderate (or 75-150 of vigorous) exercise weekly. Juicing removes fibre, so is not recommended. The emphasis is on dietary patterns (fruit, vegetables, whole grains, plant protein, fish, nuts) rather than individual nutrients.

**Teaching point:** Saturated fat <10%, trans fat <1%, sodium <2000 mg, fibre 20-30 g, waist <90/80 cm, exercise 150-300 min/week.

**Reference:** CPG Table 5; Key Recommendation 6.

### Q31
**Topic:** Screening frequency

A 34-year-old woman with a normal lipid profile (LDL-C at target, TG low) and low risk, and a 41-year-old man with diabetes and a family history of premature CVD, both had lipid screening today.

**Which re-screening intervals match the CPG?**

A. Every 6 months if normal; every 2 years if high risk
B. Every 10 years if normal; every 5 years if high risk
C. Annually for both
D. Every 3 years if normal; annually if very high or high risk

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The CPG advocates lipid profile screening for all individuals over 30. If LDL-C is at target and TG is low, screening is repeated every 3 years. In individuals at very high or high CV risk (diabetes, family history of premature CVD, etc.), screening is repeated annually.

**Teaching point:** Screen all adults >30. Repeat every 3 years if normal, annually if high risk.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 8.

### Q32
**Topic:** Cushing syndrome

A 41-year-old woman with persistent endogenous Cushing syndrome has LDL-C 3.2 mmol/l and a low calculated CV risk score. Her endocrinologist asks whether lipid therapy is warranted.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Niacin alone, aiming for LDL-C <2.6
B. No statin, since her risk score is low
C. Consider a statin whatever the risk score; LDL-C <1.8
D. Statins are contraindicated in Cushing syndrome

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** In adults with persistent endogenous Cushing syndrome, statin therapy (as an adjunct to lifestyle) should be considered irrespective of the CV risk score (IIa,C), with LDL-C as the primary target and a goal of <1.8 mmol/l (I,A). **Teaching point:** Persistent Cushing syndrome: consider a statin regardless of risk score, with LDL-C <1.8 mmol/l.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 15; Section 3.2.

### Q33
**Topic:** Secondary causes of dyslipidaemia

A 36-year-old bodybuilder has an HDL-C of 0.4 mmol/l, previously 1.1 mmol/l. He takes no prescribed medicines but uses injectable anabolic steroids.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Anabolic steroids can cut HDL-C and Lp(a) by nearly half
B. Anabolic steroids lower LDL-C only
C. Drugs never alter lipid levels
D. Anabolic steroids raise HDL-C

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The effects of drugs on lipid levels are generally small and insignificant, except for anabolic steroids, which can cause an almost 50% reduction in HDL-C and Lp(a). Exogenous steroids may also cause secondary dyslipidaemia. Treating the underlying cause improves the lipid profile (Key Recommendation 2). Other secondary causes include nephrosis, cholestatic liver disease, hypothyroidism, alcohol (TG >4.5 mmol/l), and insulin resistance with high TG and low HDL-C.

**Teaching point:** Anabolic steroids can halve HDL-C. Look for secondary causes first.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2; Key Recommendation 2.

### Q34
**Topic:** Targets and LDL-C achieved

A 60-year-old woman with established CVD has an LDL-C of 1.5 mmol/l on rosuvastatin 20 mg. She asks whether her plaque will regress and whether to lower the dose because she is "already below 1.8".

**Which statement is the most accurate?**

A. Percentage fall does not matter, only the absolute value
B. Lower is better; <1.6 regresses plaque; intensify to <1.4
C. Regression occurs only above 2.6
D. Below 1.8 is the lowest useful level; reduce the dose

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The lower the LDL-C achieved, the greater the CV benefit. Below 1.8 mmol/l there is less plaque progression, and below 1.6 mmol/l regression has been documented. Both the absolute LDL-C and the percentage reduction matter. Her secondary-prevention target is <1.4 mmol/l with a 50% reduction, so she is still above target and treatment should be intensified (for example by adding ezetimibe), not reduced. Benefit of LDL-C lowering has been documented even at baseline levels below 1 mmol/l.

**Teaching point:** Lower is better: <1.8 slows plaque progression, <1.6 regresses it. Secondary prevention target is <1.4 mmol/l.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 3; Section 7.2.1.
