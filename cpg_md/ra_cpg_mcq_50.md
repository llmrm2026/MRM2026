# Management of Rheumatoid Arthritis (CPG, MaHTAS): 50 MCQs

**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Rheumatoid Arthritis* (MaHTAS), including its tables and appendices.
**Format:** 50 single-best-answer questions, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style. At least 20 have long multilayer stems that need clinical reasoning. Several stems reproduce CPG tables as text, with the table or appendix cited. Every wrong answer is a near-miss.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from the CPG text. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

## Section 1: Diagnosis, Investigation and Classification

### Q1
**Topic:** ACR/EULAR 2010 score

A 46-year-old woman has had painful, swollen metacarpophalangeal joints and wrists for 10 weeks, with morning stiffness of 90 minutes. Examination shows definite synovitis in 6 small joints (MCP2, MCP3 and wrists, both sides). No large joint is involved. RF is 4 times the upper limit of normal. Anti-CCP is negative. CRP is raised and ESR is normal.

Adapted from CPG Table 2 (2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria):

| Domain | Category | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Joint involvement | 1 large joint | 0 |
| | 2-10 large joints | 1 |
| | 1-3 small joints | 2 |
| | 4-10 small joints | 3 |
| | >10 joints (≥1 small) | 5 |
| Serology | Negative RF and ACPA | 0 |
| | Low-positive RF or ACPA | 2 |
| | High-positive RF or ACPA | 3 |
| Acute-phase reactants | Normal CRP and ESR | 0 |
| | Abnormal CRP or ESR | 1 |
| Duration | <6 weeks / ≥6 weeks | 0 / 1 |

**What is her score and classification?**

A. 6/10; classified as RA
B. 7/10; classified as RA
C. 8/10; classified as RA
D. 5/10; does not yet fulfil criteria

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Joints: 6 small joints scores 3. Serology: RF above 3 times the upper limit of normal is high-positive, so 3 (the negative anti-CCP does not reduce it, as only one result is needed). Acute-phase reactants: abnormal CRP gives 1 (one abnormal test is enough). Duration of 10 weeks gives 1. Total 3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 8. A score of 6 or more is definite RA. The score of 6 and 7 come from missing the RF category or the acute-phase point.

**Teaching point:** ACR/EULAR 2010: add joints (0-5), serology (0-3), acute-phase reactants (0-1) and duration (0-1). A total of 6 or more means RA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4, Table 2.

### Q2
**Topic:** Joints that do not count

A 58-year-old man has had pain and swelling in 3 joints for 8 months. Examination shows definite swelling of the right second DIP joint, the left first carpometacarpal joint and the right first MTP joint. He has a negative RF and ACPA, with a normal CRP and ESR. His wife has RA.

**How should these joints be handled when applying the classification criteria?**

A. Count each as a small joint (score 2)
B. Count all three as large joints (score 1)
C. Count the DIP as small and the other two as large
D. DIP, first CMC and first MTP joints are excluded from the joint count

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The ACR/EULAR criteria exclude DIP joints, first carpometacarpal joints and first MTP joints from the joint assessment. Small joints are the MCPs, PIPs, second to fifth MTPs, thumb IPs and wrists. With no countable joints and negative serology and acute-phase tests, he scores only 1 for duration, and these distal and first-ray joints are typical of osteoarthritis (including erosive inflammatory OA) or psoriatic or gouty disease rather than RA.

**Teaching point:** DIP, first CMC and first MTP joints do not count towards RA classification. Think OA, psoriatic arthritis or gout.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4 (Joint involvement); Section 2 (differentials).

### Q3
**Topic:** Seronegative disease

A 39-year-old woman has 4 months of symmetrical swelling of MCPs and PIPs with morning stiffness of 2 hours. Her RF is negative, her CRP is raised, and her GP says "it can't be RA".

**What is the most accurate response?**

A. RA is excluded; consider fibromyalgia
B. A negative RF rules out RA with 95% specificity
C. 30-40% of patients with RA are seronegative, so check ACPA
D. RF and ACPA must both be positive to make the diagnosis

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Negative RF does not exclude RA, as 30-40% of patients are seronegative. ACPA should be considered when RF is negative in clinically suspected RA. ACPA has higher specificity (95-98%) than RF (79-85%), with sensitivities of 79% and 67% respectively. The presence of both indicates more severe disease. Neither is used for disease monitoring.

**Teaching point:** Negative RF does not exclude RA. Test ACPA too, since it is more specific.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1 (Laboratory tests).

### Q4
**Topic:** Interpreting a positive RF

A 79-year-old woman with generalised aches and a recent viral infection has a positive RF (low titre). She has no joint swelling, a normal examination and a normal ESR and CRP. Her daughter is worried that she has RA.

**What is the most appropriate interpretation?**

A. RA is confirmed
B. RF is present in the normal population, more often in the elderly, and does not equate to RA
C. RA is confirmed if ACPA is also checked
D. Start methotrexate, as RF positivity predicts erosions

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A positive RF does not equate to RA because it occurs in the normal population, with a higher incidence in the elderly. RA requires clinical synovitis (at least one swollen joint for the classification criteria) and compatible features. Without synovitis she does not meet the target population for classification, and starting a DMARD for an isolated serological result is wrong.

**Teaching point:** RF positivity alone is not RA, especially in the elderly. The diagnosis needs synovitis.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1.

### Q5
**Topic:** When to refer (long clinical stem)

A 35-year-old woman presents to her GP with 7 weeks of finger and wrist pain. She has no clearly swollen joints, but the MCP squeeze test is positive on both hands and morning stiffness lasts 50 minutes. CRP is 22 mg/l. RF is negative. She asks whether to wait for "something to show up on the X-ray".

**What is the most appropriate step?**

A. Repeat a hand X-ray in 6 months and then decide
B. Treat with NSAIDs for 3 months before any referral
C. Refer to a rheumatologist now
D. Start methotrexate in primary care

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** All patients suspected of having RA should be referred to a rheumatologist. Referral criteria include clinical suspicion with any of: more than 3 swollen joints, MCP/MTP involvement with a positive squeeze test, or morning stiffness over 30 minutes. She meets two of these. Plain hand radiographs may be normal in the first 6 months, so waiting for X-ray changes delays treatment. All RA patients should be primarily managed by rheumatologists.

**Teaching point:** Refer suspected RA early: more than 3 swollen joints, positive squeeze test, or stiffness over 30 minutes. A normal X-ray does not exclude early RA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6 (Referral); Recommendation 3.

### Q6
**Topic:** Radiographic findings

A 52-year-old man has had RA for 3 years. His hand X-ray is reviewed against the CPG's Figures 1 and 2 (early RA: periarticular osteopenia and soft-tissue swelling; advanced RA: ulnar deviation, hitchhiker's thumb, boutonniere deformity, subchondral cysts, sclerosis, joint space narrowing).

**Which radiographic pattern is characteristic of RA?**

A. Asymmetric changes with prominent DIP involvement and osteophytes
B. Asymmetrical sacroiliac fusion
C. Central erosions of DIP joints with a "gull-wing" appearance
D. Symmetrical juxta-articular demineralisation, erosions and joint space narrowing sparing the DIP joints

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** RA changes are symmetrical and spare the distal interphalangeal joints: soft-tissue swelling, juxta-articular demineralisation, joint space narrowing and bone erosions. Radiographs can be normal in the first 6 months. DIP-predominant, asymmetric osteophyte disease suggests osteoarthritis, and gull-wing DIP erosions point to erosive inflammatory OA, both listed as differentials.

**Teaching point:** RA on X-ray: symmetrical, periarticular osteopenia, erosions, narrowing, DIP joints spared.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2.1, Figures 1 and 2.

### Q7
**Topic:** Ultrasound in ACPA-negative disease

A 44-year-old man has had polyarthralgia for 3 months with equivocal joint swelling on examination. ACPA is negative and his ACR/EULAR score is 5. The rheumatologist arranges musculoskeletal ultrasound.

**What is the expected benefit of adding ultrasound-detected synovitis to the 2010 criteria in ACPA-negative patients?**

A. It lowers specificity to improve sensitivity
B. Sensitivity rises from about 55% to 72% and specificity from about 79% to 88%
C. It is only useful once erosions are present
D. It has no benefit in ACPA-negative patients

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In ACPA-negative patients, adding ultrasound-detected synovitis counts to the 2010 criteria increased diagnostic sensitivity from 55.2% to 72.4% and specificity from 78.5% to 87.7%. Ultrasound is more accurate than clinical assessment in early RA, especially in ACPA-negative patients, and detects subclinical synovitis. MRI of hands and wrists can also detect early synovitis but is limited by cost and availability.

**Teaching point:** In ACPA-negative suspected RA, ultrasound synovitis improves both sensitivity and specificity.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.2.2.

### Q8
**Topic:** Poor prognostic factors

A 50-year-old woman with newly diagnosed RA is being counselled. She smokes, has a BMI of 33 kg/m2, is positive for RF and ACPA, and has a CRP of 40 mg/l. Her haemoglobin is 98 g/l, and hand X-rays show erosions at baseline.

**Which factor in her profile is NOT listed as a poor prognostic factor in the CPG?**

A. Anaemia
B. High baseline erosion score
C. Obesity
D. Early morning stiffness exceeding 30 minutes

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The CPG's poor prognostic factors include older age, female sex, obesity, smoking, ACPA, RF, high CRP, high ESR, anaemia and a high baseline erosion score. Prolonged morning stiffness is a feature of inflammation used to suspect RA and for referral, not a listed prognostic factor. She has multiple poor prognostic factors, which supports early use of more intensive therapy if the target is not met.

**Teaching point:** Poor prognosis: ACPA/RF, smoking, obesity, high CRP/ESR, anaemia and baseline erosions.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.

### Q9
**Topic:** Pre-biologic screening (long clinical stem)

A 55-year-old man with RA has persistent moderate disease activity despite methotrexate and sulfasalazine for 8 months. His rheumatologist plans a TNF inhibitor. HBsAg is negative. He lived with a relative who had tuberculosis 20 years ago. Chest X-ray is normal. He has no cough, fever or weight loss.

**Which set of pre-biologic tests is the most appropriate?**

A. IgG, IgA and IgM levels, and Mantoux only
B. HBsAg only, as he is already negative
C. Chest X-ray only, as it is normal
D. Hepatitis B core antibody, Mantoux ± IGRA, and HIV screening

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Pre-biologic investigations are hepatitis B core antibody if HBsAg is negative, Mantoux ± IGRA, and HIV screening, with immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM) before rituximab. Chest X-ray is mandatory at baseline. A normal chest X-ray does not exclude latent TB, and anti-TNF therapy particularly increases TB reactivation risk, so a TB contact needs a Mantoux or IGRA. All patients must be screened for TB, hepatitis B and C and HIV before biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs.

**Teaching point:** Before biologics: TB (Mantoux ± IGRA plus CXR), HBsAg and anti-HBc, hepatitis C, HIV, and immunoglobulins before rituximab.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1, Table 1; Recommendation 9; Appendix 8.

### Q10
**Topic:** Latent TB before biologics

A 61-year-old woman with RA is being screened before adalimumab. Her chest X-ray is normal. Her Mantoux test shows 7 mm induration. She has no symptoms of TB and has never received TB treatment.

Adapted from CPG Appendix 8 (TB work-up before biologics):

| Result | Action |
|---|---|
| History, examination or CXR suggestive of TB | Investigate to confirm TB |
| Mantoux <5 mm or IGRA negative | No action |
| Mantoux ≥5 mm or IGRA positive, no previous TB treatment | Treat as latent TB infection |

**What is the most appropriate action?**

A. Proceed with adalimumab; Mantoux is not significant
B. Refer for anti-TB treatment of active disease
C. Treat as latent TB infection
D. Repeat Mantoux in 2 years

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** A Mantoux of 5 mm or more (or a positive IGRA) in an asymptomatic patient with a normal chest X-ray and no previous TB treatment is treated as latent TB infection before starting the biologic. The 10-mm threshold used in the general population does not apply here, because patients about to start immunosuppression use a threshold of 5 mm. She does not have active TB.

**Teaching point:** Before biologics, Mantoux ≥5 mm or positive IGRA means treat as LTBI.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 8.

### Q11
**Topic:** Disease activity: CDAI

A 48-year-old woman with RA on methotrexate has 6 tender and 4 swollen joints (28-joint count). Her global assessment of disease activity is 4 cm and the physician's global assessment is 3.5 cm (both 0-10 cm VAS).

Adapted from CPG Appendix 3 (CDAI):

| CDAI | Disease activity |
|---|---|
| ≤2.8 | Remission |
| >2.8 to ≤10 | Low |
| >10 to ≤22 | Moderate |
| >22 | High |

**What is her disease activity, and what does the treat-to-target strategy require?**

A. Low; continue treatment unchanged
B. Remission; stop methotrexate
C. High; start a biologic immediately
D. Moderate; adjust treatment, as the target is remission or low disease activity

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** CDAI = tender joints + swollen joints + patient global + physician global = 6 + 4 + 4 + 3.5 = 17.5, which is moderate activity (above 10 up to 22). The target is clinical remission or at least low disease activity within 6 months, with regular treatment adjustment. At 17.5 the target is not met, so therapy should be intensified (for example adding a second csDMARD, or considering a biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD if csDMARDs fail).

**Teaching point:** CDAI = TJC + SJC + patient global + physician global. Remission ≤2.8, low ≤10, moderate ≤22, high >22.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7; Appendix 3; Recommendation 4.

### Q12
**Topic:** Disease activity: DAS28

A 57-year-old man with RA has a DAS28-ESR of 3.0 after 6 months of methotrexate and sulfasalazine. He feels well and asks whether his treatment can be stopped.

**What does the DAS28 indicate, and what should be done?**

A. High activity; escalate to a biologic
B. Moderate activity; add prednisolone 30 mg
C. Low disease activity, which meets the minimum treat-to-target goal; continue and monitor
D. Remission; stop all DMARDs

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** DAS28-ESR thresholds are: remission ≤2.6, low >2.6 to 3.2, moderate >3.2 to 5.1, high >5.1. A score of 3.0 is low disease activity, which meets the target of remission or at least low disease activity within 6 months. Treatment continues with monitoring. Stopping DMARDs would risk a flare, and no escalation is needed.

**Teaching point:** DAS28-ESR: remission ≤2.6, low ≤3.2, moderate ≤5.1, high >5.1.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 3; Recommendation 4.

## Section 2: Non-pharmacological Care and Symptom Control

### Q13
**Topic:** Role of NSAIDs

A 62-year-old man with RA, hypertension and CKD stage 3 takes methotrexate. He asks whether regular naproxen could replace his DMARD because it "controls the pain better".

**What is the most accurate statement?**

A. NSAIDs slow joint damage and can replace DMARDs
B. NSAIDs relieve pain and inflammation but do not modify disease, and he has renal and cardiovascular risks
C. NSAIDs are safe in CKD and hypertension at standard doses
D. COX-2 inhibitors have no renal or cardiovascular effects

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** NSAIDs reduce pain and improve function but have no disease-modifying property, so they are an adjunct to DMARDs. Their gastrointestinal, renal and cardiovascular adverse effects limit use, particularly with comorbidities, so they should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Etoricoxib caused more oedema and hypertension than diclofenac. No studies guide the best analgesic choice in RA with CV and renal comorbidities.

**Teaching point:** NSAIDs help symptoms, not disease course. Use them judiciously, especially with CKD, hypertension or CVD.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1; Recommendation 6.

### Q14
**Topic:** Choice of NSAID with GI risk

A 66-year-old woman with RA and a past duodenal ulcer needs NSAID therapy for pain in addition to her DMARDs. She has normal renal function and controlled hypertension.

**Which statement best fits the CPG evidence?**

A. Naproxen is safer for the GI tract than celecoxib
B. Etoricoxib has more GI toxicity than diclofenac
C. Celecoxib 200 mg daily had GI ulcer rates similar to placebo and was safer than traditional NSAIDs
D. Topical ketoprofen has no evidence of benefit

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Celecoxib 200 mg daily is as safe as placebo for gastroduodenal ulcers of 3 mm or more and safer than traditional NSAIDs (NNH 9), with no conclusive evidence of more CV events than traditional NSAIDs. Etoricoxib 90 mg had better GI tolerability than diclofenac but more renovascular events (oedema, hypertension). A ketoprofen patch was more effective than placebo for local pain, with contact dermatitis as the commonest adverse event.

**Teaching point:** Celecoxib and etoricoxib are more GI-friendly than traditional NSAIDs. Etoricoxib increases oedema and hypertension.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.1.

### Q15
**Topic:** Corticosteroid bridging (long clinical stem)

A 49-year-old woman is diagnosed with active RA (CDAI 28). Methotrexate is started. She is in severe pain and her GP asks about steroids. She has osteopenia on a DXA scan 2 years ago and mild hypertension.

**Which approach best fits the CPG?**

A. Prednisolone 40 mg for 6 months
B. Prednisolone ≤10 mg daily for under 3 months as bridging, with calcium and vitamin D
C. Dexamethasone 4 mg daily long term
D. No steroid, since steroids are contraindicated with osteopenia

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Short-term (under 3 months) low-dose corticosteroids (oral prednisolone ≤10 mg once daily) may be used in active RA, as bridging while csDMARDs take up to 8 weeks to act. Prednisolone is preferred to dexamethasone or betamethasone because it suppresses the HPA axis less. Patients on corticosteroids need calcium and vitamin D and surveillance for infection. Long-term use predisposes to osteoporosis and infection, and cohort data link corticosteroids to MI and stroke.

**Teaching point:** Steroids in RA: prednisolone ≤10 mg for under 3 months as a bridge, with calcium and vitamin D.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.2; Recommendation 7.

### Q16
**Topic:** Non-drug measures

A 54-year-old woman with active RA of the hands and feet asks about non-drug help. She has painful foot deformities, difficulty opening jars and has been advised to try a special diet.

**Which pair of measures is supported by the CPG?**

A. Immobilisation of the wrist in a splint for 6 months
B. An elimination diet to reduce disease activity, and complete rest of the hands
C. Herbal supplements to replace DMARDs, and a high-dose fish oil diet
D. Joint protection with hand exercises, and custom orthoses under podiatry supervision

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Joint protection advice with hand strengthening and mobilisation exercises improves upper-limb function and reduces morning stiffness and pain. Custom rigid foot orthoses under podiatry supervision were more effective than usual care for foot pain. There is no strong evidence that dietary interventions reduce disease activity, and there is insufficient evidence for traditional or complementary medicines. TENS can reduce pain, and low-impact aerobic activity is preferred in active disease. Prolonged immobilisation causes contracture and atrophy.

**Teaching point:** Support: education, OT joint protection, TENS, low-impact exercise, podiatry orthoses. Not supported: special diets and herbal remedies.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.1; Section 8; Recommendation 5.

## Section 3: DMARDs

### Q17
**Topic:** First-line DMARD

A 40-year-old man with newly diagnosed seropositive RA has no renal or liver disease, a normal chest X-ray, and negative viral hepatitis serology. He is not planning a family and drinks little alcohol.

**What is the most appropriate first-line DMARD?**

A. Leflunomide
B. Sulfasalazine
C. Methotrexate
D. Hydroxychloroquine

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Methotrexate is the mainstay and the recommended first-line DMARD for all RA patients unless contraindicated. In MTX-naive patients, MTX monotherapy was as effective as MTX combined with other non-biologic DMARDs. SSZ, HCQ and LEF are alternatives or add-ons. Folic acid (at least 5 mg/week) is given to reduce adverse effects, and hepatitis B and C screening is done before starting.

**Teaching point:** MTX is first-line in RA unless contraindicated. Give folic acid ≥5 mg/week.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.3; Recommendation 8.

### Q18
**Topic:** Methotrexate dose adjustment for renal function

A 63-year-old woman with RA takes methotrexate 15 mg weekly. Her creatinine clearance has fallen to 45 ml/min/1.73 m2 after an episode of dehydration.

Adapted from CPG Appendix 6 (methotrexate renal dose adjustment):

| CrCl (ml/min/1.73 m2) | % of standard dose |
|---|---|
| ≥60 | Full dose |
| 30-59 | 50 |
| <30 | Contraindicated |

**What is the most appropriate dose?**

A. Continue 15 mg weekly
B. Stop; contraindicated
C. 5 mg weekly
D. About 7.5 mg weekly

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** At CrCl 30-59 the dose should be reduced to 50% of the standard dose, so 15 mg becomes about 7.5 mg weekly. Below 30 methotrexate is contraindicated. Continuing the full dose risks toxicity (cytopenias, mucositis). 5 mg is a 67% cut, which is more than the CPG advises. Renal function should be rechecked with her FBC and liver tests.

**Teaching point:** MTX and renal function: full dose if CrCl ≥60, 50% if 30-59, contraindicated below 30.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 6.

### Q19
**Topic:** Methotrexate monitoring thresholds (long clinical stem)

A 58-year-old man on methotrexate 20 mg weekly and folic acid has routine blood tests at 6 months:

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| WBC | 3.2 x 10^9/L |
| Neutrophils | 1.4 x 10^3/L |
| Platelets | 168 x 10^9/L |
| MCV | 98 fL |
| ALT | 1.5 x ULN |
| Creatinine | unchanged from baseline |

Adapted from CPG Appendix 7 (consider interrupting treatment if WBC <3.5, neutrophils <1.6, eosinophils >0.5, MCV >105 fL, platelets <140, creatinine up >30%, ALT/AST >3 x ULN, albumin <30 g/L).

**What is the most appropriate action?**

A. Continue; all results acceptable
B. Increase folic acid and recheck in 6 months
C. Early consultation with the rheumatology team and consider interrupting treatment
D. Stop folic acid

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** His WBC (3.2) is below 3.5 and neutrophils (1.4) are below 1.6, so both meet the thresholds at which the CPG advises early rheumatology consultation and consideration of interrupting treatment. Platelets, MCV, ALT and creatinine do not meet thresholds. MTX monitoring is 2-4 weekly for the first 3 months or after each dose increase, then 3-monthly.

**Teaching point:** MTX: consider stopping for WBC <3.5, neutrophils <1.6, platelets <140, MCV >105, creatinine rise >30%, ALT >3 x ULN or albumin <30.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 7.

### Q20
**Topic:** Planning pregnancy on methotrexate

A 31-year-old woman with well-controlled RA on methotrexate 15 mg weekly and folic acid wishes to conceive within 4 months. She is not currently using contraception reliably.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Continue methotrexate throughout pregnancy
B. Stop methotrexate at least 3 months before conception and review treatment options with her rheumatologist
C. Stop methotrexate on the day of a positive pregnancy test
D. Continue methotrexate but halve the dose

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and women should stop it for at least 3 months before conception. Leflunomide also needs a washout before conception, and tofacitinib should be stopped 2 months before. Compatible options include hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine (with folate), and the anti-TNF agents (infliximab and adalimumab up to about week 20, etanercept up to week 30-32). Referral for pre-pregnancy care is indicated.

**Teaching point:** Stop MTX at least 3 months before conception. HCQ, SSZ and TNF inhibitors are options.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.3; Section 10.2; Appendix 6.

### Q21
**Topic:** Combination versus monotherapy

Two patients with RA are reviewed. Patient P is newly diagnosed and has never taken a DMARD. Patient Q has had active disease despite methotrexate 20 mg weekly for 6 months.

**Which statement best matches the CPG evidence?**

A. For P, methotrexate monotherapy is as effective as combination; for Q, adding another csDMARD to methotrexate is more effective than methotrexate alone
B. For P, combination is far superior; for Q, switching to monotherapy with a different drug is best
C. For both, monotherapy is always superior
D. For Q, combination therapy is no better than methotrexate alone

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In MTX-naive patients, MTX monotherapy was as effective as MTX combined with other non-biologic DMARDs (ACR50 RR 1.76, not significant). In patients with an inadequate response to MTX, combination of MTX with other non-biologic DMARDs was more effective than MTX alone (ACR50 RR 4.54). Combinations cause more GI adverse events. The CPG supports bDMARDs or tsDMARDs when the target is not met with csDMARDs.

**Teaching point:** Start with MTX alone. If MTX fails, add another csDMARD or step up to a biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.3.

### Q22
**Topic:** Hydroxychloroquine safety (long clinical stem)

A 45-year-old woman weighing 78 kg (ideal body weight 55 kg) is prescribed hydroxychloroquine for RA. She has no visual symptoms. She asks about the maximum dose and eye checks.

**Which advice matches the CPG?**

A. 200 mg daily, with eye review only if symptoms develop
B. Dose by actual body weight (about 500 mg/day), with an annual eye exam from the start
C. Any dose, as ocular toxicity does not occur in RA
D. Dose up to 6.5 mg/kg of ideal body weight (about 350 mg/day), with a baseline eye exam within 1 year and annual review after 5 years

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The maximum HCQ dose is 6.5 mg/kg of ideal body weight, which is 6.5 × 55 = about 357 mg/day (within the 200-400 mg range). The CPG advises a baseline eye examination (within 1 year of starting) and annual review after 5 years of treatment. Dosing by actual weight overshoots, and waiting for visual symptoms risks irreversible retinopathy. HCQ is compatible with pregnancy and lactation.

**Teaching point:** HCQ: ≤6.5 mg/kg ideal body weight, baseline eye exam, annual screening after 5 years.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.3; Appendices 6 and 7.

### Q23
**Topic:** Sulfasalazine and G6PD

A 36-year-old man of Malay descent with RA has been prescribed sulfasalazine because methotrexate is contraindicated. His G6PD status has not been tested. His partner is pregnant.

**Which statement is most accurate?**

A. Sulfasalazine is the only DMARD that causes oligospermia
B. Sulfasalazine is contraindicated in pregnancy
C. Sulfasalazine is compatible with pregnancy with folate supplementation, and caution is needed in G6PD deficiency
D. G6PD deficiency is irrelevant to sulfasalazine

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Sulfasalazine is compatible with pregnancy when folate is supplemented, and breastfeeding is safe with a healthy full-term infant. Caution applies in premature infants, hyperbilirubinaemia and G6PD deficiency. It can cause oligospermia in men, which matters if he is trying to father a child (not here, since his partner is already pregnant). The most frequent withdrawal reasons are gastrointestinal and mucocutaneous.

**Teaching point:** SSZ: compatible with pregnancy with folate. Oligospermia in men. Caution with G6PD deficiency.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.3; Appendix 6.

### Q24
**Topic:** Leflunomide

A 44-year-old woman is on leflunomide 20 mg daily for RA after methotrexate intolerance. She develops hypertension (BP 158/96 mmHg) and mild hair thinning, and now wishes to start a family.

**Which statement is correct?**

A. Leflunomide may be continued during pregnancy
B. Hypertension is unrelated to leflunomide
C. Leflunomide is the safest DMARD in pregnancy
D. Both hypertension and alopecia are recognised adverse effects, and a washout is needed before conception

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Leflunomide causes alopecia, raised liver enzymes, abnormal FBC and elevated blood pressure, and BP and weight are recorded at each visit. It should be avoided in pregnancy and lactation, and a washout procedure should be completed before conception. Compared with methotrexate, patients on LEF had more pruritus, hypertension, diarrhoea and alopecia, but less mouth ulceration and less ALT elevation.
