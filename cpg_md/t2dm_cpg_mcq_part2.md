# Management of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (6th Edition, 2020): MCQs, Part 2 (Hard)

**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia / Academy of Medicine Malaysia, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus*, 6th Edition (2020).
**Format:** Single-best-answer, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style. Every option is a near-miss, so none can be eliminated without knowing the CPG detail. These add to the 16 existing Type 2 DM questions.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from the CPG text. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

### Q1
**Topic:** DKA: potassium before insulin

A 46-year-old man weighing 80 kg presents with vomiting and abdominal pain after 3 days of a febrile illness. Capillary glucose is 26 mmol/l, ketones 5.1 mmol/l, venous pH 7.12, bicarbonate 9 mmol/l and potassium 3.2 mmol/l. SBP is 104 mmHg. One litre of 0.9% saline is running.

**What is the most appropriate insulin management at this point?**

A. FRIII 8 U/h now
B. 8 U IV bolus, then FRIII
C. FRIII 4 U/h until potassium normalises
D. Delay insulin; replace potassium first

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** All three DKA criteria are met. The CPG FRIII rate is 0.1 U/kg/h (8 U/h here), but insulin should be delayed if the initial potassium is below 3.5 mmol/l until it is corrected, because insulin drives potassium into cells. A half-rate infusion still lowers potassium. A bolus is not part of the CPG regimen.

**Teaching point:** In DKA, if K+ is below 3.5 mmol/l, replace potassium before starting the 0.1 U/kg/h FRIII.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2 (DKA: insulin and potassium).

### Q2
**Topic:** DKA: glucose falling, ketones persisting

A 52-year-old woman weighing 60 kg is 6 hours into DKA treatment on FRIII at 6 U/h with 0.9% saline and KCl. Capillary glucose is now 12.5 mmol/l, blood ketones 2.8 mmol/l, venous pH 7.24 and potassium 4.4 mmol/l.

**What is the most appropriate adjustment?**

A. Keep the FRIII rate and add 10% dextrose
B. Stop the FRIII and give basal insulin
C. Halve the FRIII and add 5% dextrose
D. Halve the FRIII and add 10% dextrose

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Once glucose falls below 14 mmol/l, the usual step is to add 5% dextrose and halve the insulin rate. However, the CPG states that if ketonaemia persists, the insulin rate should be maintained and 10% dextrose used instead. Her ketones are still 2.8 mmol/l and she is acidotic, so the insulin is still needed to clear ketones. DKA has not resolved (pH >7.3 and ketones <0.6 mmol/l), so stopping the FRIII is premature.

**Teaching point:** In DKA, glucose below 14 mmol/l with persistent ketones means keep the insulin rate and switch to 10% dextrose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2 (Insulin; Resolution).

### Q3
**Topic:** HHS: rising sodium

A 74-year-old man with HHS weighs 70 kg. On admission: glucose 48 mmol/l, sodium 142 mmol/l, urea 22 mmol/l and calculated osmolality 332 mOsm/kg. Ketones are 0.8 mmol/l. After 2 hours of 0.9% saline (no insulin yet), glucose is 38 mmol/l, sodium 146 mmol/l and osmolality is falling by 5 mOsm/kg/h.

**What is the most appropriate fluid management?**

A. Switch to 0.45% saline
B. Switch to 5% dextrose
C. Switch to 0.18% saline with 4% dextrose
D. Continue 0.9% saline

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** As glucose falls, water moves out of cells and the measured sodium rises. The CPG states that an initial rise in sodium is expected and is not, on its own, an indication for hypotonic fluid. 0.45% saline is used if sodium is above 145 mmol/l *and* osmolality is not falling (by less than 3 mOsm/kg/h) despite adequate hydration. His osmolality is falling at 5 mOsm/kg/h, within the 3-8 target, so 0.9% saline should continue. Dextrose is added only once glucose is below 14 mmol/l.

**Teaching point:** In HHS, a rising sodium with appropriately falling osmolality is expected. Change to 0.45% saline only if osmolality stalls.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.4 (HHS: principles of treatment).

### Q4
**Topic:** HHS: insulin rate

A 79-year-old woman weighing 60 kg with HHS (glucose 41 mmol/l, osmolality 338 mOsm/kg, ketones 1.1 mmol/l, pH 7.36) has had 2 litres of 0.9% saline. Glucose has fallen to 33 mmol/l and is no longer falling with fluids alone. The team starts IV insulin.

**What is the most appropriate initial FRIII rate?**

A. 1 U/h
B. 3 U/h
C. 6 U/h
D. 9 U/h

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** HHS uses a lower insulin rate than DKA: FRIII 0.05 U/kg/h, which is 3 U/h for 60 kg. 6 U/h is the DKA rate (0.1 U/kg/h), and it risks a rapid fall in glucose and osmolality, with osmotic demyelination and cerebral oedema. The target fall in glucose is 4-6 mmol/l/h. Patients with HHS are more insulin sensitive.

**Teaching point:** FRIII is 0.05 U/kg/h in HHS and 0.1 U/kg/h in DKA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.4; Figure 4-3.

### Q5
**Topic:** Ketoacidosis with near-normal glucose

A 58-year-old woman on metformin and empagliflozin is admitted 2 days after a laparoscopic cholecystectomy with nausea and tachypnoea. Glucose is 9.8 mmol/l, blood ketones 4.6 mmol/l, pH 7.18, bicarbonate 11 mmol/l and anion gap 24. She is clinically dry.

**Which IV fluid should accompany the insulin infusion?**

A. 5% dextrose saline
B. Hartmann's solution
C. 0.9% saline alone
D. 0.45% saline

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Ketoacidosis with glucose below 11 mmol/l on an SGLT2 inhibitor after surgery is euglycaemic DKA. Treatment follows the standard DKA protocol, except that dehydration is corrected with 5% dextrose saline or 5% dextrose, so that insulin can continue without causing hypoglycaemia. 10% dextrose may be needed if glucose is low and she is not dehydrated. Saline alone would force the insulin to be cut back before the ketones clear. The SGLT2 inhibitor should be withheld until she has recovered and is eating.

**Teaching point:** Euglycaemic DKA: standard FRIII plus dextrose-containing fluid, and stop the SGLT2 inhibitor.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.3 (Euglycaemic ketoacidosis).

### Q6
**Topic:** Hypoglycaemia on acarbose

A 66-year-old man takes gliclazide MR 120 mg, metformin and acarbose 100 mg three times daily. Mid-afternoon at clinic he becomes sweaty and tremulous. Capillary glucose is 3.1 mmol/l. He is alert and able to swallow.

**What is the most appropriate immediate treatment?**

A. 150-200 ml orange juice
B. 3 teaspoons sugar in water
C. Regular soft drink 200 ml
D. Glucose (dextrose) tablets 15 g

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Acarbose inhibits α-glucosidase, so sucrose and complex carbohydrates are absorbed slowly. The CPG says people taking acarbose must treat hypoglycaemia with glucose (dextrose) tablets, or with milk or honey if tablets are unavailable. Juice, table sugar and soft drinks are the standard 15 g remedies for other patients, but their sucrose depends on the inhibited enzyme. Glucose should be rechecked at 15 minutes.

**Teaching point:** On acarbose, treat hypoglycaemia with pure glucose, not sucrose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1 (Treatment of hypoglycaemia).

### Q7
**Topic:** Classifying hypoglycaemia

A 70-year-old woman on basal insulin records a glucose of 2.8 mmol/l at 03:00 during a check prompted by night sweats. She woke, felt shaky, and treated herself with juice without help. Her next reading 15 minutes later was 5.2 mmol/l.

**How is this episode classified under the CPG?**

A. Level 1 hypoglycaemia
B. Level 2 hypoglycaemia
C. Level 3 hypoglycaemia
D. Pseudo-hypoglycaemia

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Level 1 is glucose <3.9 but ≥3.0 mmol/l. Level 2 is glucose <3.0 mmol/l, the threshold for neuroglycopenic symptoms, which needs immediate action. Level 3 is defined by altered mental or physical function needing another person's help, not by a glucose value. She treated herself, so the event is level 2 rather than level 3. Nocturnal hypoglycaemia on basal insulin should prompt checks at 2-5 am and a switch to an analogue if she is on human basal insulin.

**Teaching point:** Below 3.0 mmol/l is level 2. Level 3 is defined by needing assistance, whatever the glucose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1, Table 4-2.

### Q8
**Topic:** Basal insulin titrated, HbA1c still high

A 61-year-old man weighing 80 kg takes metformin, a DPP4 inhibitor and glargine 34 U at night, titrated over 4 months. Fasting glucose now runs at 5.5-6.5 mmol/l without hypoglycaemia. HbA1c is 8.6%. Post-dinner readings are 13-15 mmol/l. Adherence is good.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Increase glargine by 2 U every 3 days
B. Add prandial insulin 4 U before dinner
C. Switch glargine to glargine U300
D. Add a sulphonylurea

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Fasting glucose is at target, but HbA1c is high and post-dinner glucose is elevated. That pattern calls for intensification with prandial insulin (4 U per meal, or 10% of the basal dose) at the largest meal, or a switch to premixed insulin. Increasing the basal dose further risks nocturnal hypoglycaemia and "over-basalisation" (his dose is 0.43 U/kg). U300 changes the insulin profile but does not address post-meal spikes. A sulphonylurea is usually reduced or stopped when insulin is intensified.

**Teaching point:** Fasting glucose at target with a high HbA1c means adding prandial insulin, not more basal insulin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5; Figure 3-2.

### Q9
**Topic:** Very high insulin requirement

A 48-year-old woman with BMI 31 kg/m2 (90 kg) takes a basal-bolus regimen totalling 190 U/day. HbA1c remains 9.4%. She injects into the same area of her abdomen, which has a soft, rubbery swelling. There is no fever.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Review injection technique and sites
B. Switch to an insulin pump
C. Increase total dose by 10%
D. Add pioglitazone 45 mg

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Her total daily dose is 2.1 U/kg, above the 1.5-2.0 U/kg threshold that the CPG says should prompt a search for a secondary cause: non-adherence, incorrect dose, timing or injection technique, or occult infection. Lipohypertrophy from repeated injection into one site causes erratic absorption. Escalating the dose, moving to a pump, or adding pioglitazone (which adds fluid retention with insulin) are all premature.

**Teaching point:** If total insulin exceeds 1.5-2.0 U/kg/day, check adherence, technique and injection sites before escalating.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5 (General guidelines for long-term use of insulin).

### Q10
**Topic:** Glycaemic target in a complex older patient

An 81-year-old woman with T2DM has heart failure, CKD stage 3b, osteoarthritis limiting mobility and mild cognitive impairment (MoCA 21/30). She lives with her daughter. She has had two falls this year. HbA1c is 7.4% on gliclazide MR 60 mg and linagliptin.

**What is the most appropriate HbA1c goal?**

A. ≤6.5%
B. ≤7.0%
C. <8.0%
D. <8.5%

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** With several coexisting chronic illnesses and mild cognitive impairment, she falls in the CPG's "complex/intermediate" category, which has an HbA1c goal of <8.0% (fasting glucose 5-8.3 mmol/l). <8.5% is for "very complex/poor health" patients: long-term care, end-stage illness, or moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment. ≤7.0% and ≤6.5% are for healthier or newly diagnosed patients. Her sulphonylurea, falls and an HbA1c of 7.4% suggest that treatment could be de-intensified.

**Teaching point:** Older people: healthy ≤7.5%, complex <8.0%, very complex <8.5%. Base the category on frailty, not age.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.3, Table 6-2.

### Q11
**Topic:** SGLT2 inhibitor as eGFR declines

A 63-year-old man with T2DM and severely increased albuminuria (UACR 85 mg/mmol) was started on dapagliflozin 10 mg 2 years ago, when his eGFR was 41 ml/min/1.73 m2. His eGFR is now 27 ml/min/1.73 m2. He is not on dialysis and has no genital infections or volume depletion.

**What is the most appropriate action regarding dapagliflozin?**

A. Stop; eGFR now below 30
B. Continue dapagliflozin
C. Reduce to 5 mg daily
D. Switch to empagliflozin 10 mg

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The CPG says not to *initiate* an SGLT2 inhibitor at eGFR below 30, but it may be *continued* if already started, and can be used until dialysis begins for renoprotection, even though its glucose-lowering effect is reduced. Stopping it removes a proven slowing of DKD progression. A lower dose or a different drug in the same class adds nothing.

**Teaching point:** SGLT2 inhibitors: do not start below eGFR 30, but continue for renoprotection until dialysis.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.1 (SGLT2-i); Section 5.2.

### Q12
**Topic:** Creatinine rise after RAS blockade

A 59-year-old woman with T2DM, hypertension and moderately increased albuminuria starts perindopril 4 mg. Two weeks later her creatinine has risen from 90 to 110 µmol/l (22%) and her eGFR from 64 to 51 ml/min/1.73 m2 (20%). Potassium is 5.1 mmol/l. She is well and euvolaemic.

**What is the most appropriate action?**

A. Continue perindopril and recheck
B. Stop perindopril
C. Halve the perindopril dose
D. Switch to an ARB

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG advises reducing or stopping an ACE inhibitor or ARB within 2 months if creatinine stays 30% or more above baseline, eGFR falls by 25% or more, or potassium reaches 5.6 mmol/l or above. Her changes (22%, 20%, K+ 5.1) are all below these thresholds, so the haemodynamic dip is acceptable and the drug should be continued and titrated up. Switching to an ARB gives similar benefits and risks and is pointless.

**Teaching point:** After starting ACEi/ARB, act only if creatinine rises ≥30%, eGFR falls ≥25% or K+ is ≥5.6 mmol/l.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2 (ACE inhibitors/ARB).

### Q13
**Topic:** First abnormal albuminuria result

A 47-year-old man diagnosed with T2DM a year ago has a negative urine protein dipstick. An early morning spot UACR is 4.2 mg/mmol. He has no fever, urinary symptoms or heart failure. BP is 128/78 mmHg off treatment and eGFR is 96 ml/min/1.73 m2.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Start an ACE inhibitor now
B. Renal ultrasound
C. Repeat UACR twice within 3-6 months
D. 24-hour urine protein collection

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Albuminuria varies with hydration, exercise and infection. When microalbuminuria is detected, the CPG requires confirmation by repeating the test twice within 3-6 months, after excluding UTI and heart failure. DKD is diagnosed if 2 of 3 tests are positive. Starting treatment on a single result is premature. Ultrasound is for rapid decline or suspected obstruction. A 24-hour collection is less practical than UACR.

**Teaching point:** One abnormal UACR is not DKD. Confirm with 2 of 3 positive tests over 3-6 months.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2 (Assessment of albuminuria; Figure 5-1).

### Q14
**Topic:** When to refer DKD

A 66-year-old woman with T2DM and DKD is on maximum-dose losartan and empagliflozin. Over 12 months her eGFR has fallen from 52 to 44 ml/min/1.73 m2 and her uPCR is stable at 0.5 g/day. BP is 128/76 mmHg and HbA1c is 7.1%.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Refer to a nephrologist
B. Add an ACE inhibitor to losartan
C. Continue and recheck in a year
D. Stop empagliflozin

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG lists rapid decline as an indication for nephrology referral: eGFR loss of more than 5 ml/min/1.73 m2 in 1 year (she lost 8), or more than 10 in 5 years. Other indications are eGFR below 30 and heavy proteinuria (≥1 g/day) despite treatment. Combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB is not recommended because it causes hyperkalaemia and AKI without benefit. Empagliflozin is renoprotective and should continue.

**Teaching point:** Refer DKD if eGFR falls more than 5 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year, eGFR is below 30, or proteinuria is ≥1 g/day.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2 (Referral to nephrologist).

### Q15
**Topic:** Painful neuropathy in a faller

A 78-year-old woman with T2DM has burning pain in both feet that disturbs her sleep. Monofilament sensation is absent to the ankles. She has had two falls this year, lying-standing BP is 146/80 falling to 118/68 mmHg, and she lives alone.

**Which neuropathic pain drug is the least suitable for her?**

A. Pregabalin
B. Duloxetine
C. Gabapentin
D. Amitriptyline

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** All four drug classes are CPG options for painful DSPN. However, tricyclic antidepressants carry a higher risk of sedation and orthostatic hypotension and should be used with caution in older people at risk of falls. She already has a postural drop of 28 mmHg and has had falls. If response is poor, switching class or combining two drugs is preferred to escalating one.

**Teaching point:** Avoid tricyclics for painful neuropathy in fallers with postural hypotension.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.3 (Treatment of DSPN).

### Q16
**Topic:** Interpreting a high ABI

A 71-year-old man with T2DM for 20 years and CKD stage 4 has a non-healing ulcer on the right great toe. Foot pulses are difficult to feel. The ankle-brachial index is 1.42 on the right and 1.38 on the left.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Reassure; PAD excluded
B. Toe-brachial index
C. Repeat the ABI in 3 months
D. Treat as neuropathic ulcer only

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** An ABI above 1.3 may reflect medial arterial calcification, which is common in long-standing diabetes and CKD, so it does not exclude PAD. The CPG recommends another test, such as the toe-brachial index (diagnostic if below 0.70) or continuous wave Doppler. In a patient with a foot ulcer, clinical examination alone may not exclude PAD, so combined testing is advised. Falsely reassuring results lead to missed ischaemia.

**Teaching point:** ABI above 1.3 is non-diagnostic because of calcification. Use TBI or Doppler waveforms.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4C (Peripheral arterial disease), Table 5-7.

### Q17
**Topic:** Erectile dysfunction with angina

A 62-year-old man with T2DM and stable angina reports erectile dysfunction for a year. He takes metformin, atorvastatin, bisoprolol and isosorbide mononitrate 60 mg daily. Morning testosterone is normal.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. Sildenafil 50 mg as required
B. Tadalafil 5 mg daily
C. Testosterone replacement
D. Urology referral for non-PDE5 therapy

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** PDE5 inhibitors are first line for ED but are contraindicated with nitrates, unstable angina or poor exercise tolerance. A daily low-dose PDE5 inhibitor is still a PDE5 inhibitor. Testosterone helps only symptomatic hypogonadal men, and his level is normal. The CPG advises urology referral when PDE5 inhibitors are contraindicated, for options such as intracavernosal therapy, intraurethral alprostadil or vacuum devices.

**Teaching point:** Nitrates mean no PDE5 inhibitor at any dose. Refer to urology for other options.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.6.1 (Erectile dysfunction).

### Q18
**Topic:** Reduced vision with macular oedema

A 56-year-old woman with T2DM notices blurring of central vision in her right eye. Visual acuity is 6/18. Fundoscopy shows moderate non-proliferative retinopathy with hard exudates near the fovea, and optical coherence tomography confirms centre-involving macular oedema. There are no new vessels.

**What is the most appropriate treatment?**

A. Pan-retinal photocoagulation
B. Focal macular laser
C. Intravitreal anti-VEGF
D. Observation and glucose control

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For sight-threatening centre-involving diabetic macular oedema, intravitreal anti-VEGF is the treatment of choice and is superior to laser for visual gain. Pan-retinal laser is for high-risk proliferative (and some severe non-proliferative) retinopathy, which she does not have. Glucose control alone is insufficient for sight-threatening DMO.

**Teaching point:** Centre-involving DMO: anti-VEGF. Proliferative retinopathy: pan-retinal laser.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.1 (Retinopathy).

### Q19
**Topic:** Lipid add-on with retinopathy

A 60-year-old man with T2DM and moderate non-proliferative retinopathy is on atorvastatin 40 mg. LDL-C is 1.6 mmol/l, triglycerides 1.5 mmol/l and HDL-C 1.1 mmol/l. He has no ASCVD. The ophthalmologist asks whether any lipid drug could slow his retinopathy.

**Which addition is most appropriate?**

A. Ezetimibe
B. Omega-3 fatty acids 4 g
C. Nicotinic acid
D. Fenofibrate

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** In patients with established retinopathy, fenofibrate reduces progression of retinopathy whatever the baseline triglyceride or HDL-C level. Normal triglycerides are the trap. His LDL-C already meets the high-risk target, so ezetimibe adds nothing for the eye. Omega-3 and nicotinic acid are reserved for marked hypertriglyceridaemia.

**Teaching point:** Fenofibrate slows diabetic retinopathy even when triglycerides are normal.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.9.2 (Hyperlipidaemia: management).

### Q20
**Topic:** Fibrate choice with a meglitinide

A 54-year-old man takes metformin and repaglinide 2 mg with meals because he eats irregularly. Fasting triglycerides are 6.4 mmol/l despite improved diet and glucose control. Secondary causes have been excluded. A fibrate is planned.

**Which drug should not be used with his current regimen?**

A. Fenofibrate
B. Gemfibrozil
C. Omega-3 fatty acids
D. Atorvastatin

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Repaglinide combined with gemfibrozil is contraindicated because of a high risk of prolonged hypoglycaemia (gemfibrozil inhibits repaglinide metabolism). Fenofibrate is the safer fibrate for him. Fish oil 2-4 g/day is an option for TG above 5.7 mmol/l, and a statin may be combined. Meglitinides can be added to other OGLDs except sulphonylureas.

**Teaching point:** Never combine repaglinide with gemfibrozil, because of prolonged hypoglycaemia.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.1 (Meglitinides); Section 3.9.2.

### Q21
**Topic:** Add-on therapy with HFrEF

A 64-year-old man with T2DM has ischaemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 32%) and was admitted with decompensated heart failure 2 months ago. He takes metformin 1 g twice daily, sacubitril/valsartan, bisoprolol and spironolactone. HbA1c is 7.8% and eGFR is 58 ml/min/1.73 m2.

**Which glucose-lowering add-on is most appropriate?**

A. Saxagliptin
B. Pioglitazone
C. Dapagliflozin
D. Glimepiride

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** SGLT2 inhibitors reduce CV death and heart failure hospitalisation in HFrEF (DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced), with or without diabetes. The CPG says they should be considered even when HbA1c is at target. Saxagliptin increased heart failure admissions and should be avoided in pre-existing heart failure. TZDs are contraindicated in CCF. Glimepiride is CV-neutral and gives no heart failure benefit.

**Teaching point:** HFrEF plus T2DM: add an SGLT2 inhibitor. Avoid saxagliptin and TZDs.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4A (CVD management); Section 3.6.1.

### Q22
**Topic:** ASCVD with HbA1c at target

A 58-year-old woman with T2DM had a non-ST-elevation MI a year ago. She takes metformin 1 g twice daily, and her HbA1c is 6.6%. BMI is 33 kg/m2 and eGFR 85 ml/min/1.73 m2. She is keen to lose weight and asks whether any change to her diabetes treatment is needed.

**What is the most appropriate change?**

A. None; HbA1c at target
B. Add liraglutide
C. Add exenatide extended-release
D. Add sitagliptin

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** With established ASCVD, the CPG recommends adding an SGLT2 inhibitor or a GLP1-RA *with demonstrated CV benefit*, whatever the HbA1c achieved. Liraglutide reduced MACE in LEADER, and it also helps weight. Exenatide ER showed no significant MACE reduction in EXSCEL. Sitagliptin is CV-safe but not cardioprotective. An HbA1c at target is the trap.

**Teaching point:** In ASCVD, add a GLP1-RA or SGLT2 inhibitor with proven CV benefit, even if HbA1c is at target.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4A (Recommendation 5); Section 3.6.2.

### Q23
**Topic:** Family history and incretin choice

A 45-year-old woman with T2DM and BMI 36 kg/m2 has HbA1c 8.2% on metformin and gliclazide. Her brother had a total thyroidectomy for medullary thyroid carcinoma and was found to carry a RET mutation (MEN 2A). eGFR is 90 ml/min/1.73 m2.

**Which add-on is most appropriate?**

A. Semaglutide weekly
B. Dulaglutide weekly
C. Liraglutide daily
D. Empagliflozin

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** GLP1-RAs should not be used in patients with a personal or family history of MEN 2A/2B or medullary thyroid cancer. That excludes all three GLP1-RAs, however weight-effective. An SGLT2 inhibitor adds weight loss with a low risk of hypoglycaemia.

**Teaching point:** A family history of MTC or MEN2 rules out all GLP1-RAs.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.2 (Precautions for GLP1-RA).

### Q24
**Topic:** Starting a GLP1-RA on existing therapy

A 52-year-old man with T2DM takes metformin 1 g twice daily and sitagliptin 100 mg daily. HbA1c is 8.4% and BMI 34 kg/m2. Liraglutide 0.6 mg daily is to be started, titrating to 1.2-1.8 mg.

**What change should be made to his existing tablets?**

A. Stop metformin
B. Halve sitagliptin
C. Stop sitagliptin
D. No change needed

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** GLP1-RAs should not be combined with DPP4 inhibitors, as both act on the incretin pathway. A half dose of sitagliptin is still the same class. Metformin should continue as the base therapy. Keeping all three drugs is the error the question is testing.

**Teaching point:** Starting a GLP1-RA means stopping the DPP4 inhibitor.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.2 (Precautions for GLP1-RA).

### Q25
**Topic:** Missed weekly injection

A 60-year-old woman injects semaglutide 1 mg every Sunday. On Wednesday morning she realises she forgot Sunday's dose. Her next scheduled dose is in 4 days.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Inject now, then next Sunday
B. Skip it; resume next Sunday
C. Inject now; make Wednesday the new day
D. Inject 2 mg next Sunday

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** For semaglutide, if 5 or more days remain until the next dose, the missed dose is given as soon as possible. If fewer than 5 days remain, it is skipped and the next scheduled dose given. She has 4 days, so she should skip. The dulaglutide cut-off is 3 days, so under the dulaglutide rule she would have injected now, which is the near-miss. Doses are never doubled.

**Teaching point:** Missed weekly GLP1-RA: semaglutide, inject if ≥5 days to the next dose; dulaglutide, if ≥3 days.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.2, Table 3-16.

### Q26
**Topic:** Initial therapy by HbA1c band

A 49-year-old man is diagnosed with T2DM on routine screening. He has no symptoms and no weight loss. HbA1c is 9.2% and fasting glucose 11.2 mmol/l. Urine ketones are negative. eGFR is 92 ml/min/1.73 m2 and he has no CVD.

**According to the CPG treatment algorithm, what is the most appropriate initial pharmacological approach?**

A. Metformin monotherapy
B. Dual combination therapy
C. Triple combination therapy
D. Basal insulin with OGLD

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG algorithm for newly diagnosed T2DM uses HbA1c bands: <6.5% consider metformin; 6.5-7.4% mono or dual therapy; 7.5-8.4% dual; 8.5-10.0% (FPG 10.0-13.0) triple combination; >10.0% (FPG >13.0) or symptomatic, basal or premixed insulin with combination therapy. At 9.2% and asymptomatic, he is in the triple combination band.

He is below both floors and is symptomatic, so his BP treatment should be reduced. The ARB is renoprotective for his albuminuria and should be kept, so it is the non-RAS drugs that are reduced. Adding an MRA would lower BP further and raise potassium.

**Teaching point:** DKD BP target ≤130/80, but not below 120/70. Reduce non-RAS drugs first.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2 (BP targets); Section 3.9.1.

### Q54
**Topic:** Aspirin in a young adult with diabetes

A 46-year-old woman has had T2DM for 2 years. She has no hypertension, smoking, albuminuria or family history of premature CVD. LDL-C is 2.2 mmol/l on atorvastatin and HbA1c is 7.0%. She asks whether she should take "a baby aspirin like my father".

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Aspirin 100 mg daily
B. No aspirin
C. Clopidogrel 75 mg daily
D. Aspirin once HbA1c is above 8%

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In people at low risk (under 50 with no major ASCVD risk factor), the CPG does not recommend aspirin for primary prevention. Aspirin (100 mg) is considered only in those at *increased* CV risk, after discussing bleeding. Clopidogrel is for patients with ASCVD who are allergic to aspirin. HbA1c is not an aspirin threshold.

**Teaching point:** Primary prevention aspirin in diabetes: only for increased CV risk after shared discussion, not routinely.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4A (Antiplatelet therapy; Recommendation 4).

### Q55
**Topic:** Revascularisation strategy

A 62-year-old man with T2DM and stable angina has coronary angiography showing severe stenoses in the proximal LAD, circumflex and right coronary arteries. LVEF is 50%. His coronary anatomy is suitable for either PCI or surgery, and his predicted surgical mortality is low.

**What is the most appropriate revascularisation?**

A. Multivessel PCI with DES
B. CABG
C. Optimal medical therapy alone
D. Staged PCI, culprit vessel first

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In T2DM with multivessel or complex LAD disease, with anatomy suitable for both procedures and low surgical risk, CABG is superior to PCI for death, MI and stroke (Grade A). The CPG regards diabetes as a distinct entity in this decision. PCI options are the near-miss, as they are reasonable in people without diabetes. He has symptomatic three-vessel disease, so medical therapy alone is inferior.

**Teaching point:** Diabetes with multivessel CAD and suitable anatomy: CABG over PCI.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4A (Revascularisation; Recommendation 7).

### Q56
**Topic:** Proliferative retinopathy and STEMI

A 57-year-old woman with T2DM and treated proliferative diabetic retinopathy (laser 2 years ago) presents to a district hospital 2 hours after onset of an inferior STEMI. The nearest PCI centre is 4 hours away. She has no other contraindication to thrombolysis.

**What is the most appropriate reperfusion strategy?**

A. Transfer for primary PCI
B. Withhold lysis; anticoagulate
C. Give fibrinolysis
D. Half-dose fibrinolysis

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG states that diabetic retinopathy is not a contraindication to thrombolytic therapy. With a transfer time of 4 hours, primary PCI cannot be delivered in time, so fibrinolysis is indicated. Withholding or halving the dose because of retinopathy is a common misconception.

**Teaching point:** Diabetic retinopathy, even proliferative, does not contraindicate thrombolysis.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4A (Revascularisation).

### Q57
**Topic:** Cardiac screening in an asymptomatic patient

A 59-year-old man with T2DM for 8 years and hypertension is asymptomatic. He walks 5 km daily without chest pain or breathlessness. BP is 132/78 mmHg, LDL-C 1.7 mmol/l and HbA1c 7.0%. His employer's health plan offers a treadmill test.

**What cardiac investigation is most appropriate?**

A. Resting 12-lead ECG
B. Exercise treadmill test
C. CT coronary calcium score
D. No cardiac investigation

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Routine screening for CAD in asymptomatic people with diabetes is not recommended, because it does not improve outcomes when risk factors are treated to target. His are at target. However, a resting ECG *is* indicated in T2DM with hypertension, or when CVD is suspected. "No test at all" misses that recommendation. Stress testing and calcium scoring are routine screening.

**Teaching point:** Asymptomatic T2DM: no stress test, but a resting ECG if hypertensive.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4A (Recommendations: Screening for CVD).

### Q58
**Topic:** Oral health at diagnosis

A 44-year-old woman has just been diagnosed with T2DM (HbA1c 7.6%). She has no gum bleeding, loose teeth or dental pain, and last saw a dentist 4 years ago.

**What is the most appropriate dental advice?**

A. Refer only if gum symptoms develop
B. Refer if HbA1c stays above 8%
C. No referral; advise brushing only
D. Periodontal examination now

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** All newly diagnosed people with diabetes should be referred for a periodontal examination as part of their management, with annual periodontal review even if no periodontitis is found. Periodontal disease has a two-way relationship with glycaemia, and treating it improves metabolic parameters. Symptom-triggered or HbA1c-triggered referral misses silent disease.

**Teaching point:** Every new diagnosis needs a periodontal referral and then an annual review.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8 (Recommendations: Periodontal disease).

### Q59
**Topic:** Discordant HbA1c on intensive insulin

A 52-year-old man on basal-bolus insulin has an HbA1c of 8.7%, yet his logbook shows pre-meal readings of 6-8 mmol/l. He reports occasional morning headaches and vivid dreams. He tests 3 times a day before meals.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Continuous glucose monitoring
B. Increase basal insulin by 10%
C. Repeat HbA1c in 1 month
D. Add a GLP1-RA

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** CGM may be considered in patients on intensive insulin with suboptimal HbA1c or suspected unrecognised hypoglycaemia. The discordance between his HbA1c and pre-meal readings suggests undetected post-prandial peaks, and the headaches and vivid dreams suggest nocturnal hypoglycaemia. Increasing basal insulin blindly could worsen night-time lows. Repeating HbA1c, or adding a drug, does not explain the pattern.

**Teaching point:** HbA1c out of keeping with SMBG, plus nocturnal symptoms, on intensive insulin: use CGM.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.8 (Recommendations: Monitoring); Section 4.1 (Nocturnal hypoglycaemia).

### Q60
**Topic:** Converting HbA1c units

A 66-year-old woman returns from abroad with a laboratory report giving her HbA1c as 53 mmol/mol (IFCC). She asks whether she has met her clinic target of ≤7.0% (NGSP).

**What is her NGSP HbA1c?**

A. 6.0%
B. 6.5%
C. 7.0%
D. 7.5%

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** On the CPG conversion table, NGSP 7.0% corresponds to IFCC 53 mmol/mol (estimated average glucose 8.6 mmol/l). NGSP 6.0% is 42 mmol/mol and 8.0% is 64 mmol/mol. IFCC numbers are not percentages, so mistaking one unit system for the other is the trap. She is exactly at target.

**Teaching point:** HbA1c 7.0% = 53 mmol/mol = eAG 8.6 mmol/l. 6.0% = 42 and 8.0% = 64.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.8.1, Table 3-20.

### Q61
**Topic:** Metformin intolerance at high dose

A 57-year-old man takes metformin 1 g three times daily with meals. He has persistent loose stools and bloating, and HbA1c is 7.6%. eGFR is 82 ml/min/1.73 m2. He is reluctant to start an injection.

**What is the most appropriate change to his metformin?**

A. Stop metformin
B. Switch to metformin XR 2000 mg daily
C. Reduce to 500 mg three times daily
D. Take it 30 minutes before meals

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Metformin doses above 2000 mg/day give no further glycaemic benefit and significantly increase GI side effects. The extended-release formulation also reduces GI symptoms. Switching to XR 2000 mg once daily keeps the full effective dose with better tolerance. Halving the dose loses efficacy, and stopping metformin is unnecessary. Metformin should be taken with or after meals, not before.

**Teaching point:** Metformin above 2 g/day adds side effects without benefit. Switch to XR 2 g for intolerance.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.1 (Metformin, Table 3-8).

### Q62
**Topic:** New hypoglycaemia after a prescription

A 74-year-old woman on gliclazide 160 mg twice daily and metformin has been stable for years. She is admitted with a glucose of 2.2 mmol/l and confusion, 4 days after her GP started a new medicine. Her renal function is unchanged.

**Which new medicine is most likely responsible?**

A. Co-trimoxazole
B. Amoxicillin
C. Paracetamol
D. Omeprazole

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Sulphonylureas are highly protein bound. Drugs that displace them, such as sulpha drugs, NSAIDs, anti-thyroid drugs, anticoagulants and α-blockers, increase hypoglycaemia risk. Co-trimoxazole is a sulpha drug. The other options have no such interaction. Older age adds to the risk, and she is on the maximum gliclazide dose.

**Teaching point:** Sulpha drugs, NSAIDs, anticoagulants and α-blockers potentiate SUs and cause hypoglycaemia.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.1 (Sulphonylureas).

### Q63
**Topic:** Add-on choice with fragility fracture

A 68-year-old woman with T2DM had a distal radius fracture after a minor fall last year. DXA shows osteoporosis. She takes metformin, and HbA1c is 7.9%. eGFR is 70 ml/min/1.73 m2 and she has no heart failure.

**Which add-on is the least suitable for her?**

A. Sitagliptin
B. Empagliflozin
C. Gliclazide MR
D. Pioglitazone

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** TZD side effects include weight gain, fluid retention, heart failure, macular oedema and osteoporosis. TZD-associated fractures mainly affect the distal upper and lower limbs, which matches her previous fracture site. The other agents have no comparable bone risk, although gliclazide's hypoglycaemia risk needs attention in a faller.

**Teaching point:** Avoid pioglitazone in osteoporosis, especially with distal limb fractures.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.1 (Thiazolidinediones).

### Q64
**Topic:** Early combination therapy

A 45-year-old man has newly diagnosed T2DM with HbA1c 7.2%. He has no CVD, DKD or heart failure. He is keen to "keep things under control for as long as possible" and is willing to take two tablets from the start.

**Which initial combination has trial evidence of more durable glycaemic control?**

A. Metformin plus gliclazide
B. Metformin plus pioglitazone
C. Metformin plus vildagliptin
D. Metformin plus acarbose

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The VERIFY trial showed that starting metformin plus vildagliptin within 24 months of diagnosis delayed loss of glycaemic control compared with sequential therapy. The CPG algorithm footnote specifically suggests a DPP4 inhibitor (vildagliptin) for the HbA1c 6.5-7.4% band. The other combinations are acceptable but lack this durability evidence.

### Q65
**Topic:** Changing basal insulin formulation

A 63-year-old woman takes glargine U100 30 U at night and has recurrent nocturnal hypoglycaemia despite reasonable control. She is switched to glargine U300 at the same number of units, with weekly titration.

**What final dose change is most likely to be needed?**

A. About 20% lower than U100
B. Same dose as U100
C. About 10-17% higher than U100
D. About double the U100 dose

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Glargine U300 is non-inferior to U100 for control, with less overall and nocturnal hypoglycaemia, but the dose needed is generally 10-17% higher. A lower dose is the intuitive error, because the switch is for hypoglycaemia. Unit-for-unit conversion followed by upward titration is the usual practice.

**Teaching point:** U300 glargine reduces nocturnal hypoglycaemia but needs 10-17% more units.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5 (Insulin types).

### Q66
**Topic:** Timing of intermediate-acting insulin

A 70-year-old man takes NPH (Insulatard) 22 U at 18:00 with his evening tablets. He has had three episodes of sweating and confusion at around 02:00-03:00 with glucose of 3.1-3.4 mmol/l. His fasting glucose is 8-9 mmol/l.

**What is the most appropriate change?**

A. Reduce NPH by 50% at 18:00
B. Move NPH to bedtime, after 22:00
C. Add a bedtime snack, same NPH
D. Move NPH to before breakfast

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Basal intermediate-acting insulin should be given pre-bed (preferably not earlier than 22:00), because an earlier dose peaks at 2-5 am and causes nocturnal hypoglycaemia. Moving it later both prevents the 3 am low and lowers his high fasting glucose. Halving the dose worsens the fasting hyperglycaemia. Snacks are not needed with intermediate or long-acting insulin. A morning dose does not cover the night.

**Teaching point:** Give bedtime NPH no earlier than 22:00. An early dose causes 3 am hypos with high fasting glucose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5 (General guidelines for long-term use of insulin).

### Q67
**Topic:** Balancing basal and prandial insulin

A 58-year-old woman weighing 70 kg uses glargine 18 U and aspart 16 U before each meal (total 66 U/day). She has frequent post-meal hypoglycaemia, yet fasting glucose is 10-11 mmol/l. Adherence and technique are good.

**What is the most appropriate adjustment?**

A. Increase glargine; reduce aspart
B. Increase all aspart doses
C. Add a fourth bolus at supper
D. Switch to premixed three times daily

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Prandial insulin should generally not exceed 50% of the total daily dose. Hers is 48/66 (73%), while basal is only 27%. Excess bolus causes post-meal lows, and insufficient basal leaves fasting hyperglycaemia. Rebalancing towards basal and reducing the bolus doses corrects both. More or extra boluses worsen the imbalance. Premixed insulin three times daily still links basal to meal doses.

**Teaching point:** Prandial insulin should be no more than 50% of TDD. A high fasting glucose with post-meal lows means basal is too low and bolus too high.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5 (General guidelines).

### Q68
**Topic:** Before vigorous exercise

A 62-year-old man with T2DM for 15 years, a 40 pack-year smoking history and intermittent claudication has been sedentary for years. He wants to start training for a 10 km run. He has no chest pain. BP is 138/84 mmHg.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Start running now, building gradually
B. Walking only; avoid vigorous exercise
C. Exercise treadmill test for all diabetics
D. Pre-exercise assessment first

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Before vigorous exercise, older, previously sedentary people with long-standing T2DM, multiple risk factors or known atherosclerosis (he has PAD) should be considered for pre-exercise assessment, including for complications that limit exercise. Starting straight away ignores his risk. Banning vigorous exercise outright is unnecessary. Screening every diabetic is not recommended. His eventual target is 150 min/week moderate (or 75 vigorous) plus two resistance sessions.

**Teaching point:** High-risk sedentary patients need assessment before vigorous exercise, not a blanket ban.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.5.2 (Recommendations: Physical activity).

### Q70
**Topic:** Adolescent failing metformin

A 15-year-old boy with T2DM and BMI 34 kg/m2 has HbA1c 8.3% on metformin 1 g twice daily, despite good adherence and dietitian input. He has no ketosis. His family are keen to avoid insulin if possible.

**Which add-on does the CPG suggest may be considered?**

A. Sitagliptin
B. Gliclazide
C. Liraglutide
D. Empagliflozin

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Metformin and insulin remain the mainstay of adolescent T2DM treatment. The CPG adds that the GLP1-RA liraglutide may be considered. Adolescent T2DM progresses faster, with β-cell loss of 20-35% a year compared with 7-11% in adults, so treatment needs early escalation. The other drugs are adult options not endorsed for adolescents in the CPG.

**Teaching point:** Adolescent T2DM: metformin and insulin, with liraglutide as an option. Escalate early.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.2 (T2DM in adolescents).
