# Management of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (6th Edition, 2020): MCQs, Part 2 (Hard)

**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia / Academy of Medicine Malaysia, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus*, 6th Edition (2020).
**Format:** Single-best-answer, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style. Every option is a near-miss, so none can be eliminated without knowing the CPG detail. These add to the 16 existing Type 2 DM questions.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from the CPG text. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

---

### Q1
**Topic:** DKA: potassium before insulin

A 46-year-old man weighing 80 kg presents with vomiting and abdominal pain after 3 days of a febrile illness. Capillary glucose is 26 mmol/l, ketones 5.1 mmol/l, venous pH 7.12, bicarbonate 9 mmol/l and potassium 3.2 mmol/l. SBP is 104 mmHg. One litre of 0.9% saline is running.

**What is the most appropriate insulin management at this point?**

A. FRIII 8 U/h now
B. 8 U IV bolus, then FRIII
C. FRIII 4 U/h until potassium normalises
D. Delay insulin; replace potassium first

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** All three DKA criteria are met. The CPG FRIII rate is 0.1 U/kg/h (8 U/h here), but insulin should be delayed if the initial potassium is below 3.5 mmol/l until it is corrected, because insulin drives potassium into cells. A half-rate infusion still lowers potassium. A bolus is not part of the CPG regimen.

**Teaching point:** In DKA, if K+ is below 3.5 mmol/l, replace potassium before starting the 0.1 U/kg/h FRIII.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2 (DKA: insulin and potassium).

### Q2
**Topic:** DKA: glucose falling, ketones persisting

A 52-year-old woman weighing 60 kg is 6 hours into DKA treatment on FRIII at 6 U/h with 0.9% saline and KCl. Capillary glucose is now 12.5 mmol/l, blood ketones 2.8 mmol/l, venous pH 7.24 and potassium 4.4 mmol/l.

**What is the most appropriate adjustment?**

A. Keep the FRIII rate and add 10% dextrose
B. Stop the FRIII and give basal insulin
C. Halve the FRIII and add 5% dextrose
D. Halve the FRIII and add 10% dextrose

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Once glucose falls below 14 mmol/l, the usual step is to add 5% dextrose and halve the insulin rate. However, the CPG states that if ketonaemia persists, the insulin rate should be maintained and 10% dextrose used instead. Her ketones are still 2.8 mmol/l and she is acidotic, so the insulin is still needed to clear ketones. DKA has not resolved (pH >7.3 and ketones <0.6 mmol/l), so stopping the FRIII is premature.

**Teaching point:** In DKA, glucose below 14 mmol/l with persistent ketones means keep the insulin rate and switch to 10% dextrose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.2 (Insulin; Resolution).

### Q3
**Topic:** HHS: rising sodium

A 74-year-old man with HHS weighs 70 kg. On admission: glucose 48 mmol/l, sodium 142 mmol/l, urea 22 mmol/l and calculated osmolality 332 mOsm/kg. Ketones are 0.8 mmol/l. After 2 hours of 0.9% saline (no insulin yet), glucose is 38 mmol/l, sodium 146 mmol/l and osmolality is falling by 5 mOsm/kg/h.

**What is the most appropriate fluid management?**

A. Switch to 0.45% saline
B. Switch to 5% dextrose
C. Switch to 0.18% saline with 4% dextrose
D. Continue 0.9% saline

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** As glucose falls, water moves out of cells and the measured sodium rises. The CPG states that an initial rise in sodium is expected and is not, on its own, an indication for hypotonic fluid. 0.45% saline is used if sodium is above 145 mmol/l *and* osmolality is not falling (by less than 3 mOsm/kg/h) despite adequate hydration. His osmolality is falling at 5 mOsm/kg/h, within the 3-8 target, so 0.9% saline should continue. Dextrose is added only once glucose is below 14 mmol/l.

**Teaching point:** In HHS, a rising sodium with appropriately falling osmolality is expected. Change to 0.45% saline only if osmolality stalls.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.4 (HHS: principles of treatment).

### Q4
**Topic:** HHS: insulin rate

A 79-year-old woman weighing 60 kg with HHS (glucose 41 mmol/l, osmolality 338 mOsm/kg, ketones 1.1 mmol/l, pH 7.36) has had 2 litres of 0.9% saline. Glucose has fallen to 33 mmol/l and is no longer falling with fluids alone. The team starts IV insulin.

**What is the most appropriate initial FRIII rate?**

A. 1 U/h
B. 3 U/h
C. 6 U/h
D. 9 U/h

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** HHS uses a lower insulin rate than DKA: FRIII 0.05 U/kg/h, which is 3 U/h for 60 kg. 6 U/h is the DKA rate (0.1 U/kg/h), and it risks a rapid fall in glucose and osmolality, with osmotic demyelination and cerebral oedema. The target fall in glucose is 4-6 mmol/l/h. Patients with HHS are more insulin sensitive.

**Teaching point:** FRIII is 0.05 U/kg/h in HHS and 0.1 U/kg/h in DKA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.4; Figure 4-3.

### Q5
**Topic:** Ketoacidosis with near-normal glucose

A 58-year-old woman on metformin and empagliflozin is admitted 2 days after a laparoscopic cholecystectomy with nausea and tachypnoea. Glucose is 9.8 mmol/l, blood ketones 4.6 mmol/l, pH 7.18, bicarbonate 11 mmol/l and anion gap 24. She is clinically dry.

**Which IV fluid should accompany the insulin infusion?**

A. 5% dextrose saline
B. Hartmann's solution
C. 0.9% saline alone
D. 0.45% saline

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Ketoacidosis with glucose below 11 mmol/l on an SGLT2 inhibitor after surgery is euglycaemic DKA. Treatment follows the standard DKA protocol, except that dehydration is corrected with 5% dextrose saline or 5% dextrose, so that insulin can continue without causing hypoglycaemia. 10% dextrose may be needed if glucose is low and she is not dehydrated. Saline alone would force the insulin to be cut back before the ketones clear. The SGLT2 inhibitor should be withheld until she has recovered and is eating.

**Teaching point:** Euglycaemic DKA: standard FRIII plus dextrose-containing fluid, and stop the SGLT2 inhibitor.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.3 (Euglycaemic ketoacidosis).

### Q6
**Topic:** Hypoglycaemia on acarbose

A 66-year-old man takes gliclazide MR 120 mg, metformin and acarbose 100 mg three times daily. Mid-afternoon at clinic he becomes sweaty and tremulous. Capillary glucose is 3.1 mmol/l. He is alert and able to swallow.

**What is the most appropriate immediate treatment?**

A. 150-200 ml orange juice
B. 3 teaspoons sugar in water
C. Regular soft drink 200 ml
D. Glucose (dextrose) tablets 15 g

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Acarbose inhibits α-glucosidase, so sucrose and complex carbohydrates are absorbed slowly. The CPG says people taking acarbose must treat hypoglycaemia with glucose (dextrose) tablets, or with milk or honey if tablets are unavailable. Juice, table sugar and soft drinks are the standard 15 g remedies for other patients, but their sucrose depends on the inhibited enzyme. Glucose should be rechecked at 15 minutes.

**Teaching point:** On acarbose, treat hypoglycaemia with pure glucose, not sucrose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1 (Treatment of hypoglycaemia).

### Q7
**Topic:** Classifying hypoglycaemia

A 70-year-old woman on basal insulin records a glucose of 2.8 mmol/l at 03:00 during a check prompted by night sweats. She woke, felt shaky, and treated herself with juice without help. Her next reading 15 minutes later was 5.2 mmol/l.

**How is this episode classified under the CPG?**

A. Level 1 hypoglycaemia
B. Level 2 hypoglycaemia
C. Level 3 hypoglycaemia
D. Pseudo-hypoglycaemia

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Level 1 is glucose <3.9 but ≥3.0 mmol/l. Level 2 is glucose <3.0 mmol/l, the threshold for neuroglycopenic symptoms, which needs immediate action. Level 3 is defined by altered mental or physical function needing another person's help, not by a glucose value. She treated herself, so the event is level 2 rather than level 3. Nocturnal hypoglycaemia on basal insulin should prompt checks at 2-5 am and a switch to an analogue if she is on human basal insulin.

**Teaching point:** Below 3.0 mmol/l is level 2. Level 3 is defined by needing assistance, whatever the glucose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1, Table 4-2.

### Q8
**Topic:** Basal insulin titrated, HbA1c still high

A 61-year-old man weighing 80 kg takes metformin, a DPP4 inhibitor and glargine 34 U at night, titrated over 4 months. Fasting glucose now runs at 5.5-6.5 mmol/l without hypoglycaemia. HbA1c is 8.6%. Post-dinner readings are 13-15 mmol/l. Adherence is good.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Increase glargine by 2 U every 3 days
B. Add prandial insulin 4 U before dinner
C. Switch glargine to glargine U300
D. Add a sulphonylurea

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Fasting glucose is at target, but HbA1c is high and post-dinner glucose is elevated. That pattern calls for intensification with prandial insulin (4 U per meal, or 10% of the basal dose) at the largest meal, or a switch to premixed insulin. Increasing the basal dose further risks nocturnal hypoglycaemia and "over-basalisation" (his dose is 0.43 U/kg). U300 changes the insulin profile but does not address post-meal spikes. A sulphonylurea is usually reduced or stopped when insulin is intensified.

**Teaching point:** Fasting glucose at target with a high HbA1c means adding prandial insulin, not more basal insulin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5; Figure 3-2.

### Q9
**Topic:** Very high insulin requirement

A 48-year-old woman with BMI 31 kg/m2 (90 kg) takes a basal-bolus regimen totalling 190 U/day. HbA1c remains 9.4%. She injects into the same area of her abdomen, which has a soft, rubbery swelling. There is no fever.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Review injection technique and sites
B. Switch to an insulin pump
C. Increase total dose by 10%
D. Add pioglitazone 45 mg

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Her total daily dose is 2.1 U/kg, above the 1.5-2.0 U/kg threshold that the CPG says should prompt a search for a secondary cause: non-adherence, incorrect dose, timing or injection technique, or occult infection. Lipohypertrophy from repeated injection into one site causes erratic absorption. Escalating the dose, moving to a pump, or adding pioglitazone (which adds fluid retention with insulin) are all premature.

**Teaching point:** If total insulin exceeds 1.5-2.0 U/kg/day, check adherence, technique and injection sites before escalating.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.6.5 (General guidelines for long-term use of insulin).

### Q10
**Topic:** Glycaemic target in a complex older patient

An 81-year-old woman with T2DM has heart failure, CKD stage 3b, osteoarthritis limiting mobility and mild cognitive impairment (MoCA 21/30). She lives with her daughter. She has had two falls this year. HbA1c is 7.4% on gliclazide MR 60 mg and linagliptin.

**What is the most appropriate HbA1c goal?**

A. ≤6.5%
B. ≤7.0%
C. <8.0%
D. <8.5%

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** With several coexisting chronic illnesses and mild cognitive impairment, she falls in the CPG's "complex/intermediate" category, which has an HbA1c goal of <8.0% (fasting glucose 5-8.3 mmol/l). <8.5% is for "very complex/poor health" patients: long-term care, end-stage illness, or moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment. ≤7.0% and ≤6.5% are for healthier or newly diagnosed patients. Her sulphonylurea, falls and an HbA1c of 7.4% suggest that treatment could be de-intensified.

**Teaching point:** Older people: healthy ≤7.5%, complex <8.0%, very complex <8.5%. Base the category on frailty, not age.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.3, Table 6-2.
