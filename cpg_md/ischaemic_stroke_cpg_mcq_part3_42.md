## Section 1: Primary Prevention

### Q1
**Topic:** Antiplatelet for primary prevention

A 62-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes for 10 years attends a review. She has no history of stroke, TIA, angina or claudication. She takes metformin, perindopril and atorvastatin 20 mg. BP is 128/76 mmHg, LDL-C 2.0 mmol/l, HbA1c 6.9% and eGFR 75 ml/min/1.73 m2. A friend has told her that "all diabetics should be on aspirin".

**What is the most appropriate antithrombotic advice?**

A. Aspirin 75 mg daily
B. Aspirin 150 mg daily
C. Clopidogrel 75 mg daily
D. No antiplatelet therapy

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The stroke CPG does not recommend daily aspirin for primary prevention of stroke in diabetics, moderate-risk individuals or older people, because bleeding risk outweighs benefit (Level I, Grade A). She has no vascular events. Clopidogrel has no primary-prevention role either. Her risk is better addressed by continuing BP, lipid and glucose control.

**Teaching point:** Diabetes alone is not an indication for aspirin to prevent a first stroke.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.10 (Medical therapy).

### Q2
**Topic:** BP target with diabetes

A 55-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and microalbuminuria, but no previous stroke or coronary disease, is reviewed. On amlodipine 10 mg his clinic BP averages 138/84 mmHg and home readings are similar. He has no postural symptoms.

**What is the most appropriate BP management?**

A. Continue; BP is at target
B. Add an ACE inhibitor
C. Add a beta-blocker
D. Add perindopril plus losartan

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** For diabetics the stroke CPG target is below 130/80 mmHg, preferably below 120 mmHg systolic if tolerated (Level I, Grade A). At 138/84 he is above target. With albuminuria, an ACE inhibitor (or ARB) is the logical add-on. "At target" applies the general <140/90 threshold, which is wrong for diabetes. Reducing treatment or deferring review leaves him undertreated.

**Teaching point:** Diabetes means a BP target below 130/80 mmHg. Add RAS blockade, especially if there is albuminuria.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.2 (Hypertension); Section 5.4.6.

### Q3
**Topic:** Menopausal women and stroke risk

A 54-year-old woman with troublesome vasomotor symptoms has a mother and sister who both had ischaemic strokes in their 60s. She has no other vascular risk factors. She asks whether combined HRT would "protect her brain and heart" as well as treat her symptoms.

**What is the most accurate advice?**

A. HRT reduces stroke risk if started early
B. HRT is neutral for stroke risk
C. HRT may increase stroke risk
D. HRT is neutral if transdermal

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Post-menopausal hormone therapy may increase stroke risk and is not recommended for primary stroke prevention (Level II, Grade B). In the WHI, oestrogen plus progestin increased stroke by 31%. The "timing hypothesis" and claims of neutrality or protection are common misconceptions. HRT may still be used for symptoms on its own merits, but not for stroke prevention.

**Teaching point:** HRT does not prevent stroke. Combined HRT increased stroke risk by about 31% in the WHI.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.5.

### Q4
**Topic:** Statin in a high-risk patient with normal lipids

A 58-year-old man with intermittent claudication confirmed on ABI (0.72) is reviewed. He has never had a stroke or myocardial infarction. Total cholesterol is 4.6 mmol/l and LDL-C 2.4 mmol/l. He stopped smoking last year. BP is 132/80 mmHg on amlodipine.

**What is the most appropriate lipid management for stroke prevention?**

A. Diet only, as LDL-C is normal
B. Fenofibrate 145 mg
C. Ezetimibe 10 mg alone
D. Start high-intensity statin

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Peripheral arterial disease puts him in the high-risk group. In high-risk people, including those with occlusive arterial disease, statin therapy is recommended to reduce coronary events and ischaemic stroke even when cholesterol is normal (Level I, Grade A). Intensive lipid lowering is superior to standard therapy in this group. A "normal" LDL-C is the trap.

**Teaching point:** In high vascular risk (CVD, PAD, diabetes), give a statin whatever the baseline cholesterol.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.7 (Hyperlipidaemia).

### Q5
**Topic:** Asymptomatic stenosis with limited life expectancy

A 79-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer (estimated survival under 2 years), COPD on home oxygen and an LVEF of 30% is found to have an asymptomatic 85% left ICA stenosis on a duplex scan done for a bruit. He has never had neurological symptoms.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. Best medical therapy
B. Carotid endarterectomy
C. Carotid stenting
D. EC-IC bypass

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** CEA for high-grade asymptomatic stenosis "may be considered", but only after careful selection that takes account of comorbidity, life expectancy and preference. Asymptomatic patients should not be offered CEA if their 5-year probability of dying from other causes is high. On medical therapy the annual stroke risk is about 2%, and CEA reduces it by only about 1% per year, a benefit he will not live long enough to gain. Stenting carries more periprocedural stroke, and EC-IC bypass is not recommended.

**Teaching point:** Asymptomatic stenosis surgery requires a good life expectancy. If 5-year survival is poor, use medical therapy.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.1 (Primary prevention).

### Q6
**Topic:** Asymptomatic stenosis in a fit patient

A 66-year-old woman who is fit and independent, with an expected survival well beyond 5 years, has an incidental 80% right ICA stenosis on duplex, confirmed on CTA. She has never had a TIA or stroke. She is on a statin and her BP is controlled. The local vascular surgeon's audited stroke and death rate for asymptomatic CEA is 2%.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. Urgent CEA within 2 weeks
B. Consider elective CEA
C. Carotid stenting as first choice
D. CEA only if stenosis 90% or more

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** For asymptomatic 70-99% stenosis, CEA may be considered if the surgeon's morbidity and mortality is below 3%, after shared discussion of risks and benefits (Level I, Grade A). Her surgeon's 2% meets this. The 2-week urgency applies to symptomatic disease. Stenting is an alternative when surgery is difficult, not first choice. Surveillance alone ignores a reasonable option.

**Teaching point:** Asymptomatic CEA needs a surgeon with a complication rate below 3% (below 6% for symptomatic disease) and a patient with a good life expectancy.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.1.

### Q7
**Topic:** Hypertension in the very elderly

An 84-year-old woman lives alone, walks to the market daily and has an MMSE of 29/30. Home and clinic readings average 168/88 mmHg over 2 months with no postural drop. She has no previous stroke. Her family feel that treatment "isn't worth it at her age".

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. Lifestyle advice alone
B. Treat only if above 180 mmHg
C. No treatment over age 80
D. Start antihypertensives

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Newly diagnosed hypertension in the very elderly (over 80) should be treated (Level I, Grade B). An individualised approach is needed for frail, multimorbid or cognitively impaired older people, but she is fit and independent. Age is not a reason to withhold treatment, and inventing a higher threshold is not supported.

**Teaching point:** Treat hypertension in fit people over 80. Base targets on frailty, not age.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.2; Section 12.3.1.

## Section 2: Secondary Prevention

### Q8
**Topic:** Antiplatelet after aspirin anaphylaxis

A 59-year-old man has a right internal capsule infarct (NIHSS 5) 3 days after a pure motor hemiparesis began. He had anaphylaxis to aspirin as a teenager. ECG and 72-hour Holter show sinus rhythm, echo is normal, and carotid duplex shows less than 30% stenosis bilaterally.

**What is the most appropriate long-term antithrombotic?**

A. Clopidogrel 75 mg daily
B. Warfarin, INR 2-3
C. Aspirin desensitisation
D. Aspirin plus clopidogrel

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** This is small vessel disease, which needs long-term antiplatelet therapy. With aspirin allergy, an alternative antiplatelet is used, and clopidogrel 75 mg daily is the standard choice. Warfarin is not indicated in sinus rhythm without a cardioembolic source. Desensitisation is unnecessary when effective alternatives exist. The aspirin-containing DAPT regimen is barred by anaphylaxis, and with NIHSS 5 he is outside the DAPT criteria anyway.

**Teaching point:** If aspirin is impossible, use clopidogrel 75 mg (or cilostazol, triflusal or ticlopidine). Never use warfarin in sinus rhythm.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1 (Anti-platelet therapy); Table 14.1.

### Q9
**Topic:** Recurrence on aspirin

A 64-year-old woman has a second left hemispheric infarct while taking aspirin 100 mg daily, which she takes reliably. ECG and a 72-hour Holter show sinus rhythm, TTE is normal and carotid stenosis is below 50%. LDL-C is 3.1 mmol/l on simvastatin 10 mg and BP is 152/90 mmHg. Her son wants her switched to warfarin because "aspirin has failed".

**What is the most appropriate change?**

A. Switch to warfarin
B. Add rivaroxaban to aspirin
C. Switch to apixaban
D. Optimise risk factors

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Warfarin is not indicated for secondary prevention in sinus rhythm without a cardioembolic source (Level I, Grade A). A recurrence on aspirin should prompt a review of the modifiable risks. Her LDL-C is far above the 1.8 mmol/l target and her BP is uncontrolled. An alternative antiplatelet can also be considered. DOACs have no role in non-cardioembolic stroke, and in ESUS rivaroxaban was no better than aspirin but caused more bleeding.

**Teaching point:** Recurrence on aspirin in sinus rhythm means optimising risk factors, not anticoagulating.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1-5.5.3.

### Q10
**Topic:** BP lowering after stroke in a normotensive patient

A 61-year-old man is seen 3 weeks after a minor left MCA branch infarct. He has made a full recovery. His BP is 128/80 mmHg off all treatment, and repeated readings are similar. He takes clopidogrel and atorvastatin 40 mg. eGFR is 82 ml/min/1.73 m2.

**What is the most appropriate BP management?**

A. No treatment; BP normal
B. Start an ACE inhibitor
C. Start atenolol 50 mg
D. Start a thiazide alone

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** From 2 weeks or more after stroke, ACE inhibitor-based therapy reduces recurrence in both normotensive and hypertensive patients (Level I, Grade A). An ACE inhibitor with a thiazide was beneficial when started 2 weeks after the event. A normal BP is the trap. Beta-blockers are not the evidence-based class (losartan outperformed atenolol in LVH).

**Teaching point:** After the acute phase, offer ACE inhibitor-based therapy even when BP is normal.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.2 (Anti-hypertensive treatment).

### Q11
**Topic:** Bleeding risk with anticoagulation

A 72-year-old man with AF (CHA2DS2-VASc 5) is starting warfarin after a TIA. Hypertension is poorly controlled (systolic 170 mmHg). Renal and liver function are normal. He has had no bleeding. His INR was labile on a previous warfarin trial. He takes naproxen daily for knee pain and drinks within safe limits.

**What is his HAS-BLED score?**

A. 3
B. 4
C. 5
D. 6

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** HAS-BLED scores 1 point each for: uncontrolled hypertension (1), abnormal renal or liver function (0), stroke (TIA counts, 1), bleeding history (0), labile INR (1), elderly over 65 (1), and drugs such as NSAIDs or aspirin (1) or alcohol abuse (0). Total 5. Several items are modifiable (BP, naproxen, INR control) and should be addressed. A DOAC would also remove the labile-INR point.

**Teaching point:** HAS-BLED identifies bleeding risks to correct, such as BP, NSAIDs and INR control. It is not a reason to deny OAC.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.7 (HAS-BLED); Section 13.3.

### Q12
**Topic:** Antiplatelet when two drugs are excluded

A 58-year-old man has a minor non-cardioembolic left hemispheric infarct. He had angioedema with aspirin, and a severe exfoliative rash with clopidogrel last year. He is in sinus rhythm with no significant carotid disease.

**Which antiplatelet is most appropriate?**

A. Prasugrel
B. Ticagrelor
C. Ticagrelor plus aspirin
D. Dipyridamole plus aspirin

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Ticagrelor was not superior to aspirin overall in SOCRATES. The Asian sub-analysis showed a trend towards benefit with similar safety, so the CPG considers ticagrelor a reasonable alternative when aspirin is contraindicated. Both combination options contain aspirin, which he cannot take. Prasugrel is not a CPG option for stroke prevention.

**Teaching point:** If aspirin and clopidogrel are both excluded, ticagrelor is a reasonable alternative.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1 (Ticagrelor).

### Q13
**Topic:** Which carotid?

A 66-year-old woman had 20 minutes of left arm and leg weakness 5 weeks ago, with full recovery. Carotid duplex and CTA show 60% stenosis of the left ICA and 25% stenosis of the right ICA. The local centre's perioperative stroke and death rate is 4%.

**What is the most appropriate management of her carotid disease?**

A. Left carotid endarterectomy
B. Right carotid endarterectomy
C. Best medical therapy
D. Left carotid stenting

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Left limb weakness localises to the right hemisphere, so the symptomatic artery is the right ICA, which is only 25% stenosed. CEA is not recommended for stenosis under 50%. The 60% left ICA stenosis is effectively asymptomatic, and the CPG supports CEA for asymptomatic disease only at 70-99%. Operating on, or stenting, the left side treats the wrong artery.

**Teaching point:** Localise the symptoms first, then apply the stenosis thresholds to the artery that supplies that territory.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.1 and 5.8.2 (Revascularisation procedures).

### Q14
**Topic:** Timing of endarterectomy

A 69-year-old man had 2 hours of right hand weakness and dysphasia 4 days ago, with full recovery. CTA shows 85% stenosis of the left ICA. He is independent. The vascular unit's audited perioperative stroke and death rate is 3.5%. The surgeon offers a date in 8 weeks.

**What is the most appropriate timing of revascularisation?**

A. Within 6 weeks
B. Within 4 weeks
C. Within 2 weeks
D. Only if symptoms recur

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For symptomatic 70-99% stenosis without a severe deficit, early CEA within 2 weeks of the event is indicated in centres with a perioperative stroke and death rate below 6%. Recurrence risk is highest early. Delaying to 8 weeks or 3 months loses much of the benefit, and waiting for recurrence is unsafe. He should stay on antithrombotic therapy before and after surgery.

**Teaching point:** Symptomatic 70-99% stenosis: CEA within 2 weeks, in a centre with a complication rate below 6%.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.2.

### Q15
**Topic:** Revascularisation in a hostile neck

A 62-year-old man had a radical neck dissection and radiotherapy for laryngeal cancer 6 years ago. He now has a left hemispheric TIA with 85% left ICA stenosis. The vascular surgeon considers his neck unsuitable for open surgery. His aortic arch is type I with minimal tortuosity.

**What is the most appropriate revascularisation?**

A. Carotid endarterectomy
B. Carotid artery stenting
C. EC-IC bypass
D. Best medical therapy only

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Carotid stenting is a feasible alternative to CEA when surgery is undesirable, technically difficult or inaccessible, as with a post-radiation, post-dissection neck. Simple arch anatomy lowers his stenting risk (complex arches and ICA tortuosity increase it). Medical therapy alone leaves a symptomatic 85% stenosis untreated, and EC-IC bypass is not recommended.

**Teaching point:** For a hostile neck with symptomatic high-grade stenosis, use stenting. Arch anatomy predicts stenting risk.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.9 (Angioplasty or stenting).

### Q16
**Topic:** Antiplatelet around carotid surgery

A 69-year-old woman is listed for left CEA 8 days after a TIA with 80% stenosis. She is on aspirin 100 mg and atorvastatin 80 mg. The anaesthetic pre-assessment nurse writes "stop aspirin 7 days pre-op, as per elective surgery policy".

**What is the most appropriate antiplatelet plan?**

A. Stop aspirin 7 days before surgery
B. Stop aspirin; bridge with LMWH
C. Switch to warfarin pre-operatively
D. Continue aspirin through surgery

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Patients should remain on antithrombotic therapy before and after carotid surgery (Level II-2, Grade B). Stopping aspirin in the highest-risk early period after a TIA invites recurrence. Bridging with LMWH and switching to warfarin have no role.

**Teaching point:** Do not stop antiplatelet therapy for CEA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.2.

### Q17
**Topic:** Haemorrhagic transformation and AF

A 71-year-old woman with newly diagnosed AF is admitted with a right MCA infarct (NIHSS 6). Day-2 CT shows confluent haemorrhagic transformation occupying much of the infarct, with mild mass effect. She is clinically stable.

**What is the most appropriate timing for starting oral anticoagulation?**

A. Day 3
B. Day 6
C. Delay 1-2 weeks
D. Day 12

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The 1-3-6-12 day rule (NIHSS <8 would suggest day 3) applies only after haemorrhagic transformation has been excluded. With substantial haemorrhage, the CPG advises delaying anticoagulation for 1-2 weeks. Applying day 3 ignores the bleed. OAC remains indicated in the long term, as she has AF and a previous stroke.

**Teaching point:** The 1-3-6-12 rule assumes no haemorrhagic transformation. A substantial bleed means delaying OAC for 1-2 weeks.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3; Table 5.5.

## Section 3: Atrial Fibrillation and Cardioembolism

### Q18
**Topic:** AF on aspirin

A 76-year-old man with permanent non-valvular AF, hypertension and diabetes has been on aspirin 100 mg for 3 years "because warfarin is dangerous". He has had no bleeding. eGFR is 62 ml/min/1.73 m2, Hb 138 g/l, and BP 134/80 mmHg.

**What is the most appropriate stroke prevention?**

A. Continue aspirin
B. Aspirin plus clopidogrel
C. Aspirin plus rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily
D. Switch to a DOAC

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** His CHA2DS2-VASc is 4 (age 75 or over = 2, hypertension, diabetes), above the male threshold of 2 for OAC. Antiplatelet monotherapy is not indicated for stroke prevention in NVAF (Level I), and OAC is superior to aspirin. DOACs are preferred over VKA. DAPT is inferior to OAC and also bleeds. A higher aspirin dose adds bleeding without protection.

**Teaching point:** Aspirin does not prevent stroke in AF. Use OAC, preferably a DOAC.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.2; Key Recommendations 4 and 6 (Chapter 13).

### Q19
**Topic:** AF with rheumatic mitral disease

A 48-year-old woman has a left MCA TIA. She has AF and a low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at the apex with an opening snap. Echo shows a mitral valve area of 1.1 cm2. Her eGFR is normal and she asks for apixaban because it "needs no blood tests".

**Which anticoagulant is most appropriate?**

A. Apixaban
B. Dabigatran
C. Rivaroxaban
D. Warfarin

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** An opening snap and mid-diastolic rumble with a valve area of 1.1 cm2 indicate moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis. For valvular AF (moderate-to-severe MS) and mechanical valves, the OAC is a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) (Level I, Grade A), targeting INR 2.5 (range 2.0-3.0). DOACs are preferred only for non-valvular AF.

**Teaching point:** Moderate-to-severe MS or a mechanical valve: warfarin, not a DOAC.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.2.

### Q20
**Topic:** Timing of DOAC: moderate stroke

A 72-year-old woman with newly found AF has a left MCA infarct with aphasia and right arm weakness (NIHSS 11 on admission). Repeat CT on day 2 shows no haemorrhagic transformation. She is stable and swallowing safely.

**On which day should her DOAC be started?**

A. Day 1
B. Day 3
C. Day 6
D. Day 12

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Under the CPG 1-3-6-12 day rule (after excluding haemorrhagic transformation): TIA, day 1; mild (NIHSS <8), day 3; moderate (NIHSS 8-15), day 6; severe (NIHSS ≥16), day 12. NIHSS 11 is moderate, so day 6. Aspirin may be used until then.

**Teaching point:** 1-3-6-12: TIA day 1, NIHSS <8 day 3, NIHSS 8-15 day 6, NIHSS ≥16 day 12.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3 (1-3-6-12 day rule).

### Q21
**Topic:** Timing of DOAC: severe stroke

A 79-year-old man with known AF (anticoagulation previously stopped because of non-adherence) presents with left hemiplegia, gaze deviation and neglect (NIHSS 18). There is no haemorrhagic transformation on day-3 CT. His son asks for "blood thinners straight away to stop another clot".

**What is the most appropriate anticoagulation plan?**

A. Start DOAC today
B. LMWH now, DOAC at day 6
C. Start DOAC on day 6
D. Start DOAC on day 12

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** NIHSS 16 or above is a severe stroke, so the DOAC starts on day 12 under the 1-3-6-12 rule. Urgent anticoagulation is not recommended for moderate-to-large infarcts because of the high risk of intracranial haemorrhage, and parenteral heparin bridging after cardioembolic stroke is not recommended.

**Teaching point:** In a large AF stroke, wait about 12 days and do not bridge with heparin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3; Table 5.5.

### Q22
**Topic:** Bridging after cardioembolic stroke

A 68-year-old woman with AF has a moderate left MCA infarct (NIHSS 10). There is no haemorrhage on CT. The house officer prescribes enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily from day 1, "to cover her until the DOAC starts".

**What is the most appropriate antithrombotic plan until the DOAC starts?**

A. Aspirin only
B. Therapeutic enoxaparin
C. IV unfractionated heparin
D. Clopidogrel load, then DOAC

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** After a cardioembolic stroke, parenteral anticoagulation (heparin or LMWH) is not recommended to prevent secondary stroke (Level I, Grade A), because it increases bleeding without improving outcome. Aspirin can be considered before OAC is started. Leaving her with no antithrombotic therapy at all is not the CPG approach.

**Teaching point:** Use aspirin, not heparin, until the 1-3-6-12 start day.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3; Table 5.5.

### Q23
**Topic:** OAC with stable coronary disease

A 74-year-old man with non-valvular AF had a TIA last week. He had a drug-eluting stent for stable angina 4 years ago and has had no coronary events since. He currently takes aspirin 100 mg. Apixaban is to be started.

**What is the most appropriate antithrombotic regimen?**

A. Apixaban plus aspirin
B. Apixaban alone
C. Apixaban, aspirin and clopidogrel
D. Aspirin plus clopidogrel

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The CPG warns that bleeding risk is high when OAC is combined with an antiplatelet for secondary stroke prevention. He has had stable CAD for years after his stent, with no recent events, so OAC alone is appropriate. Triple therapy greatly increases bleeding. DAPT without OAC is inadequate for AF.

**Teaching point:** Late after a stent with stable CAD, AF stroke prevention is OAC alone, without added antiplatelets.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3.

### Q24
**Topic:** Restarting OAC after ICH

A 70-year-old woman with non-valvular AF (CHA2DS2-VASc 5) had a lobar ICH 3 weeks ago while on warfarin. Her INR was 4.8 at the time because of an interaction with clarithromycin. Her BP is now controlled at 128/76 mmHg and she is recovering well. MRI shows no microbleeds elsewhere.

**When should oral anticoagulation be restarted?**

A. In 1-2 weeks
B. In 12 weeks
C. In 4-8 weeks
D. In 6 months

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** After ICH, OAC could be restarted after 4-8 weeks in an NVAF patient with a high CHA2DS2-VASc score once the underlying cause and bleeding risk factors are treated (Level II, Grade B). Her bleed had a correctable cause (an interaction producing a supratherapeutic INR), and her BP is controlled. A DOAC would be preferred. Never restarting leaves a high stroke risk untreated, and restarting now is too early.

**Teaching point:** Restart OAC 4-8 weeks after ICH in high-risk AF once the cause is corrected.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3.

### Q25
**Topic:** Poor warfarin control with a mechanical valve

A 66-year-old man with a mechanical mitral valve had a small cardioembolic stroke. His INR log over 6 months shows only 52% of readings in range, with frequent missed clinic visits. He asks to change to "the new tablets that don't need monitoring".

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. Switch to apixaban
B. Switch to dabigatran
C. Add aspirin to warfarin
D. Optimise warfarin control

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Mechanical valves need a VKA, so DOACs are excluded. The CPG requires regular INR monitoring with time in therapeutic range above 70%. At 52% the priority is improving control: adherence, follow-up, interactions and diet. Adding aspirin increases bleeding without fixing the problem.

**Teaching point:** For a mechanical valve, stay on warfarin and aim for a TTR above 70%.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.2; Key Recommendation 5 (Chapter 13).

### Q26
**Topic:** Anticoagulant-related bleeding

A 77-year-old woman presents with a large right basal ganglia haemorrhage. She takes a twice-daily anticoagulant for AF and her last dose was 3 hours ago. aPTT is 58 s (normal 26-36) and thrombin time is markedly prolonged. INR is 1.3. Anti-factor Xa activity is undetectable.

**What is the most appropriate specific reversal agent?**

A. Idarucizumab
B. Vitamin K
C. Andexanet alfa
D. Protamine sulfate

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** A prolonged aPTT and thrombin time, a near-normal INR and no anti-Xa activity identify a direct thrombin inhibitor: dabigatran. Its specific reversal is idarucizumab 5 g IV (two 2.5 g infusions). Andexanet is for factor Xa inhibitors, vitamin K is for warfarin and protamine is for heparin. Dabigatran is also removable by haemodialysis.

**Teaching point:** The clotting profile identifies the drug: thrombin time and aPTT mean dabigatran, reversed with idarucizumab.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.4 (General measures for DOACs in bleeding).

### Q27
**Topic:** Searching for the embolic source

A 67-year-old man has a right parietal cortical infarct and a separate small left occipital infarct on MRI. Admission ECG shows sinus rhythm. CTA shows no significant extracranial or intracranial stenosis. He has palpitations "now and then".

**What is the most appropriate next investigation?**

A. 72-hour Holter monitor
B. Daily 12-lead ECG for a week
C. Cardiac MRI
D. Cerebral catheter angiography

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Infarcts in more than one vascular territory with clean vessels suggest cardioembolism, and AF (often paroxysmal) causes about half of cardiogenic emboli. All stroke patients need a 12-lead ECG, and a 72-hour Holter is required to detect paroxysmal AF. Echo is also indicated. Exercise testing, coronary angiography and catheter angiography do not answer the question.

**Teaching point:** Embolic infarcts in several territories with clean vessels: look for paroxysmal AF with a 72-hour Holter.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.1.

### Q28
**Topic:** When TTE is not enough

A 44-year-old woman has a cryptogenic left MCA branch infarct. ECG, 72-hour Holter and TTE are normal. Leg ultrasound shows no DVT. A bubble study was not performed. The team still suspects a cardiac source of embolism.

**Which investigation is most likely to reveal the source?**

A. Repeat TTE in 3 months
B. Cardiac MRI
C. Transoesophageal echo
D. Coronary CT angiography

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** TOE is superior to TTE for left atrial and LAA thrombus, PFO, atrial septal aneurysm and aortic arch atheroma, which are the main possibilities in a young patient with a normal TTE. All patients under 45, and those with no apparent cause, need echo, with TOE where suspicion persists. Repeating TTE adds little, and coronary CT does not look for embolic sources.

**Teaching point:** Young cryptogenic stroke with a normal TTE: TOE for PFO, ASA, LAA thrombus and arch atheroma.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.1; Section 14.4.

### Q29
**Topic:** Embolic stroke after anterior MI

A 57-year-old man has a right MCA branch infarct 3 weeks after a large anterior STEMI treated with primary PCI. He is in sinus rhythm on a 72-hour Holter. Echo shows apical akinesia with a 1.5 cm mobile apical thrombus and an LVEF of 35%.

**What is the most appropriate anticoagulation?**

A. Warfarin for 3-6 months
B. Warfarin for 6-12 months
C. Lifelong warfarin therapy
D. No anticoagulation

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** For MI with LV thrombus, the CPG suggests warfarin for 6-12 months (target INR 2.5, range 2.0-3.0). High-risk features without thrombus warrant 3-6 months followed by aspirin. Lifelong warfarin is suggested for dilated non-ischaemic cardiomyopathies, including peripartum. Sinus rhythm does not remove the need for anticoagulation when a thrombus is present.

**Teaching point:** Post-MI LV thrombus: warfarin for 6-12 months. High risk but no thrombus: 3-6 months.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.7, Table 5.4 (Cardiac conditions predisposing to ischaemic stroke).

## Section 4: Stroke in the Young and Special Circumstances

### Q30
**Topic:** Embolic-looking stroke with no source

A 49-year-old man has a 2 cm cortical left MCA branch infarct. CTA shows no stenosis over 30%. ECG, 72-hour Holter and TTE show no major cardiac source. Thrombophilia and autoimmune screens are negative. He asks whether a "stronger blood thinner" would be safer than aspirin.

**What is the most appropriate long-term management?**

A. Rivaroxaban 15 mg daily
B. Aspirin, prolonged ECG monitoring
C. Warfarin, target INR 2-3, lifelong
D. Apixaban 5 mg twice daily

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** He meets the ESUS criteria: a non-lacunar infarct, no ≥50% stenosis in the supplying artery, no major-risk cardiac source and no other cause. Management is long-term antiplatelet therapy. Rivaroxaban was not superior to aspirin and caused more bleeding, and there is no role for empirical anticoagulation. Prolonged ambulatory ECG monitoring matters because occult AF is found in about 25% of ESUS.

**Teaching point:** ESUS: antiplatelet therapy and keep looking for AF. Do not anticoagulate empirically.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.2 (ESUS); Table 14.3.

### Q31
**Topic:** Young cryptogenic stroke with a shunt

A 38-year-old woman has a right parietal cortical infarct. Vascular imaging, 72-hour Holter, thrombophilia screen and leg Doppler are all normal. TOE shows a PFO with a large right-to-left shunt and an atrial septal aneurysm.

**What is the most appropriate secondary prevention?**

A. Lifelong warfarin, INR 2-3
B. Rivaroxaban
C. PFO closure + antiplatelet
D. Aspirin alone

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** PFO closure has moderate benefit in young and middle-aged patients with cryptogenic stroke, and closure plus antiplatelet therapy is recommended (Level I). An atrial septal aneurysm or large shunt further favours closure, which lowered recurrence compared with antiplatelets alone, though closure increases AF. Anticoagulation is reserved for patients who also have a venous source of embolism.

**Teaching point:** Young cryptogenic stroke with a high-risk PFO: closure plus antiplatelet.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.3 (PFO).

### Q32
**Topic:** Paradoxical embolism

A 41-year-old man develops left hemiparesis 2 days after a 14-hour flight. MRI shows a right MCA infarct. TOE shows a PFO with a right-to-left shunt on Valsalva. Lower limb ultrasound shows a left popliteal DVT. He has no contraindication to anticoagulation.

**What is the most appropriate antithrombotic management?**

A. Anticoagulation
B. Aspirin alone
C. IVC filter
D. Urgent PFO closure alone

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** A PFO with a venous thrombus suggests paradoxical embolism. For stroke patients with both a PFO and a venous source, anticoagulation is indicated, timed according to the size of the infarct (Level I). An IVC filter is used only when anticoagulation is contraindicated. PFO closure may be considered later depending on the risk of recurrent DVT, but closure alone does not treat the DVT.

**Teaching point:** PFO plus DVT: anticoagulate. IVC filter only if anticoagulation is contraindicated.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.3.

### Q33
**Topic:** Neck pain, Horner's and stroke

A 36-year-old woman develops left-sided neck pain and headache after a chiropractic neck manipulation. Two days later she notices left ptosis with a small left pupil, followed by clumsiness of the right hand. MRI shows a small left MCA infarct. Fat-suppressed T1 MRI shows a crescentic intramural signal in the left ICA.

**What is the most appropriate treatment?**

A. Antiplatelet therapy
B. High-dose corticosteroids
C. Immunosuppression
D. Supportive care alone

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Neck pain, an ipsilateral Horner's syndrome and a crescentic intramural haematoma after manipulation indicate internal carotid dissection, a leading cause of stroke in young adults. The CPG lists dissection management as antiplatelet treatment, with endovascular treatment in selected cases. Steroids and immunosuppression are for vasculitis. Supportive care alone is listed for illicit-drug-related stroke.

**Teaching point:** Cervical dissection: antiplatelet therapy, with endovascular treatment for selected cases.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.2, Table 14.1; Section 14.4 (Advanced imaging).

### Q34
**Topic:** Familial small vessel disease

A 42-year-old man has migraine with aura, two lacunar infarcts in 3 years, and early executive dysfunction. His mother had dementia in her 50s and his maternal uncle had "several strokes". MRI shows confluent white matter hyperintensities extending into the anterior temporal poles and external capsules. He has no hypertension or diabetes.

**Which test is most likely to confirm the diagnosis?**

A. Plasma α-galactosidase A
B. Serum lactate/pyruvate ratio
C. NOTCH3 mutation analysis
D. TREX1 mutation analysis

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Migraine with aura, recurrent lacunes, early cognitive decline, an autosomal dominant family history and anterior temporal pole involvement point to CADASIL, caused by NOTCH3 mutations. α-Gal tests for Fabry disease (X-linked). Lactate and pyruvate test for MELAS. TREX1 is for RVCL, which affects the retina as well as white matter. Small vessel disease is managed with long-term antiplatelet therapy.

**Teaching point:** CADASIL: NOTCH3. Fabry: α-gal. MELAS: lactate. RVCL: TREX1.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.1; Section 14.4 (Investigation of young stroke).

### Q35
**Topic:** Stroke in a young man with haemolytic anaemia

A 19-year-old man of African descent has a left MCA infarct. Hb is 78 g/l with a high reticulocyte count. The blood film shows irreversibly sickled cells and target cells. Haemoglobin electrophoresis shows 88% HbS. He recovers well.

**What is the most appropriate long-term secondary prevention?**

A. Lifelong warfarin
B. Aspirin alone
C. Hydroxycarbamide alone
D. Chronic transfusion

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** This is sickle cell disease. For patients with sickle cell disease and a previous ischaemic stroke or TIA, long-term blood transfusion to reduce HbS below 30% of total haemoglobin is recommended (Level I, Grade B). Anticoagulation has no role. Aspirin or folate alone do not address the sickling vasculopathy.

**Teaching point:** Sickle cell stroke: chronic transfusion to keep HbS below 30%.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.3 (Treatment of stroke in certain circumstances).

### Q36
**Topic:** Raised homocysteine after stroke

A 51-year-old woman has a lacunar stroke. Fasting homocysteine is 24 µmol/l (normal <15). B12 and folate are in the low-normal range. She has read that "B vitamins prevent strokes" and would prefer them to aspirin and a statin.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Vitamins can replace aspirin
B. They lower homocysteine only
C. They reduce recurrence by 25%
D. They help only if B12 is low

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In adults with recent stroke or TIA and mild to moderate hyperhomocysteinaemia, folate, B6 and B12 safely lower homocysteine but have not been shown to prevent stroke (Level III, Grade B). VITATOPS showed no overall secondary-prevention benefit. She still needs antiplatelet therapy, a statin and BP control. The vitamins are safe, so they are not contraindicated.

**Teaching point:** B vitamins correct the homocysteine level but do not prevent recurrence. Keep standard secondary prevention.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.3; Table 16.1.

### Q37
**Topic:** Headache, seizure and a bleed on the pill

A 29-year-old woman on the combined oral contraceptive pill has 5 days of worsening headache, then a focal motor seizure of the right leg. CT shows a small left parasagittal haemorrhage surrounded by oedema that does not follow an arterial territory. CT venography shows a filling defect in the superior sagittal sinus.

**What is the most appropriate initial antithrombotic treatment?**

A. Withhold anticoagulation
B. Therapeutic LMWH
C. Dabigatran
D. Systemic thrombolysis

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A parasagittal haemorrhagic venous infarct with a sinus filling defect is cerebral venous thrombosis. Acute CVT should be treated with therapeutic heparin, including in patients with ICH at baseline (Level I). LMWH is preferred over UFH. DOACs are not recommended, especially in the acute phase. Thrombolysis is not recommended in low-risk patients. She also needs an antiepileptic drug and should stop the pill.

**Teaching point:** In CVT, haemorrhage is not a contraindication. Give therapeutic LMWH.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.5, Table 14.5.

### Q38
**Topic:** Continuing treatment after CVT

A 34-year-old man with left transverse sinus thrombosis has improved after 10 days of LMWH. No transient precipitant was found. Thrombophilia testing is pending. He asks how long he needs "blood thinners" and whether he can use "one of the new tablets".

**What is the most appropriate ongoing anticoagulation?**

A. DOAC for 6 weeks
B. Aspirin long term only
C. Lifelong warfarin therapy
D. Warfarin for 3-12 months

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** After CVT, a VKA for a variable period of 3-12 months is recommended to prevent recurrent CVT and other VTE. Permanent anticoagulation is reserved for recurrent venous thrombosis or a high-risk prothrombotic condition. DOACs are not recommended for CVT in the CPG, especially in the acute phase. Aspirin is not adequate venous prophylaxis.

**Teaching point:** After CVT: VKA for 3-12 months. Lifelong treatment only for recurrence or severe thrombophilia.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.5; Table 14.4.

## Section 5: Pregnancy, Older Persons and Prognosis

### Q39
**Topic:** Imaging a pregnant patient

A 31-year-old woman at 24 weeks' gestation develops sudden left face and arm weakness 2 hours ago. She is haemodynamically stable with BP 128/80 mmHg and has no proteinuria. The obstetric registrar asks which scan to request.

**What is the most appropriate imaging?**

A. Non-contrast MRI brain
B. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI
C. CT perfusion
D. Non-contrast CT brain

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** MRI brain without gadolinium is the modality of choice for stroke in pregnancy. Antenatal MRI has not been associated with fetal harm, but antenatal gadolinium exposure has been linked to stillbirth, neonatal death and skin conditions. CT involves radiation and is not the CPG modality of choice in pregnancy. CTP adds contrast as well.

**Teaching point:** Stroke in pregnancy: MRI without gadolinium.

**Reference:** CPG Section 15.2.

### Q40
**Topic:** Secondary prevention in pregnancy

A 33-year-old woman at 18 weeks' gestation has a small arterial ischaemic stroke. Work-up shows no cardiac source or thrombophilia, so she has a low-risk condition. Before pregnancy she took atorvastatin for familial hypercholesterolaemia.

**What is the most appropriate secondary prevention regimen?**

A. Aspirin; stop atorvastatin
B. Clopidogrel; continue atorvastatin
C. Aspirin; continue atorvastatin
D. Clopidogrel; stop atorvastatin

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In pregnant patients with a well-defined low-risk condition, aspirin (up to 150 mg daily) is the only antiplatelet that should be prescribed. In the second trimester, low-dose aspirin is the standard choice. Statins should be stopped before conception and throughout pregnancy because their safety has not been established. Clopidogrel is not permitted, and continuing the statin is inappropriate.

**Teaching point:** In pregnancy: aspirin is the only antiplatelet, and statins are stopped.

**Reference:** CPG Section 15.3, Table 15.1.

### Q41
**Topic:** Thrombolysis in the very old

An 88-year-old woman lives independently, walks with a stick and manages her own finances (Clinical Frailty Scale 4). She presents 2 hours after onset of aphasia and right hemiparesis (NIHSS 12). CT is normal and CTA shows no LVO. BP is 170/90 mmHg and bloods are normal. Her son asks whether she is "too old".

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. IV tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg
B. IV alteplase 0.6 mg/kg
C. IV alteplase 0.9 mg/kg
D. IV alteplase only if under 85

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Older patients benefit from thrombolysis at least as much as those under 80, so there is no upper age limit, particularly within 3 hours (Level I, Grade A). Frailty screening with a validated tool tailors care, and she is only mildly frail. The standard dose is 0.9 mg/kg. Low-dose alteplase was not equivalent for disability. Age alone justifies neither aspirin-only treatment nor palliation.

**Teaching point:** Age alone never excludes thrombolysis. Assess frailty and function, and use the standard dose.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.1; Section 12.2.1; Section 9.5.

### Q42
**Topic:** Prognosis by stroke subtype

A 70-year-old man has sudden weakness of the left face, arm and leg with no sensory, visual, language or cognitive deficit. He is orientated, can lift the left arm against gravity and walks with help on day 2. MRI shows a 1 cm infarct in the right internal capsule.

**Compared with other ischaemic stroke subtypes, which subtype does he have, and what is its mortality?**

A. Lacunar infarct, lowest mortality
B. Cardioembolic infarct, highest mortality
C. Large hemispheric infarct, high mortality
D. Brainstem infarct, high mortality

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** A pure motor syndrome with a small (under 1.5 cm) deep infarct in perforator territory is a lacunar infarct, which has the lowest mortality of all stroke types. Brainstem, large hemispheric and cardioembolic infarcts carry a poor prognosis. He also has several features that predict independent survival: age under 75, orientated verbal GCS, preserved arm power and the ability to walk.

**Teaching point:** Lacunar infarcts have the best survival. Predictors of independence are age under 75, orientation, arm power, walking and pre-stroke Barthel.

**Reference:** CPG Chapter 4 (Prognosis), Section 4.1; Section 14.2 (lacunar definition).
