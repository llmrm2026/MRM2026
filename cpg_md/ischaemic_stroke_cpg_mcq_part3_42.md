## Section 1: Primary Prevention

### Q1
**Topic:** Aspirin for primary prevention

A 62-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes for 10 years attends clinic. She has no history of stroke, TIA, coronary or peripheral arterial disease. BP is 128/76 mmHg on perindopril, LDL-C is 2.0 mmol/l on atorvastatin, and HbA1c is 6.9%. She asks whether she should start daily aspirin to prevent a stroke.

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Start aspirin 75 mg daily
B. Start aspirin 300 mg daily
C. Aspirin is not recommended for primary prevention in her case
D. Start clopidogrel 75 mg daily instead

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Daily aspirin is not recommended for primary prevention of stroke in diabetics, moderate-risk individuals or elderly patients, because the bleeding risk outweighs any benefit (Level I, Grade A). Clopidogrel has no primary-prevention role either. Her risk is better reduced by continuing BP, lipid and glycaemic control.

**Teaching point:** There is no aspirin for primary stroke prevention, even in diabetes.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.10 (Medical therapy).

### Q2
**Topic:** BP target in diabetes

A 55-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and no previous stroke has clinic BP readings averaging 142/86 mmHg on amlodipine. He tolerates his medication well.

**What target BP does the CPG recommend for primary stroke prevention in a person with diabetes?**

A. <150/90 mmHg
B. <140/90 mmHg
C. <135/85 mmHg
D. <130/80 mmHg, preferably <120 mmHg systolic if tolerated

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The target BP for diabetics is <130 mmHg systolic and <80 mmHg diastolic, preferably <120 mmHg systolic if tolerated (Level I, Grade A). Tight BP control in diabetics reduces stroke incidence. By contrast, in high-risk non-diabetic hypertensives, intensive lowering below 120 mmHg caused excess adverse events without reducing stroke.

**Teaching point:** For diabetics the target is <130/80 mmHg, and <120 mmHg systolic if tolerated.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.2 (Hypertension); Section 5.4.6.

### Q3
**Topic:** Hormone replacement therapy

A 54-year-old woman with troublesome menopausal symptoms has a strong family history of stroke. She asks whether combined oestrogen-progestogen HRT would also help protect her from stroke.

**What is the CPG position?**

A. HRT is recommended for primary stroke prevention in women with a family history
B. HRT may increase stroke risk and is not recommended for primary stroke prevention
C. Oestrogen alone is recommended for stroke prevention
D. HRT halves stroke risk if started within 5 years of menopause

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Post-menopausal hormone therapy may increase stroke risk and is not recommended for primary prevention (Level II, Grade B). The WHI oestrogen plus progestin study showed a 31% increase in stroke. HRT may still be considered for symptoms on its own merits, but not as stroke prevention.

**Teaching point:** HRT does not prevent stroke. Combined HRT increased stroke by about 31% in the WHI trial.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.5.

### Q4
**Topic:** Lifestyle prescription

A 47-year-old taxi driver with hypertension, BMI 29 kg/m2 and a sedentary lifestyle asks how much exercise he needs to reduce his stroke risk.

**Which recommendation matches the CPG?**

A. More than 30 minutes daily or 150 minutes per week
B. 20 minutes once a week
C. 60 minutes of vigorous exercise only on weekends
D. 10,000 steps per week

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG lifestyle recommendation is daily exercise of more than 30 minutes, or 150 minutes per week. It is combined with a diet rich in fruit and vegetables, no smoking, low to moderate alcohol and a BMI of 18.5-25 kg/m2. Increased physical activity is recommended for healthy and high-risk adults (Level I, Grade A).

**Teaching point:** Do at least 150 minutes of activity per week and aim for a BMI of 18.5-25 kg/m2.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.1 (Lifestyle changes); Section 5.4.9 (Physical activity).

### Q5
**Topic:** Diet for BP lowering

A 50-year-old woman with stage 1 hypertension wants to try dietary change before medication. She asks which eating pattern the CPG specifically recommends to reduce BP.

**Which diet is recommended at Level I, Grade A for lowering BP?**

A. Ketogenic diet
B. Mediterranean diet with nuts
C. Intermittent fasting
D. DASH diet

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The DASH diet, which is high in vegetables and fruit and low in saturated fat, is recommended to reduce BP (Level I, Grade A). The Mediterranean diet with olive oil or nuts is beneficial for stroke prevention, but the evidence level is lower (Level II, Grade B). Ketogenic diets and intermittent fasting are not CPG recommendations.

**Teaching point:** DASH lowers BP (Grade A). Mediterranean and fruit-and-vegetable diets reduce stroke (Grade B).

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.8 (Dietary factors).

### Q6
**Topic:** Asymptomatic carotid stenosis

A 68-year-old man has a carotid bruit noted at a health screening. Duplex shows 80% stenosis of the right ICA. He has never had a TIA or stroke. He is on a statin and his BP is controlled. The local vascular surgeon's audited stroke and death rate is 2%.

**What is the most appropriate statement?**

A. CEA is mandatory within 2 weeks
B. CEA is contraindicated in asymptomatic disease
C. Carotid stenting is the first-line option
D. CEA may be considered if surgical morbidity and mortality is <3%, after discussing risks

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** For high-grade (70-99%) asymptomatic stenosis, endarterectomy may be considered when the surgeon's morbidity and mortality is below 3% (Level I, Grade A). Selection should be careful, guided by comorbidity, life expectancy and preference, after discussing the risks. On medical therapy the annual stroke risk is about 2%, and CEA reduces it by about 1% per year. The 2-week urgency applies to symptomatic disease.

**Teaching point:** Asymptomatic 70-99% stenosis: CEA may be considered if the surgeon's complication rate is <3%. Symptomatic disease: <6%.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.1 (Primary prevention).

### Q7
**Topic:** Primary prevention in the very elderly

An 84-year-old woman who is independent and cognitively intact has newly diagnosed hypertension, with repeated readings of 168/88 mmHg. Her family worry that treatment is pointless at her age.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. Avoid treatment over 80 years
B. Treat the hypertension
C. Treat only if systolic BP is over 200 mmHg
D. Treat only if she has had a previous stroke

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Newly diagnosed hypertension in the very elderly (over 80 years) should be treated (Level I, Grade B). For frail, multimorbid or cognitively impaired older people the approach is individualised, but she is fit. Age alone is not a reason to withhold treatment.

**Teaching point:** Treat hypertension in the over-80s. Individualise targets for frailty.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.2; Section 12.3.1.

## Section 2: Secondary Prevention

### Q8
**Topic:** Aspirin allergy

A 59-year-old man with a lacunar infarct has documented aspirin-induced angioedema. He is in sinus rhythm with no carotid stenosis. His NIHSS is 5, so he does not meet the minor-stroke DAPT criteria.

**Which is the most appropriate long-term antithrombotic?**

A. Warfarin, INR 2-3
B. Clopidogrel 75 mg daily
C. Aspirin desensitisation, then aspirin 300 mg
D. No antithrombotic

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Alternative antiplatelets can be used for aspirin allergy, failure, intolerance or contraindication. Clopidogrel 75 mg daily is slightly superior to aspirin 325 mg. Other options include ticlopidine 250 mg twice daily, triflusal 600 mg daily and cilostazol 100 mg twice daily. Warfarin is not indicated in sinus rhythm without a cardioembolic source. Small vessel disease needs long-term antiplatelet therapy.

**Teaching point:** If aspirin is not possible, use clopidogrel 75 mg (or cilostazol 100 mg twice daily, triflusal 600 mg daily, or ticlopidine 250 mg twice daily). Warfarin has no role in sinus rhythm.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1 (Anti-platelet therapy).

### Q9
**Topic:** Warfarin in sinus rhythm

A 64-year-old woman has a second non-cardioembolic ischaemic stroke while taking aspirin. ECG and a 72-hour Holter show sinus rhythm. Echocardiography is normal. Her son asks whether she should be switched to warfarin because aspirin has "failed".

**What is the most appropriate advice?**

A. Switch to warfarin, INR 2-3
B. Add warfarin to aspirin
C. Warfarin is not indicated; review the stroke mechanism and choose antiplatelet therapy
D. Switch to rivaroxaban

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Warfarin is not indicated for secondary prevention in patients in sinus rhythm without other conditions that predispose to cardioembolism (Level I, Grade A). Recurrence on aspirin should prompt a review of the mechanism, risk factors and adherence, and a change to an alternative antiplatelet if appropriate. DOACs have no role in non-cardioembolic stroke. Rivaroxaban was not better than aspirin even in ESUS.

**Teaching point:** "Aspirin failure" in sinus rhythm is not an indication for anticoagulation.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1.

### Q10
**Topic:** BP lowering after stroke in normotensives

A 61-year-old man is reviewed 3 weeks after a minor ischaemic stroke. His BP is 128/80 mmHg off treatment. He asks why he needs a BP tablet if his BP is normal.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. Start an ACE inhibitor-based regimen, as it reduces recurrence even in normotensives
B. BP treatment only if systolic is ≥140 mmHg
C. Beta-blocker monotherapy
D. No treatment until a second stroke

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** After the acute phase (2 weeks or more after stroke), ACE inhibitor-based therapy reduces recurrent stroke in both normotensive and hypertensive patients (Level I, Grade A). An ACE inhibitor with a thiazide was effective when started 2 weeks after the event. ARB-based therapy is effective in selected high-risk groups. BP lowering cuts stroke risk by about 30-40%.

**Teaching point:** From 2 weeks after stroke, offer ACE inhibitor-based therapy even if BP is normal.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.2 (Anti-hypertensive treatment).

### Q11
**Topic:** Aspirin dose

A 70-year-old woman is started on aspirin after an atherothrombotic stroke. The pharmacist queries the dose range.

**What dose range of aspirin does the CPG recommend after stroke?**

A. 10-50 mg daily
B. 75-325 mg daily
C. 500-1000 mg daily
D. 300 mg twice daily long term

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The recommended dose of oral aspirin after stroke is 75-325 mg daily (Level I, Grade A). Trials comparing doses found no difference in recurrence. Aspirin given within 48 hours reduces recurrent stroke and death, with an overall risk reduction of about 25%.

**Teaching point:** Aspirin 75-325 mg daily. Higher doses add no benefit.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1.

### Q12
**Topic:** Ticagrelor as alternative

A 58-year-old man with a minor non-cardioembolic stroke is intolerant of aspirin and also had a severe rash with clopidogrel. The team looks for another antiplatelet supported by Asian sub-group data.

**Which statement about ticagrelor is consistent with the CPG?**

A. It is superior to aspirin in all patients
B. It is contraindicated after stroke
C. It must always be combined with warfarin
D. It may be a reasonable alternative when aspirin is contraindicated

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** In SOCRATES, ticagrelor was not superior to aspirin overall, but the Asian sub-analysis showed a trend towards benefit and there was no significant safety difference. The CPG therefore says ticagrelor may be a reasonable alternative when aspirin is contraindicated (Level II, Grade B). Ticagrelor plus aspirin (in THALES) cut 30-day stroke or death in NIHSS ≤5 strokes and TIA, at the cost of more severe bleeding.

**Teaching point:** Ticagrelor is an option when aspirin cannot be used, but it is not superior to aspirin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5.1 (Ticagrelor).

### Q13
**Topic:** Symptomatic moderate carotid stenosis

A 66-year-old woman had a right hemispheric TIA 5 weeks ago with full recovery. Carotid duplex and CTA show 60% stenosis of the left ICA. The local centre's audited perioperative stroke and death rate is 4%.

**What does the CPG say about carotid endarterectomy for her?**

A. CEA may be indicated (Level III, Grade C)
B. CEA is not recommended, as stenosis is under 70%
C. CEA must be done within 48 hours
D. EC-IC bypass is preferred

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The trap is laterality. Her TIA was right hemispheric, but the 60% stenosis is in the left ICA, which does not supply the symptomatic territory. That makes the stenosis asymptomatic for decision purposes. The CPG supports CEA for asymptomatic stenosis only at 70-99%, so CEA is not recommended at 60%. Had the stenosis been ipsilateral, CEA for 50-69% symptomatic disease within 180 days in a centre with <6% risk "may be indicated" (Level III). EC-IC bypass is not recommended.

**Teaching point:** Always match the stenosis to the symptomatic hemisphere before applying the CEA thresholds.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.1 and 5.8.2 (Revascularisation procedures).

### Q14
**Topic:** Mild stenosis after TIA

A 71-year-old man had a left hemispheric TIA 10 days ago. Duplex shows 40% stenosis of the left ICA. He asks about surgery.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. Urgent CEA within 2 weeks
B. Carotid stenting
C. EC-IC bypass
D. Best medical therapy; CEA not recommended for stenosis under 50%

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** CEA is not recommended for carotid stenosis under 50% (Level I, Grade A). Management is medical: antiplatelet therapy (DAPT if he meets high-risk TIA criteria), statin to LDL-C 1.8 mmol/l, BP control and lifestyle change. Stenting is an alternative only where intervention is indicated but surgery is unsuitable. EC-IC bypass is not recommended.

**Teaching point:** For symptomatic stenosis: <50% medical therapy, 50-69% CEA may be indicated, 70-99% CEA (ideally within 2 weeks).

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.2.

### Q15
**Topic:** Carotid stenting as alternative

A 62-year-old man had radical neck dissection and radiotherapy for laryngeal cancer 6 years ago. He now has a left hemispheric TIA with 85% left ICA stenosis. The vascular surgeon considers his neck hostile for surgery.

**What does the CPG suggest?**

A. Carotid artery stenting as an alternative
B. CEA regardless of neck anatomy
C. No revascularisation because of radiotherapy
D. EC-IC bypass

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Carotid stenting is a feasible alternative to CEA for secondary prevention when surgery is undesirable, technically difficult or inaccessible, such as a hostile neck after radiation or dissection. Over 4 years, CAS and CEA gave similar death, stroke and MI outcomes, although periprocedural stroke was higher with CAS. Complex arch anatomy and ICA tortuosity raise CAS risk. EC-IC bypass is not recommended.

**Teaching point:** Use stenting when CEA is technically difficult or unsuitable. Arch complexity and tortuosity increase stenting risk.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.9 (Angioplasty or stenting).

### Q16
**Topic:** Antithrombotics around CEA

A 69-year-old woman is listed for left CEA 8 days after a TIA with 80% stenosis. She is on aspirin and atorvastatin. The surgical team asks whether aspirin should be stopped 7 days before surgery.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. Stop aspirin 7 days before surgery
B. Stop aspirin and bridge with LMWH
C. Continue antithrombotic therapy before and after surgery
D. Switch to warfarin before surgery

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Patients should stay on antithrombotic therapy before and after carotid surgery (Level II-2, Grade B). Stopping aspirin exposes her to recurrent stroke in the highest-risk early period. Bridging and warfarin are not indicated.

**Teaching point:** Do not stop antiplatelets for CEA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.8.2.

### Q17
**Topic:** Risk of recurrent vascular events

A 60-year-old man asks what his ongoing risk is after an ischaemic stroke with good recovery.

**According to the CPG, what is the approximate annual risk of recurrent stroke after stroke or TIA?**

A. About 0.5% per year
B. About 5% per year
C. About 15% per year
D. About 25% per year

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The risk of recurrent vascular events after stroke or TIA is about 5% per year for stroke, 3% per year for MI, and 7% per year for any of stroke, MI or vascular death. It is higher where the cerebrovascular atherosclerotic burden is high or there is ipsilateral high-grade (≥70%) carotid stenosis. In the early period, recurrence is 3-4% in the first month and 12% in the first year.

**Teaching point:** After stroke, recurrence runs at about 5% per year, with 3-4% at 1 month and about 12% in the first year.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.5; Chapter 4 (Prognosis).

## Section 3: Atrial Fibrillation and Cardioembolism

### Q18
**Topic:** Aspirin in NVAF

A 76-year-old man with permanent non-valvular AF, hypertension and diabetes (CHA2DS2-VASc 4) has been on aspirin 100 mg for 3 years "because warfarin is dangerous". His eGFR is 62 ml/min/1.73 m2 and he has no bleeding history.

**What is the most appropriate change to his stroke prevention?**

A. Add clopidogrel to aspirin
B. Stop aspirin and start a DOAC
C. Increase aspirin to 300 mg
D. Continue aspirin alone

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Antiplatelet monotherapy is not indicated for stroke prevention in NVAF (Level I, Grade A). OAC is superior to aspirin or no treatment, and is recommended for men with CHA2DS2-VASc ≥2. DOACs are preferred over VKA. Aspirin plus clopidogrel is still inferior to OAC and bleeds more. Raising the aspirin dose adds bleeding without protection.

**Teaching point:** Aspirin is not stroke prevention in AF. Use OAC, preferably a DOAC.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.2; Key Recommendations 4 and 6 (Chapter 13).

### Q19
**Topic:** Valvular AF

A 48-year-old woman with rheumatic heart disease has AF and moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis (valve area 1.1 cm2). She had a left MCA TIA last week. She asks whether she can take apixaban, like her neighbour.

**Which anticoagulant is indicated?**

A. Apixaban
B. Dabigatran
C. Warfarin
D. Aspirin plus clopidogrel

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For valvular AF (moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis) and mechanical heart valves, the OAC of choice is a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) (Level I, Grade A). DOACs are preferred only for non-valvular AF. Antiplatelets are not adequate prevention. The usual target INR is 2.5 (range 2.0-3.0).

**Teaching point:** Use warfarin, not a DOAC, for AF with moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis or a mechanical valve.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.2.

### Q20
**Topic:** Timing of DOAC after AF stroke (moderate)

A 72-year-old woman with newly found AF has a left MCA infarct (NIHSS 11 on admission). Repeat CT on day 2 shows no haemorrhagic transformation. She is stable.

**Using the 1-3-6-12 day rule, when should her DOAC start?**

A. Day 1
B. Day 3
C. Day 6
D. Day 12

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG's 1-3-6-12 day rule (after haemorrhagic transformation has been excluded) is: TIA, day 1; mild stroke (NIHSS <8), day 3; moderate stroke (NIHSS 8-15), day 6; severe stroke (NIHSS ≥16), day 12. With NIHSS 11 she is moderate, so day 6. Aspirin can be considered until OAC starts.

**Teaching point:** 1-3-6-12: TIA day 1, NIHSS <8 day 3, NIHSS 8-15 day 6, NIHSS ≥16 day 12.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3 (1-3-6-12 day rule).

### Q21
**Topic:** Timing of DOAC after AF stroke (severe)

A 79-year-old man with known AF (anticoagulation stopped previously because of non-adherence) presents with a large right MCA infarct (NIHSS 18). There is no haemorrhagic transformation on day-3 CT.

**When should his DOAC be started?**

A. Day 1
B. Day 3
C. Day 6
D. Day 12

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** NIHSS ≥16 is a severe stroke, so a DOAC starts on day 12 under the 1-3-6-12 rule, once haemorrhagic transformation is excluded. Urgent anticoagulation is specifically not recommended for moderate-to-large infarcts because of the high risk of intracranial bleeding.

**Teaching point:** Severe AF stroke (NIHSS ≥16): wait about 12 days before starting OAC.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3; Table 5.5.

### Q22
**Topic:** Heparin bridging after cardioembolic stroke

A 68-year-old woman with AF has a moderate cardioembolic stroke. The house officer suggests full-dose subcutaneous enoxaparin from day 1 to "cover her" until the DOAC starts.

**What is the CPG position on parenteral anticoagulation here?**

A. Not recommended to prevent secondary stroke
B. Recommended from day 1
C. Recommended only for NIHSS above 15
D. Recommended with aspirin

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** After a cardioembolic stroke, parenteral anticoagulation (heparin or LMWH) is not recommended to prevent secondary stroke (Level I, Grade A). Urgent routine anticoagulation does not improve neurological outcome or prevent early recurrence, and it raises the bleeding risk. Aspirin can be considered before OAC starts.

**Teaching point:** Do not bridge with heparin after an AF stroke. Use aspirin until the DOAC start day.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3; Table 5.5.

### Q23
**Topic:** OAC plus antiplatelet

A 74-year-old man with NVAF and stable coronary disease (PCI 4 years ago, no events since) is starting apixaban after a TIA. He is already on aspirin and asks whether he should continue both.

**What does the CPG highlight about this combination?**

A. It is mandatory for all patients with AF and CAD
B. It reduces bleeding compared with OAC alone
C. Bleeding risk is high with OAC plus antiplatelet, so the combination should be avoided unless specifically indicated
D. Aspirin should replace apixaban

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG notes that bleeding risk is high after starting OAC with an antiplatelet for secondary stroke prevention. With stable CAD more than a year after PCI and no recent events, OAC alone is usually adequate. Aspirin is not a substitute for OAC in AF.

**Teaching point:** OAC plus antiplatelet means more bleeding. Reserve it for clear indications such as recent ACS or a stent.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3.

### Q24
**Topic:** Restarting OAC after ICH

A 70-year-old woman with NVAF (CHA2DS2-VASc 5) had a warfarin-related lobar ICH 3 weeks ago. Her INR was 4.8 at the time because of an antibiotic interaction. BP is now controlled. She is recovering well.

**When could oral anticoagulation be restarted, according to the CPG?**

A. Never
B. After 48 hours
C. After 4-8 weeks, if the cause and risk factors are treated
D. After 12 months

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** After intracranial haemorrhage, OAC could be restarted after 4-8 weeks in an NVAF patient with a high CHA2DS2-VASc score if the underlying cause and bleeding risk factors have been treated (Level II, Grade B). Her bleed had a correctable cause (supratherapeutic INR from an interaction) and her BP is controlled. A DOAC is preferred.

**Teaching point:** Restart OAC 4-8 weeks after ICH in high-risk AF once the cause is corrected.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.3.

### Q25
**Topic:** Warfarin control

A 66-year-old man with a mechanical mitral valve is on warfarin. His INR log over 6 months shows that 52% of readings were in range.

**What target for time in therapeutic range (TTR) does the CPG set?**

A. >50%
B. >60%
C. >70%
D. >90%

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Patients on VKA should have regular INR monitoring with a TTR above 70%. At 52% his control is inadequate. Causes such as adherence, interactions, diet and alcohol should be addressed. A DOAC cannot replace warfarin here because of the mechanical valve.

**Teaching point:** The warfarin TTR target is >70%. Mechanical valves stay on warfarin.

**Reference:** CPG Key Recommendation 5 (Chapter 13).

### Q26
**Topic:** Reversal of dabigatran

A 77-year-old woman on dabigatran presents with a large ICH. Her last dose was 3 hours ago. aPTT is prolonged.

**What is the specific reversal agent and dose?**

A. Vitamin K 10 mg IV
B. Protamine 50 mg IV
C. Andexanet alfa
D. Idarucizumab 5 g IV (two infusions of 2.5 g)

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Dabigatran is reversed specifically by idarucizumab 5 g IV, given as two 2.5 g infusions. Andexanet alfa is for factor Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban), and was not available when the CPG was written. Vitamin K reverses warfarin and protamine reverses heparin. General measures include activated charcoal if ingestion was within 2 hours, and haemodialysis (for dabigatran only).

**Teaching point:** Dabigatran → idarucizumab 5 g; Xa inhibitors → andexanet; warfarin → vitamin K + PCC.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.4 (General measures for DOACs in bleeding).

### Q27
**Topic:** Detecting paroxysmal AF

A 67-year-old man has a right parietal cortical infarct. Admission ECG shows sinus rhythm. CTA shows no significant carotid or intracranial stenosis.

**Which investigation does the CPG specify to detect paroxysmal AF?**

A. 72-hour Holter monitor
B. Exercise treadmill test
C. Repeat 12-lead ECG at discharge only
D. Coronary angiography

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** All stroke and TIA patients need a 12-lead ECG, and a 72-hour Holter is required to detect paroxysmal AF. Patients under 45 and those with no apparent cause on baseline tests also need a transthoracic echo, with TOE if suspicion of cardioembolism remains. An embolic-looking cortical infarct with clean vessels makes occult AF a key concern.

**Teaching point:** After stroke: 12-lead ECG for all, then 72-hour Holter for paroxysmal AF.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.1.

### Q28
**Topic:** TOE vs TTE

A 44-year-old woman has a cryptogenic left MCA branch infarct. Her TTE is normal. The team still suspects a cardiac source.

**For which finding is TOE superior to TTE, according to the CPG?**

A. LV ejection fraction
B. Left atrial appendage thrombus
C. Pericardial effusion
D. LV hypertrophy

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** TOE is superior to TTE for detecting thrombi in the left atrium and left atrial appendage, PFO, atrial septal aneurysm and aortic arch atheroma. LV function, hypertrophy and effusion are adequately assessed by TTE.

**Teaching point:** Request TOE for LA/LAA thrombus, PFO, atrial septal aneurysm and arch atheroma.

**Reference:** CPG Section 13.1.

### Q29
**Topic:** Anterior MI with LV thrombus

A 57-year-old man has an embolic stroke 3 weeks after a large anterior STEMI. Echo shows apical akinesia with a mobile LV thrombus. He is in sinus rhythm.

**What anticoagulation does the CPG suggest?**

A. Aspirin alone
B. DAPT for 21 days
C. No anticoagulation
D. Warfarin for 6-12 months

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** For MI with LV thrombus, the CPG suggests considering warfarin for 6-12 months (target INR 2.5, range 2.0-3.0). With high-risk features but no thrombus, warfarin for 3-6 months followed by aspirin is suggested. Dilated cardiomyopathies (including peripartum) may need lifelong warfarin. Antiplatelets alone do not treat LV thrombus.

**Teaching point:** LV thrombus after MI: warfarin for 6-12 months. No thrombus but high risk: 3-6 months.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.7, Table 5.4 (Cardiac conditions predisposing to ischaemic stroke).

## Section 4: Stroke in the Young and Special Circumstances

### Q30
**Topic:** ESUS

A 49-year-old man has a non-lacunar cortical infarct. CTA shows no stenosis over 50%. Echo, ECG and a 72-hour Holter show no major cardiac source. No other cause is found.

**What is the most appropriate long-term management?**

A. Long-term antiplatelet with prolonged ambulatory ECG monitoring
B. Rivaroxaban, as it is superior to aspirin in ESUS
C. Warfarin, INR 2-3
D. No antithrombotic

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** He fits ESUS: a non-lacunar infarct, no ≥50% stenosis in the supplying artery, no major-risk cardiac source and no other cause. Management is long-term antiplatelet therapy. There is no evidence for anticoagulants: rivaroxaban was not superior to aspirin and caused more bleeding. Prolonged monitoring matters because occult AF is found in about 25% of ESUS.

**Teaching point:** ESUS: antiplatelet therapy, not empirical anticoagulation. Look hard for occult AF.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.2 (ESUS); Table 14.3.

### Q31
**Topic:** PFO closure

A 38-year-old woman has a cortical infarct. Full work-up including vascular imaging, 72-hour Holter, thrombophilia screen and leg Doppler is unremarkable. TOE shows a PFO with an atrial septal aneurysm and a large right-to-left shunt.

**What is the most appropriate secondary prevention?**

A. Warfarin, INR 2-3, lifelong
B. PFO closure plus antiplatelet therapy
C. Rivaroxaban
D. No treatment, as PFO is common in the general population

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** PFO closure has moderate benefit in young and middle-aged patients with cryptogenic stroke, and closure combined with antiplatelet therapy is recommended (Level I). An associated atrial septal aneurysm or large shunt further favours closure, although closure is associated with more AF. Anticoagulation is reserved for patients who also have a venous source of embolism.

**Teaching point:** Cryptogenic stroke with PFO in a younger patient: closure plus antiplatelet therapy.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.3 (PFO).

### Q32
**Topic:** PFO with DVT

A 41-year-old man who recently flew long-haul has a right MCA infarct. TOE shows a PFO with right-to-left shunt. Lower limb ultrasound shows a left popliteal DVT.

**What is the most appropriate antithrombotic approach?**

A. Antiplatelet alone
B. Urgent PFO closure, then aspirin alone
C. Anticoagulation, timed according to stroke characteristics
D. IVC filter as first-line

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** For stroke or TIA patients with both a PFO and a venous source of embolism, anticoagulation is indicated, depending on the characteristics of the stroke (Level I). The timing depends on infarct size and the risk of haemorrhagic transformation. An IVC filter is considered only when anticoagulation is contraindicated. PFO closure may be considered later depending on the risk of recurrent DVT.

**Teaching point:** PFO plus DVT means paradoxical embolism: anticoagulate. Use an IVC filter only if anticoagulation is contraindicated.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.3.

### Q33
**Topic:** Cervical artery dissection

A 36-year-old woman develops left neck pain and a left Horner's syndrome after a chiropractic manipulation. Two days later she has right hand weakness. MRI shows a small left MCA infarct and an intramural haematoma of the left ICA.

**What is the management approach for arterial dissection listed in the CPG?**

A. Antiplatelet treatment, with endovascular treatment in selected cases
B. Long-term warfarin for all
C. Immunosuppression
D. Supportive care only

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Arterial dissection, whether spontaneous or after trauma, is a leading cause of stroke in young adults. The CPG's Table 14.1 lists its management as antiplatelet treatment, with endovascular treatment where needed. Immunosuppression is for primary vasculitis, and supportive care alone is listed for illicit-drug-related stroke.

**Teaching point:** Dissection: antiplatelet therapy, with endovascular treatment for selected cases.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.2, Table 14.1.

### Q34
**Topic:** CADASIL

A 42-year-old man has migraine with aura, recurrent subcortical lacunar infarcts and early cognitive decline. His mother developed dementia in her 50s. MRI shows confluent white matter hyperintensities involving the anterior temporal poles.

**Which test is most likely to confirm the diagnosis?**

A. Plasma α-galactosidase A activity
B. Serum and CSF lactate/pyruvate ratio
C. NOTCH3 mutation analysis
D. MTHFR gene polymorphism

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Migraine, recurrent small-vessel strokes, early dementia, an autosomal dominant family history and anterior temporal white matter changes point to CADASIL, which is caused by NOTCH3 mutations. α-Gal activity tests for Fabry disease. Lactate and pyruvate test for MELAS. MTHFR relates to hyperhomocysteinaemia. Management of small vessel disease is long-term antiplatelet therapy.

**Teaching point:** CADASIL is diagnosed by NOTCH3 testing. Fabry by α-gal, MELAS by lactate, and RVCL by TREX1.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.1; Section 14.4 (Investigation of young stroke).

### Q35
**Topic:** Sickle cell disease

A 19-year-old man with sickle cell anaemia has an ischaemic stroke. He recovers well. The haematologist asks about long-term prevention.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. Lifelong warfarin
B. Aspirin alone
C. Hydroxocobalamin injections
D. Long-term transfusion to keep HbS below 30% of total haemoglobin

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** For sickle cell disease with a previous ischaemic stroke or TIA, long-term blood transfusion to reduce HbS to under 30% of total haemoglobin is recommended (Level I, Grade B). Anticoagulation has no role, and B12 is irrelevant.

### Q36
**Topic:** Hyperhomocysteinaemia

A 51-year-old woman has a lacunar stroke. Fasting homocysteine is moderately raised. She asks whether vitamins will prevent another stroke.

**What does the CPG state about folate, B6 and B12 supplements?**

A. They lower homocysteine but have not been shown to prevent stroke
B. They prevent recurrent stroke and replace aspirin
C. They are contraindicated after stroke
D. They are only effective given intravenously

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In adults with recent stroke or TIA and mild to moderate hyperhomocysteinaemia, folate, B6 and B12 safely lower homocysteine but have not been shown to prevent stroke (Level III, Grade B). VITATOPS showed no overall secondary-prevention benefit. She still needs antiplatelet therapy, statin and BP control.

**Teaching point:** B vitamins fix the homocysteine level but do not prevent stroke. Keep standard secondary prevention.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.3; Table 16.1.

### Q37
**Topic:** Acute cerebral venous thrombosis

A 29-year-old woman on the combined pill has 5 days of worsening headache, then a focal seizure and right leg weakness. CT shows a small left parasagittal haemorrhagic venous infarct. CT venography shows superior sagittal sinus thrombosis.

**What is the most appropriate initial treatment?**

A. Avoid anticoagulation because of the haemorrhage
B. Therapeutic LMWH despite the haemorrhage
C. Dabigatran
D. Systemic thrombolysis

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Adults with acute CVT should be treated with therapeutic heparin, including those with ICH at baseline (Level I, Grade B). LMWH is preferred to UFH unless fast reversal is needed or LMWH is contraindicated. DOACs are not recommended, especially in the acute phase. Thrombolysis is not recommended for low-risk patients. She also needs an AED for a supratentorial lesion with seizures, and should stop the pill.

**Teaching point:** In CVT, a haemorrhagic venous infarct is not a contraindication. Give therapeutic LMWH.

**Reference:** CPG Section 14.5, Table 14.5.

### Q38
**Topic:** Long-term treatment after CVT

A 34-year-old man with transverse sinus thrombosis has improved on LMWH. Thrombophilia testing is pending. No permanent risk factor has been found so far.

**What long-term anticoagulation does the CPG recommend?**

A. Lifelong warfarin
B. DOAC for 6 weeks
C. Aspirin only
D. VKA for 3-12 months

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Oral anticoagulation with a VKA for a variable period (3-12 months) after CVT is recommended to prevent recurrent CVT and other VTE. Permanent anticoagulation is for recurrent venous thrombosis or a high-risk prothrombotic condition. DOACs are not recommended in the CPG, particularly in the acute phase. Routine thrombophilia screening does not improve outcomes, but it may be done when pre-test probability is high.

**Teaching point:** After CVT: VKA for 3-12 months. Lifelong treatment only for recurrence or severe thrombophilia.

**Reference:** CPG Table 14.5; Table 14.4.

## Section 5: Pregnancy, Older Persons and Prognosis

### Q39
**Topic:** Imaging in pregnancy

A 31-year-old woman at 24 weeks' gestation develops sudden left arm and face weakness. She is haemodynamically stable. The obstetric registrar asks for the preferred imaging.

**Which imaging modality does the CPG recommend for investigating stroke in pregnancy?**

A. MRI brain with gadolinium
B. MRI brain without gadolinium
C. Contrast-enhanced CT with CTP
D. Cerebral DSA

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** MRI brain without gadolinium contrast is the modality of choice for stroke in pregnancy. Antenatal MRI is not associated with fetal harm, but antenatal gadolinium exposure was linked to stillbirth, neonatal death and skin conditions in one study. MRI should only be used when it answers the clinical question and benefits the mother.

**Teaching point:** Stroke in pregnancy: MRI without gadolinium.

**Reference:** CPG Section 15.2.

### Q40
**Topic:** Antiplatelet in pregnancy

A 33-year-old woman at 18 weeks' gestation has a small arterial ischaemic stroke with no high-risk cardiac or thrombophilic condition. The team chooses antiplatelet prevention.

**Which antiplatelet does the CPG permit?**

A. Aspirin up to 150 mg daily
B. Clopidogrel 75 mg daily
C. Ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily
D. Cilostazol 100 mg twice daily

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Aspirin up to 150 mg daily is well tolerated in pregnancy. In patients with well-defined low-risk conditions, no antiplatelet other than aspirin should be prescribed. Low-risk patients may instead have UFH or LMWH in the first trimester, then low-dose aspirin in the second and third trimesters. Aspirin can be continued through induced labour. Statins should be stopped before conception and throughout pregnancy.

**Teaching point:** Aspirin is the only antiplatelet in pregnancy, at up to 150 mg daily.

**Reference:** CPG Section 15.3, Table 15.1.

### Q41
**Topic:** Frailty and thrombolysis

An 88-year-old woman who lives independently and walks with a stick (Clinical Frailty Scale 4) presents 2 hours after onset of aphasia and right hemiparesis (NIHSS 12). CT is normal. Her son asks whether she is "too old" for clot-busting treatment.

**What is the most appropriate answer?**

A. IVT is contraindicated over 85
B. IVT is only for patients under 80
C. There is no upper age limit; older people benefit if they meet the criteria
D. Only half-dose alteplase is allowed over 80

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Older patients benefit from thrombolysis at least as much as those under 80, so there is no upper age limit, particularly within the first 3 hours. An older person should receive and can benefit from IVT (Level I, Grade A). All older people should be screened for frailty with a validated tool (for example the Clinical Frailty Scale) to tailor care, but age alone does not exclude anyone. The dose stays at 0.9 mg/kg.

**Teaching point:** Age alone never excludes reperfusion. Assess frailty and function instead.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.1; Section 12.2.1.

### Q42
**Topic:** Predictors of independent survival

A 70-year-old man is assessed 2 days after a stroke. He is orientated and was independent before (Barthel 20/20). He can lift his affected arm against gravity and can walk with help.

**Which set of variables does the CPG describe as predicting independent survival at 3 and 12 months?**

A. Age <75, verbal GCS orientated, arm power, ability to walk and pre-stroke Barthel ≥12
B. Sex, smoking, cholesterol, BMI and alcohol
C. NIHSS, infarct volume, glucose, BP and atrial fibrillation
D. HbA1c, eGFR, LDL-C, troponin and CRP

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG lists five variables that predict independent survival at 3 and 12 months: age <75, orientated verbal GCS, arm power, ability to walk, and pre-stroke independence (Barthel ≥12/20). The other options are risk factors or acute markers, not this validated set. Lacunar infarcts carry the lowest mortality. Brainstem, large hemispheric and cardioembolic strokes carry a poor prognosis.

**Teaching point:** The five predictors are age, verbal GCS, arm power, walking and pre-stroke Barthel.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.1 (Survival after stroke).
