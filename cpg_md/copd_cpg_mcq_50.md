# Management of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (2nd Edition, 2009): 50 MCQs

**Source:** Malaysian Thoracic Society / Ministry of Health Malaysia / Academy of Medicine Malaysia, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease*, Second Edition (2009).
**Format:** 50 single-best-answer questions, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style with layered reasoning. Each question has the key, an explanation of the key and the distractors, a teaching point and a CPG reference.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from this CPG, which predates later GOLD revisions; answers reflect the CPG as written. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

---

## Section 1: Diagnosis and Severity

### Q1
**Topic:** Confirming the diagnosis

A 58-year-old man who has smoked 25 cigarettes a day for 35 years has a 2-year history of morning cough with sputum and exertional breathlessness. Spirometry after 400 µg salbutamol shows FEV1 2.1 L (68% predicted) and FVC 3.4 L.

**Which finding confirms COPD according to the CPG?**

A. FEV1 below 80% predicted alone
B. A post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.70
C. A pre-bronchodilator peak flow below 80% predicted
D. Chronic cough with a smoking history alone

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The diagnosis should be confirmed by spirometry showing a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio of less than 0.70. His ratio is 2.1/3.4 = 0.62. A reduced FEV1 alone (A) grades severity but does not show obstruction. Peak flow (C) has poor specificity. Symptoms and exposure (D) make COPD likely but need spirometric confirmation.

**Teaching point:** COPD = post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70; FEV1 % predicted then grades severity.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 1.3 and 4.1; Recommendations: Diagnosis and Assessment.

### Q2
**Topic:** Spirometric staging

A 66-year-old woman with COPD has a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC of 0.55 and FEV1 of 42% predicted. Arterial blood gases on air are PaO2 72 mmHg and PaCO2 41 mmHg. She has no oedema.

**Which COPD stage is she in?**

A. Stage II (moderate)
B. Stage IV (very severe)
C. Stage III (severe)
D. Stage I (mild)

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Table 1-1 grades stage I as FEV1 ≥80%, stage II as 50-80%, stage III as 30-50% and stage IV as FEV1 <30%, or <50% plus chronic respiratory failure (PaO2 <60 mmHg with or without PaCO2 >50 mmHg). Her FEV1 of 42% with normal gases is stage III. B would need respiratory failure or cor pulmonale.

**Teaching point:** FEV1 30-50% is stage III, unless chronic respiratory failure upgrades it to stage IV.

**Reference:** CPG Section 1.3, Table 1-1.

### Q3
**Topic:** Chronic respiratory failure and staging

A 70-year-old man has FEV1 46% predicted and ankle oedema with a raised JVP. ABG on air shows PaO2 54 mmHg and PaCO2 52 mmHg.

**How should his COPD be staged?**

A. Stage III, because FEV1 is between 30% and 50%
B. Stage II, because FEV1 is above 40%
C. Unstageable until FEV1 falls below 30%
D. Stage IV, because FEV1 is below 50% with chronic respiratory failure and cor pulmonale

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Stage IV (very severe) is FEV1 <30% predicted, or FEV1 <50% predicted plus chronic respiratory failure. Respiratory failure is PaO2 <60 mmHg with or without PaCO2 >50 mmHg. His gases and signs of right heart failure place him in stage IV even though his FEV1 alone suggests stage III, which makes A the trap.

**Teaching point:** Respiratory failure or right heart failure moves FEV1 <50% into stage IV.

**Reference:** CPG Section 1.3, Tables 1-1 and 1-3.

### Q4
**Topic:** MMRC dyspnoea scale

A 63-year-old woman with COPD reports that she has to stop for breath after walking about 100 metres on the level. She can dress herself without difficulty.

**What is her Modified Medical Research Council (MMRC) dyspnoea grade?**

A. Grade 1
B. Grade 2
C. Grade 3
D. Grade 4

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** MMRC grade 3 is stopping for breath after about 100 m or a few minutes on the level. Grade 1 is breathlessness when hurrying or on a slight hill, grade 2 is walking slower than peers or stopping at own pace, and grade 4 is being too breathless to leave the house or to dress.

**Teaching point:** MMRC 3 = stops after 100 m; MMRC 4 = breathless dressing or housebound.

**Reference:** CPG Section 1.4, Table 1-2.

### Q5
**Topic:** When FEV1 and symptoms disagree

A 61-year-old man has a post-bronchodilator FEV1 of 62% predicted, but he is too breathless to leave the house (MMRC 4). Echocardiography and haemoglobin are normal.

**How should his severity be classified for treatment decisions, according to the CPG?**

A. By symptoms, which places him in the severe category
B. By FEV1 alone, as moderate
C. By the average of the two
D. Severity cannot be assigned until FEV1 is repeated

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Table 1-3 classifies severity by both spirometry and symptoms, and states that when FEV1 and symptoms disagree, symptoms should be followed. MMRC 4 corresponds to the severe category. Management should not be based solely on spirometry, so B is wrong. C and D have no basis.

**Teaching point:** If FEV1 and symptoms disagree, follow the symptoms.

**Reference:** CPG Section 1.4, Table 1-3.

### Q6
**Topic:** Predicting survival

A respiratory registrar is counselling a 68-year-old man with COPD about prognosis.

**Which measure does the CPG state predicts survival better than FEV1?**

A. Peak expiratory flow rate
B. Chest radiograph hyperinflation score
C. Serum alpha-1 antitrypsin level
D. The BODE index (BMI, airflow obstruction, dyspnoea and exercise capacity)

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The multidimensional BODE index better predicts survival in COPD patients than FEV1. The locally developed SAFE index is another multidimensional tool. Peak flow (A) correlates poorly with FEV1 in COPD. B and C are not prognostic indices in the CPG.

**Teaching point:** BODE (BMI, Obstruction, Dyspnoea, Exercise) outperforms FEV1 for survival.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 1.4 and 4.7.

### Q7
**Topic:** Bronchodilator reversibility

A 52-year-old woman with a 15 pack-year history has FEV1 1.60 L before and 2.10 L after salbutamol. She has a history of childhood eczema and nocturnal cough.

**How should this response be interpreted?**

A. Significant reversibility, which should raise the possibility of asthma
B. Not significant, as the increase is under 200 mL
C. Typical of COPD, which never shows any reversibility
D. A test error, because COPD is fully irreversible

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** A bronchodilator response is significant if FEV1 rises by at least 200 mL and 12%. Her rise is 500 mL (31%). If there is a marked response, asthma should be considered, supported here by atopy and nocturnal cough. B misreads the change. C and D are wrong because some COPD patients show significant reversibility, but airflow never normalises.

**Teaching point:** A rise of ≥200 mL and ≥12% is significant; a marked response suggests asthma.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.6; Table 4-2.

### Q8
**Topic:** Pack-years

A 55-year-old man has smoked 30 cigarettes a day for 20 years.

**What is his smoking history in pack-years?**

A. 20 pack-years
B. 60 pack-years
C. 30 pack-years
D. 600 pack-years

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Total pack-years = (number of cigarettes per day ÷ 20) x number of years smoked = (30 ÷ 20) x 20 = 30. A ignores the daily amount, B doubles it, and D multiplies cigarettes by years without dividing by 20. COPD patients usually have more than 10 pack-years.

**Teaching point:** Pack-years = (cigarettes per day / 20) x years.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.3; Table 4-2.

### Q9
**Topic:** Alpha-1 antitrypsin screening

A 39-year-old man who has smoked 8 cigarettes a day since age 25 has post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC of 0.58. A chest radiograph shows lower-zone hyperlucency.

**Which additional investigation is most appropriate?**

A. Sweat chloride test
B. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency screening
C. Bronchial provocation test
D. High-resolution CT as a routine baseline

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Alpha-1 antitrypsin screening should be performed in young COPD patients (<45 years) or those with a strong family history. Severe deficiency causes panlobular emphysema in smokers and non-smokers. His age, modest smoking history and lower-zone emphysema fit. A and C test other conditions. D is wrong because HRCT is not routinely recommended unless there is diagnostic uncertainty.

**Teaching point:** COPD under 45 years: screen for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 3.2 and 4.8(vi).

### Q10
**Topic:** When to check arterial blood gases in stable COPD

A 71-year-old woman with stable COPD has FEV1 34% predicted. Resting SpO2 on air is 89%. She has no ankle oedema.

**Why should arterial blood gases be performed?**

A. They are only needed during an exacerbation
B. They are routine for all COPD patients at diagnosis
C. They are only needed if PaCO2 is known to be high
D. Her FEV1 is <40% predicted with SpO2 <92%, so she may qualify for long-term oxygen therapy

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** ABG should be performed in patients with FEV1 <40% predicted if SpO2 is less than 92% on pulse oximetry, or there are signs of respiratory failure or cor pulmonale, as these patients may benefit from long-term oxygen therapy. A is wrong because LTOT assessment must be done when stable. B and C do not match the CPG indication.

**Teaching point:** FEV1 <40% plus SpO2 <92% (or cor pulmonale): check ABG for LTOT.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.8(iii); Section 7.2.

### Q11
**Topic:** Distinguishing COPD from asthma

A 48-year-old man has 6 months of breathlessness. Several features are noted.

**Which feature most favours COPD rather than asthma according to Table 4-2?**

A. Morning cough productive of sputum with a 25 pack-year history
B. Nocturnal cough and wheeze on exertion
C. Onset of symptoms in childhood
D. A family history of eczema and hay fever

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Table 4-2 lists COPD features as onset usually after 40, usually >10 pack-years, infrequent atopy, persistent and progressive symptoms, morning cough with sputum, and airflow limitation that may improve but never normalises. B, C and D are asthma features (nocturnal or exertional cough, early onset, atopic family history).

**Teaching point:** Morning productive cough, >10 pack-years and onset after 40 point to COPD.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4.9, Table 4-2.

### Q12
**Topic:** When spirometry is not available

A district clinic without spirometry sees a 60-year-old smoker with chronic cough and breathlessness. Peak flow is 55% predicted with little bronchodilator response.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Diagnose COPD on peak flow alone and discharge
B. Diagnose asthma because peak flow is reduced
C. Refer for spirometry to confirm the diagnosis
D. Screen all adults in the district with spirometry

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Where spirometry is unavailable, symptoms with a low peak flow can help, but peak flow has poor specificity and is only reduced in advanced COPD, so every effort should be made to refer for spirometry. A accepts a non-specific test. B is unsupported. D is wrong because spirometry is not recommended for screening all adults; it is a diagnostic test for those at risk.

**Teaching point:** Peak flow cannot confirm COPD; refer at-risk patients for spirometry, but do not screen everyone.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 4.1, 9.2 and 9.4.

## Section 2: Risk Reduction and Vaccination

### Q13
**Topic:** Most effective intervention

A 54-year-old woman with stage II COPD who smokes 15 cigarettes a day asks which single step will most slow her disease.

**Which intervention does the CPG identify as the single most effective?**

A. Smoking cessation
B. Long-acting anticholinergic inhaler
C. Inhaled corticosteroid
D. Pulmonary rehabilitation

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Smoking cessation is the single most effective and cost-effective intervention to reduce the risk of developing COPD and stop its progression. All smokers should be offered intervention. Inhaled therapies improve symptoms and exacerbations but, for example, tiotropium did not reduce the rate of FEV1 decline in UPLIFT. Rehabilitation improves exercise tolerance and quality of life.

**Teaching point:** Only smoking cessation changes the course of COPD this decisively.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 5.2 and 5.3; Recommendation: Smoking Cessation.

### Q14
**Topic:** The 5A model

A doctor sees a 47-year-old smoker with COPD who has been advised to quit. The doctor now asks whether he is willing to make a quit attempt within the next 30 days.

**Which step of the 5A model is this?**

A. Ask
B. Assess
C. Assist
D. Arrange

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In the 5A model, Ask identifies tobacco users at every visit, Advise strongly urges quitting, Assess determines willingness to make a quit attempt (for example within the next 30 days), Assist helps with a quit plan and pharmacotherapy, and Arrange schedules follow-up.

**Teaching point:** Ask, Advise, Assess (willingness within 30 days), Assist, Arrange.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.4.

### Q15
**Topic:** Brief advice

A busy primary care doctor has 3 minutes with a smoker attending for an unrelated sprained ankle.

**Which statement about brief counselling is consistent with the CPG?**

A. Brief advice is ineffective without pharmacotherapy
B. Advice should be given only to smokers with respiratory symptoms
C. Even 3 minutes of counselling achieves quit rates of about 5-10% and should be given at every visit
D. Counselling is only effective in group programmes

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Even a brief three-minute period of counselling urging a smoker to quit results in cessation rates of 5-10%, and at the very least this should be done for every smoker at every visit, including those attending for unrelated reasons. A, B and D contradict this.

**Teaching point:** Three minutes of advice works; give it to every smoker, every visit.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 5.3 and 5.5.

### Q16
**Topic:** Varenicline adverse effects

A 50-year-old man with COPD and a history of depression starts varenicline.

**Which adverse effects should he be counselled about?**

A. Hyperkalaemia and gynaecomastia
B. Nausea, and possible depressed mood, agitation or suicidal thinking
C. Tachyarrhythmia and seizures only
D. Weight loss and diarrhoea

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The main adverse effect of varenicline, a nicotinic receptor partial agonist, is mild to moderate nausea that usually subsides. There are concerns that it may be linked with depressed mood, agitation or suicidal thinking and behaviour, which matters given his history. A is unrelated. D describes PDE4 inhibitors.

**Teaching point:** Varenicline: nausea commonly; watch mood.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.6.

### Q17
**Topic:** Influenza vaccination

A 69-year-old man with COPD stage III asks whether the yearly influenza vaccine is worthwhile.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Influenza vaccination does not affect COPD exacerbations
B. It is only recommended after an influenza admission
C. It should be given once in a lifetime
D. Annual vaccination reduces influenza-related respiratory illness by about 76% and reduces exacerbation risk

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Influenza vaccination reduces the risk of COPD exacerbation, and annual vaccination reduces influenza-related acute respiratory illness by 76%. In elderly patients with chronic lung disease, vaccination reduced hospitalisation by 52% and death by 70%. A, B and C contradict this.

**Teaching point:** Annual influenza vaccine for all COPD patients.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.3.

### Q18
**Topic:** Pneumococcal vaccination

A 57-year-old woman with COPD has FEV1 36% predicted. She has never had pneumococcal vaccination.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. PPV23 is not indicated before age 65
B. PPV23 annually
C. PPV23, as she is under 65 with FEV1 <40%; repeat after ≥5 years, to a maximum of two doses in a lifetime
D. PPV23 only after an episode of pneumonia

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** PPV23 is recommended for patients younger than 65 with FEV1 <40% predicted, in whom it reduced community-acquired pneumonia. The panel supports vaccinating all COPD patients at least once, repeated after ≥5 years, with a maximum of two doses in a lifetime. A is wrong for her. B exceeds the maximum. D waits for the complication.

**Teaching point:** PPV23: once, repeat after 5 years, maximum two doses.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.4.

## Section 3: Pharmacological Treatment of Stable COPD

### Q19
**Topic:** Mild COPD with infrequent symptoms

A 56-year-old man with stage I COPD (FEV1 84% predicted) has breathlessness only when hurrying, a few times a month. He has stopped smoking.

**What is the most appropriate pharmacological treatment?**

A. Short-acting beta-2 agonist as needed
B. Tiotropium daily
C. ICS/LABA combination twice daily
D. Oral theophylline

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Figure 6-1 recommends SABA as needed for mild COPD with infrequent symptoms, alongside education, smoking cessation, exercise and vaccination. All patients should have a short-acting bronchodilator as needed. Long-acting agents (B, C) are for persistent symptoms in moderate to very severe disease. D is third line.

**Teaching point:** Mild, infrequent symptoms: as-needed SABA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1, Figure 6-1; Recommendation 3.

### Q20
**Topic:** Persistent symptoms in moderate COPD

A 62-year-old woman with stage II COPD needs her salbutamol inhaler most days (more than twice a week). She has had no exacerbations in the past year.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Add an inhaled corticosteroid alone
B. Start a long-acting anticholinergic or a long-acting beta-2 agonist
C. Start maintenance oral prednisolone
D. Start long-term oxygen

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Persistent symptoms are defined as needing rescue bronchodilators more than twice a week. In moderate to very severe COPD with persistent symptoms but without frequent exacerbations, either a LAAC or a LABA may be initiated. A is not the recommended first long-acting step. C is discouraged because maintenance oral steroids increase mortality. D needs chronic hypoxaemia.

**Teaching point:** Persistent symptoms (rescue >2/week): start LAAC or LABA.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1, Figure 6-1; Recommendation 4.

### Q21
**Topic:** Escalation after a single long-acting bronchodilator

A 67-year-old man with stage III COPD remains breathless on salmeterol 50 µg twice daily, with correct inhaler technique. He has not had an exacerbation.

**Which change is most consistent with Figure 6-1?**

A. Replace salmeterol with an ICS/LABA combination, or add an ICS/LABA to a LAAC
B. Add oral prednisolone 10 mg daily
C. Stop salmeterol and use salbutamol only
D. Add a second LABA

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** If symptoms persist on LAAC or LABA, an ICS/LABA combination should be added to a LAAC, or the LABA replaced with an ICS/LABA combination (with or without theophylline). B is discouraged. C is a step down. D duplicates the class.

**Teaching point:** Still symptomatic on LABA: switch to ICS/LABA (or add ICS/LABA to LAAC).

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1, Figure 6-1; Recommendation 4.

### Q22
**Topic:** Defining frequent exacerbations

A 64-year-old woman with stage III COPD had one exacerbation 7 months ago that needed oral prednisolone and amoxicillin, but no admission.

**How is her exacerbation history classified in the CPG algorithm?**

A. Infrequent, because she was not admitted
B. Infrequent, because frequent means at least 3 per year
C. Not relevant to treatment choice
D. Frequent, because one or more exacerbations needing systemic steroids and/or antibiotics or hospitalisation occurred in the past year

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Frequent exacerbation is defined as one or more episodes requiring systemic corticosteroids with or without antibiotics, and/or hospitalisation, over the past year. In moderate to very severe COPD this points to LAAC, ICS/LABA, or LAAC plus ICS/LABA, with or without theophylline. A and B use the wrong definition. C ignores the algorithm.

**Teaching point:** In this CPG, a single steroid- or antibiotic-treated exacerbation in a year counts as frequent.

**Reference:** CPG Figure 6-1, note c.

### Q23
**Topic:** Frequent exacerbations in mild COPD

A 50-year-old woman with spirometry consistent with stage I COPD has had three courses of antibiotics and steroids for "chest infections" this year.

**What does the CPG algorithm advise?**

A. Start triple therapy immediately
B. Start long-term oxygen
C. Consider an alternative cause, as frequent exacerbations are uncommon in mild COPD
D. Add maintenance oral prednisolone

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Figure 6-1 notes that it is less common for patients with mild COPD to have frequent exacerbations, so an alternative cause should be explored even if the COPD diagnosis is firmly established (for example bronchiectasis, asthma or heart failure). A and B escalate without addressing the mismatch. D is discouraged.

**Teaching point:** Frequent exacerbations or respiratory failure in mild COPD: look for another cause.

**Reference:** CPG Figure 6-1, note d.

### Q24
**Topic:** Tiotropium evidence

A 66-year-old man with stage III COPD starts tiotropium 18 µg daily and asks what it will achieve.

**Which finding from the UPLIFT trial is reported in the CPG?**

A. Tiotropium reduced exacerbations and exacerbation-related hospitalisation but did not slow the rate of FEV1 decline
B. Tiotropium halted the decline in FEV1
C. Tiotropium increased exacerbations
D. Tiotropium is effective for only 6 hours

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** In the 4-year UPLIFT trial (FEV1 <70%), tiotropium reduced exacerbations and hospitalisation due to exacerbation but did not reduce the rate of FEV1 decline compared with placebo. Tiotropium is effective for at least 24 hours. B overstates the effect, and C and D contradict the CPG.

**Teaching point:** Tiotropium reduces exacerbations; it does not slow FEV1 decline.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1 (Inhaled LAAC).

### Q25
**Topic:** Combinations without evidence

A 70-year-old woman with stage III COPD is on tiotropium. Her GP proposes adding budesonide alone.

**Which statement about tiotropium plus ICS alone best reflects the CPG?**

A. It is the preferred combination in COPD
B. There is no data to support the use of this combination
C. It reduces mortality more than ICS/LABA
D. It is preferred over adding an ICS/LABA

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The CPG states there is no data to support combining a LAAC with an ICS. Where a LAAC is insufficient, an ICS/LABA combination is added. A, C and D have no support.

**Teaching point:** Add ICS as part of an ICS/LABA, not alone on top of tiotropium.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1 (Combination of LAAC and ICS).

### Q26
**Topic:** ICS dose in COPD

A 68-year-old man with stage III COPD and frequent exacerbations is to start an ICS-containing combination.

**What minimum daily ICS dose does the CPG specify?**

A. 100 µg fluticasone or 200 µg budesonide
B. 250 µg fluticasone or 400 µg budesonide
C. 2,000 µg fluticasone
D. At least 500 µg fluticasone or 800 µg budesonide

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The notes to Figures 6-1 and 6-2 state that the ICS dose per day should be at least 500 µg of fluticasone or 800 µg of budesonide (for example fluticasone 500 µg twice daily or budesonide 400 µg twice daily). A and B are too low, and C is far above this.

**Teaching point:** COPD ICS: at least 500 µg fluticasone or 800 µg budesonide a day.

**Reference:** CPG Figure 6-1, note 2; Section 6.5.1.

### Q27
**Topic:** Maintenance oral corticosteroids

A 74-year-old man with very severe COPD has taken prednisolone 10 mg daily for 2 years because he "feels better on it". He now has vertebral fractures and diabetes.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Maintenance oral steroids are associated with dose-dependent increased mortality and serious side effects, so prolonged courses should be discouraged
B. About half of COPD patients respond to oral steroids
C. Maintenance oral steroids slow FEV1 decline
D. The dose should be increased to 20 mg to improve benefit

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Only about 10% of COPD patients show significant improvement in lung function with oral corticosteroids. Chronic use causes osteoporosis, cataract, myopathy, diabetes and hypertension, and maintenance therapy in severe COPD is associated with increased mortality in a dose-dependent manner. Prolonged courses should be discouraged. B, C and D contradict this.

**Teaching point:** Avoid maintenance oral steroids in COPD; they increase mortality.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1 (Oral corticosteroids).

### Q28
**Topic:** Theophylline

A 72-year-old woman with stage IV COPD remains symptomatic despite tiotropium and fluticasone/salmeterol. Theophylline is considered.

**Which statement is most accurate?**

A. Theophylline is first-line therapy in stable COPD
B. It is a third-line add-on; monitor levels aiming for 10-20 mg/L and watch for nausea and arrhythmias
C. No monitoring is needed
D. Its therapeutic range is 40-60 mg/L

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Theophylline is a weak bronchodilator relegated to third-line therapy, added only if inhaled long-acting bronchodilators and ICS do not control symptoms. Because of the narrow therapeutic window and toxicity (nausea, abdominal discomfort, diarrhoea, arrhythmias), drug level monitoring is desirable, with a therapeutic range of 10-20 mg/L. A, C and D contradict this.

**Teaching point:** Theophylline: third line, level 10-20 mg/L.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1 (Methylxanthines); Recommendation 5.

### Q29
**Topic:** PDE4 inhibitors

A 65-year-old man with severe COPD and chronic bronchitic symptoms (daily productive cough) has two exacerbations a year despite a LABA.

**Which statement about roflumilast is consistent with the CPG?**

A. It acts mainly as a bronchodilator with no effect on exacerbations
B. Its main adverse effect is oral candidiasis
C. It reduced exacerbations by about 17% in severe COPD with bronchitic symptoms, but can cause nausea, diarrhoea, weight loss and headache
D. It is contraindicated with a LABA

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Two 12-month studies in severe COPD with bronchitic symptoms showed roflumilast reduced exacerbations by 17% per patient per year, even with concomitant LABA, and improved pre-bronchodilator FEV1 by 48-80 mL. Adverse effects include nausea, diarrhoea, weight loss and headache. A, B and D are wrong.

**Teaching point:** PDE4 inhibitors suit bronchitic, exacerbating COPD; watch GI effects and weight loss.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.5.1 (PDE4 inhibitors).

### Q30
**Topic:** Resource-limited settings

A rural clinic with no access to long-acting bronchodilators manages a 60-year-old man with stage III COPD and persistent symptoms.

**Which regimen does the resource-limited algorithm suggest?**

A. Regular SABA/SAAC combination, adding theophylline and/or ICS if symptoms persist
B. No treatment until long-acting bronchodilators are available
C. Maintenance oral prednisolone
D. Long-term oxygen for all patients

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Figure 6-2 recommends regular SABA/SAAC combination for persistent symptoms, adding theophylline and/or ICS if symptoms persist, and considering referral to a tertiary centre to obtain long-acting bronchodilators for frequent exacerbations. B withholds treatment. C is discouraged. D requires chronic hypoxaemia.

**Teaching point:** Without long-acting agents: regular SABA/SAAC, then add ICS and/or theophylline.

**Reference:** CPG Figure 6-2; Recommendation 6.

## Section 4: Non-pharmacological Management

### Q31
**Topic:** Pulmonary rehabilitation

A 69-year-old woman with stage III COPD has reduced exercise tolerance despite optimal inhalers.

**Which statement about pulmonary rehabilitation is consistent with the CPG?**

A. It improves survival in COPD
B. It is only for stage I disease
C. Respiratory muscle training is more helpful than general aerobic conditioning
D. Programmes usually last 6-12 weeks and improve exercise tolerance, dyspnoea and quality of life, with benefits lasting 12-18 months

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Most programmes last 6-12 weeks, with benefits lasting 12-18 months. Benefits include better exercise tolerance, less dyspnoea, better HRQoL, fewer hospital days and fewer exacerbations. There is insufficient evidence that it improves survival (A). It is recommended for symptomatic stage II-IV patients (B). General aerobic conditioning is more helpful than specific respiratory muscle training (C).

**Teaching point:** Rehabilitation improves function and quality of life, not proven survival.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.1; Recommendations: Pulmonary Rehabilitation.

### Q32
**Topic:** LTOT indication, severe hypoxaemia

A 70-year-old man with stable COPD, on optimal treatment and no longer smoking, has ABG on air on two occasions 3 weeks apart showing PaO2 52 mmHg and PaCO2 48 mmHg. He has no oedema.

**What is the most appropriate management?**

A. No oxygen, because he has no cor pulmonale
B. Oxygen only during exertion
C. Long-term oxygen therapy for more than 15 hours a day
D. Nocturnal oxygen only

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** LTOT is indicated when PaO2 is ≤55 mmHg (7.3 kPa) or SaO2 ≤88%, with or without hypercapnia, on two stable measurements. Oxygen for more than 15 hours a day increases survival in chronic respiratory failure. A requires cor pulmonale only for the 55-60 mmHg band. B and D do not provide the survival benefit; nocturnal desaturation alone does not improve survival.

**Teaching point:** PaO2 ≤55 mmHg when stable: LTOT for >15 hours a day.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.

### Q33
**Topic:** LTOT indication, borderline hypoxaemia

A 66-year-old woman with stable COPD has PaO2 57 mmHg on air on two occasions. Her haematocrit is 58%.

**Does she meet the CPG criteria for long-term oxygen therapy?**

A. No, because PaO2 is above 55 mmHg
B. Yes, because PaO2 is 55-60 mmHg with polycythaemia (haematocrit >55%)
C. No, because polycythaemia is not relevant
D. Yes, but only if PaCO2 is raised

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** LTOT is indicated with PaO2 between 55 and 60 mmHg (7.3-8.0 kPa) or SaO2 89% if there is pulmonary hypertension, peripheral oedema suggesting heart failure, or polycythaemia (haematocrit >55%). Her haematocrit of 58% qualifies her. A ignores the second criterion. C is wrong. D adds a requirement the CPG does not make.

**Teaching point:** PaO2 55-60 qualifies for LTOT only with pulmonary hypertension, oedema or haematocrit >55%.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.

### Q34
**Topic:** When LTOT is not indicated

A 64-year-old man with COPD and PaO2 53 mmHg on two stable measurements continues to smoke 20 cigarettes a day and declines cessation support.

**What is the most appropriate response to his request for home oxygen?**

A. Prescribe LTOT, as hypoxaemia alone determines eligibility
B. Prescribe oxygen cylinders for use while smoking
C. Prescribe oxygen only at night
D. LTOT is not indicated while he continues to smoke; address smoking first

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Oxygen therapy is not indicated for patients who continue to smoke, those who maintain PaO2 >60 mmHg, those who have not had adequate other therapy, and those not motivated to undertake oxygen therapy. Smoking near oxygen is also a fire hazard. A ignores these exclusions. B and C are unsafe or ineffective.

**Teaching point:** Continued smoking is an exclusion for home oxygen.

### Q35
**Topic:** Timing of LTOT assessment

A 73-year-old woman is admitted with an exacerbation of COPD. On day 3 her SpO2 on air is 86%, and the team wants to arrange home oxygen at discharge.

**What is the most appropriate approach?**

A. Assess for LTOT when she is stable on optimal treatment, with ABG on two occasions, not during the exacerbation or its recovery
B. Prescribe LTOT on day 3 based on this single reading
C. Use an SpO2 of 86% on day 3 as diagnostic
D. LTOT assessment is only possible in hospital during exacerbations

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Assessment for LTOT should not be done during an exacerbation or its recovery period. ABG should be measured on two occasions when the patient is stable and on optimal treatment. Patients started on oxygen are reassessed after 1-2 months and at least annually. B, C and D contradict this.

**Teaching point:** Assess for LTOT only when stable, with two ABGs.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2.

### Q36
**Topic:** Nutrition

A 71-year-old man with very severe COPD has lost 6 kg over a year. His BMI is 18.5 kg/m2 and serum albumin is 31 g/L.

**What is the most appropriate nutritional goal?**

A. Nutritional supplements alone, which improve lung function
B. Anabolic steroids to reverse muscle loss
C. Aim for BMI 22-27 kg/m2 and albumin above 35 g/L through balanced, adequate-calorie diet combined with exercise
D. Weight loss to reduce the work of breathing

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Low BMI is associated with higher mortality. The goal is a BMI of 22-27 kg/m2 and albumin above 35 g/L, using a balanced diet with adequate calories together with exercise. Supplements alone have not improved lung function or exercise capacity (A), and anabolic steroids have been disappointing (B). D applies only to obese patients.

B. She is under 60 with FEV1 <25% predicted after bronchodilator

**Answer:** B

B. Use general anaesthesia with high-dose opioids
C. Prescribe prophylactic long-term antibiotics
D. Stop smoking (ideally 4-8 weeks before surgery) and optimise his COPD treatment, considering regional anaesthesia where feasible

**Answer:** D

### Q40
**Topic:** Risk before pneumonectomy

A 66-year-old woman with COPD and lung cancer is assessed for pneumonectomy. Her preoperative FEV1 is 1.6 L (48% predicted) and DLCO is 45% predicted.

B. She is low risk and needs no further testing
C. Pneumonectomy is safe whenever FEV1 is above 1 L

## Section 5: Acute Exacerbations

### Q41
**Topic:** Definition of an exacerbation

A 69-year-old man with COPD has had 3 days of increased breathlessness and sputum beyond his usual day-to-day variation.

**Which feature is part of the CPG definition of an acute exacerbation?**

A. Symptoms lasting at least 2 weeks
B. A fall in FEV1 of more than 50%
C. Fever above 38°C
D. Sustained worsening for 48 hours or more beyond normal day-to-day variation, acute in onset, that may warrant a change in medication

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** An exacerbation is a sustained (48 hours or more) worsening of baseline dyspnoea, cough and/or sputum beyond normal day-to-day variation, acute in onset, that may warrant a change in regular medication. Spirometry is not recommended during an exacerbation, and fever is not required. A, B and C are not part of the definition.

**Teaching point:** Exacerbation = sustained worsening for ≥48 hours beyond usual variation.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.2.

### Q42
**Topic:** Home management with steroids

A 63-year-old woman with COPD (baseline FEV1 44% predicted) has an exacerbation with increased dyspnoea. She is alert, eating, and has good home support. She is not hypoxic.

**Which corticosteroid regimen is most appropriate?**

A. Oral prednisolone 30-40 mg daily for 7-14 days
B. No steroid, as she is managed at home
C. Prednisolone 60 mg daily for 6 weeks
D. Inhaled steroid alone at double dose

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Systemic corticosteroids should be added to bronchodilators in an exacerbation with significant increase in dyspnoea or a baseline FEV1 <50% predicted. A dose of 30-40 mg prednisolone daily for 7-14 days suits most patients, with no advantage to prolonged courses. She meets both criteria. B omits indicated treatment. C is too long. D is not the recommended regimen.

**Teaching point:** Exacerbation steroids: prednisolone 30-40 mg for 7-14 days.

### Q43
**Topic:** When to give antibiotics

A 60-year-old man with COPD has 3 days of increased breathlessness and more sputum, which remains white. He is afebrile.

**Should antibiotics be given according to the CPG?**

A. No, because purulent sputum is essential
B. No, because antibiotics are never indicated in exacerbations
C. Yes, because he has 2 of the 3 cardinal symptoms (increased dyspnoea and increased sputum volume)
D. Only if the white cell count is raised

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Antibiotics should be given to patients with at least 2 of 3 cardinal symptoms: purulent sputum, increased sputum volume and/or increased dyspnoea. Patients needing invasive or non-invasive ventilation should also receive antibiotics. A requires purulence, which is only one of three. B is wrong. D is wrong because a normal white count does not rule out sepsis.

### Q44
**Topic:** Antibiotic choice in complicated COPD

A 72-year-old woman with COPD (FEV1 38% predicted) on home oxygen has an exacerbation with purulent sputum and increased dyspnoea. She has no risk factors for Pseudomonas and is managed at home.

**Which antibiotic regimen is preferred in Table 8-2?**

A. No antibiotic
B. Oral doxycycline 100 mg once only
C. Topical antibiotic
D. A beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor, such as amoxicillin/clavulanate 2 g twice daily, or a 2nd/3rd-generation cephalosporin

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Complicated COPD (FEV1 <50%, >4 exacerbations a year, age >65, significant co-morbidity, home oxygen, chronic oral steroids, or antibiotics in the past 3 months) is treated preferentially with a beta-lactam plus beta-lactamase inhibitor (amoxicillin 1 g three times daily or amoxicillin/clavulanate 2 g twice daily) or a 2nd/3rd-generation cephalosporin, with or without an extended-spectrum macrolide. **Reference:** CPG Section 8.9.4, Table 8-2.

### Q46
**Topic:** Controlled oxygen in hospital

A 70-year-old woman with an exacerbation of COPD arrives in the emergency department with SpO2 82% on air. Blood gas results are not yet available.

**What is the most appropriate oxygen therapy?**

A. 15 l/min via non-rebreather mask
B. No oxygen until blood gases are available
C. Controlled oxygen at 24-28% via Venturi mask (or 1-2 l/min nasal prongs), aiming for SpO2 90-93%, with ABG checked after 30-60 minutes
D. 100% oxygen via CPAP mask

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Controlled oxygen therapy is the cornerstone of hospital treatment: 24-28% via a Venturi mask, or 1-2 l/min via nasal prongs, to achieve PaO2 ≥60 mmHg or SpO2 ≥90% without worsening hypercapnia. The algorithm targets SpO2 90-93%, as morbidity and mortality rise in hypercapnic failure when SpO2 exceeds 93-95%. ABG should be checked 30-60 minutes later. A and D risk CO2 narcosis. B leaves her hypoxic.

**Teaching point:** AECOPD oxygen: 24-28% Venturi, target SpO2 90-93%, recheck ABG in 30-60 minutes.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 8.5.3 and 8.9.1; Figures 8-2 and 8-3.

### Q47
**Topic:** Non-invasive ventilation

A 66-year-old man with an exacerbation has received nebulised bronchodilators, steroids, antibiotics and controlled oxygen for 2 hours. He is alert and cooperative. Respiratory rate is 28/min with accessory muscle use. ABG: pH 7.29, PaCO2 9.1 kPa, PaO2 8.3 kPa.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Non-invasive ventilation in a dedicated setting with trained staff
B. Immediate intubation
C. Increase oxygen to 60%
D. Continue current treatment and repeat ABG tomorrow

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** NIV is indicated for persistent hypercapnic respiratory failure despite optimal medical therapy, with moderate to severe dyspnoea, respiratory rate >25/min and moderate to severe acidosis (pH 7.25-7.35) and/or PaCO2 >6.0 kPa. It reduces intubation, complications, mortality and length of stay, and should be delivered by trained staff with a plan for deterioration. B is for NIV failure, pH <7.25, or contraindications. C worsens hypercapnia. D delays treatment.

**Teaching point:** pH 7.25-7.35 with hypercapnia despite treatment: NIV.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.11, Tables 8-3 and 8-4.

### Q48
**Topic:** Contraindication to NIV

A 74-year-old woman with an exacerbation and pH 7.30 has become drowsy and uncooperative, with copious secretions she cannot clear.

**Why is NIV inappropriate?**

A. Her pH is too high for NIV
B. NIV is only for asthma
C. Impaired mental status and copious secretions are contraindications to NIV
D. NIV is contraindicated in all patients over 70

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Contraindications to NIV include respiratory arrest, cardiovascular instability, high aspiration risk, impaired mental status or an uncooperative patient, viscous or copious secretions, facial injury or surgery and others. Impaired mental status and somnolence are also indications for invasive ventilation. A misreads the pH (7.25-7.35 is the NIV range). B and D are wrong.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 8.11 and 8.12, Tables 8-4 and 8-5.

### Q49
**Topic:** Intravenous aminophylline

A 69-year-old man with a severe exacerbation has an inadequate response to nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium. He takes oral sustained-release theophylline 300 mg twice daily at home.

**What is the correct approach to intravenous aminophylline?**

A. Give a loading dose of 5 mg/kg over 20 minutes, then infusion
B. Do not give a loading dose because he is already on theophylline; start the maintenance infusion guided by plasma levels
C. Give twice the usual loading dose
D. Aminophylline is contraindicated in exacerbations

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Intravenous methylxanthines can be considered in severe exacerbations with inadequate response to SABA and SAAC. The loading dose is 250-500 mg (5 mg/kg) over 20 minutes, then 500 µg/kg/hour, adjusted to a plasma level of 10-20 mg/L. Patients already on maintenance theophylline should not be given a loading dose, which makes A the trap. C risks toxicity. D is wrong.

**Teaching point:** No aminophylline loading dose in patients already taking theophylline.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.9.2.

### Q50
**Topic:** Discharge and follow-up

A 67-year-old woman admitted with an exacerbation is improving. She uses her nebuliser every 6 hours, walks across the ward, sleeps without waking breathless, and her SpO2 and clinical state have been stable for 24 hours.

**Which statement about discharge is consistent with the CPG?**

A. She must stay until spirometry is normal
B. She should not be discharged until she needs no bronchodilator at all
C. Follow-up is only needed if she deteriorates
D. She meets the discharge criteria; arrange review within 8 weeks to check spirometry, inhaler technique, smoking status and need for LTOT or rehabilitation

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Discharge criteria include inhaled bronchodilator needed no more often than every 4 hours, ability to walk across the room, eating and sleeping without frequent waking by dyspnoea, clinical and ABG/SpO2 stability for 12-24 hours, understanding of treatment, and organised follow-up. Review should be within 8 weeks, covering spirometry, inhaler technique, smoking, LTOT, rehabilitation and vaccination. A and B set impossible targets. C omits planned follow-up.

**Teaching point:** Discharge when bronchodilator needs are ≤4-hourly and stable 12-24 hours; review within 8 weeks.

**Reference:** CPG Sections 8.13 and 8.14.
