# Management of Gout (2nd Edition): 50 MCQs

**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Gout*, 2nd Edition, including its tables and appendices.
**Format:** 50 single-best-answer questions, 4 options (A-D), MRCP Part 1 style. At least 20 have long multilayer stems that need clinical reasoning. Several stems reproduce CPG tables as text, with the table or appendix cited. Every wrong answer is a near-miss.
**Note:** Every fact is taken from the CPG text. Practice use only, not for real patient care.

## Section 1: Diagnosis

### Q1
**Topic:** ACR/EULAR gout classification score

A 52-year-old man has had one episode of severe pain, redness and swelling of the right great toe MTP joint. He could not bear the bedsheet touching it. Pain peaked within 12 hours and the episode settled in 9 days with complete resolution. No joint aspiration was done and no imaging is available. Serum urate (uricase method, in the intercritical period) is 0.52 mmol/l. There is no tophus.

Adapted from CPG Table 2 (ACR/EULAR 2015 gout classification, clinical and laboratory items):

| Item | Category | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Joint pattern | Ankle or midfoot (no first MTP) | 1 |
| | First MTP involved | 2 |
| Episode characteristics | One / two / three of: erythema, cannot bear touch, great difficulty walking | 1 / 2 / 3 |
| Time course | One typical episode / recurrent typical episodes | 1 / 2 |
| Tophus | Present | 4 |
| Serum urate | <0.24 / 0.36 to <0.48 / 0.48 to <0.60 / ≥0.60 mmol/l | -4 / 2 / 3 / 4 |

Classification as gout needs a score of 8 or more (in the absence of MSU crystals).

**What is his score and classification?**

A. 6; not classified as gout
B. 7; not classified as gout
C. 8; classified as gout
D. 9; classified as gout

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Joint pattern: first MTP gives 2. Characteristics: erythema (reported) and cannot bear touch are two characteristics, so 2 (the third item, difficulty walking, was not stated). Time course: pain peaking under 24 hours, resolution within 14 days and complete resolution between episodes meet at least two typical features, but it is one episode, so 1. Tophus: none, 0. Serum urate 0.52 mmol/l falls in 0.48 to <0.60, giving 3. Synovial fluid and imaging not done, so 0. Total 2 + 2 + 1 + 3 = 8, which classifies as gout.

**Teaching point:** ACR/EULAR 2015: add the clinical (first MTP 2, characteristics 1-3, time course 1-2, tophus 4) and laboratory (urate) items. 8 or more means gout.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.5, Table 2.

### Q2
**Topic:** Sufficient criterion

A 61-year-old man presents with an acutely swollen right knee. Aspiration shows needle-shaped, negatively birefringent crystals under polarised light, with strong yellow colour parallel to the compensator axis. Serum urate that day is 0.31 mmol/l.

**What is the most appropriate interpretation?**

A. Gout is excluded because the serum urate is normal
B. Pseudogout, because the crystals are needle-shaped
C. Gout needs a score of 8 or more even with crystals present
D. Gout is confirmed; MSU crystals in a symptomatic joint are sufficient for classification

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Demonstration of MSU (negatively birefringent, needle-shaped) crystals in synovial fluid or tophus aspirate is the gold standard and is 100% specific. Under the ACR/EULAR approach, crystals in a symptomatic joint are a sufficient criterion, so no scoring is needed. A normal or low urate during a flare does not exclude gout. Calcium pyrophosphate crystals are rhomboid and weakly positively birefringent.

**Teaching point:** MSU crystals confirm gout whatever the serum urate. Urate can be normal during a flare.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.1; Section 7.3; Table 2 (sufficient criterion).

### Q3
**Topic:** Normal urate during a flare

A 45-year-old man has the first attack of podagra with a 2-day history of a red, exquisitely tender first MTP joint. Serum urate is 0.33 mmol/l. His doctor says gout is "ruled out" and prescribes antibiotics.

**What is the most appropriate approach to the urate result?**

A. Accept; normal urate excludes gout
B. Repeat urate in 24 hours
C. Treat as gout if the picture is typical, and repeat urate at least 2 weeks after the flare resolves
D. Start allopurinol immediately without further testing

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** A normal or low serum urate during a flare does not exclude gout, because the level may not be elevated during an attack. If suspicion is high, urate may be repeated two weeks or more after complete resolution. Diagnosis may rest on typical clinical features when crystals cannot be demonstrated. Urate-lowering therapy is not started on the strength of a single normal result during a flare, and the repeat test is not done at 24 hours.

**Teaching point:** Flare urate can be falsely normal. Repeat it at least 2 weeks after resolution.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.3.

### Q4
**Topic:** Septic arthritis versus gout (long clinical stem)

A 68-year-old woman with long-standing hypertension on hydrochlorothiazide and CKD stage 3 presents with a hot, swollen, very painful right knee for 2 days. Temperature is 38.6°C, pulse 112/min, WCC 17 x10^9/l and CRP 210 mg/l. She had a steroid injection into the knee 5 days ago. Serum urate is 0.58 mmol/l. She has no tophi and no previous flares. Her son suggests treating it as gout with colchicine at home.

**What is the most appropriate next step?**

A. Colchicine and review in 48 hours
B. Plain radiograph only and oral NSAID
C. Allopurinol now, to reduce urate
D. Joint aspiration for Gram stain, culture and crystals, with urgent assessment for septic arthritis

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Septic arthritis is the key differential in acute monoarthritis. This patient has several risk factors: knee involvement, systemic features (fever, tachycardia), leucocytosis, a high CRP and a recent intra-articular injection. A raised urate (also explained by thiazide and CKD) does not exclude infection, and gout and sepsis can coexist. Aspiration for culture and crystals is essential before treating as gout.

**Teaching point:** Hot knee with fever and a recent injection: aspirate and exclude sepsis first, whatever the urate.

**Reference:** CPG Section 9 (Differential diagnoses).

### Q5
**Topic:** Pseudogout

A 74-year-old woman with osteoarthritis of both knees presents with an acute, warm, swollen left knee. She is afebrile with a normal CRP. A knee radiograph shows a thin linear calcification within the hyaline cartilage. Her serum urate is 0.36 mmol/l.

**Which differential does the CPG list for this picture?**

A. Septic arthritis with a normal CRP
B. Psoriatic arthritis
C. Reactive arthritis
D. Acute calcium pyrophosphate crystal arthritis

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Acute calcium pyrophosphate crystal arthritis is suggested by age over 60, involvement of a degenerative joint, and chondrocalcinosis on radiograph. Psoriatic arthritis requires psoriasis or nail dystrophy, and reactive arthritis follows a genitourinary or GI infection with urethral discharge, rash on the soles or conjunctivitis. Septic arthritis is excluded in practice only by aspiration, but she lacks fever and systemic features.

**Teaching point:** CPPD: over 60, degenerative joint, chondrocalcinosis. Confirm with rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent crystals.

**Reference:** CPG Section 9.

### Q6
**Topic:** Ultrasound in atypical gout

A 38-year-old man has recurrent episodes of ankle pain and swelling. Aspiration of an effusion yielded no crystals on two occasions. His serum urate is 0.47 mmol/l. A rheumatologist performs musculoskeletal ultrasound.

**Which findings support the diagnosis of gout?**

A. Bone marrow oedema
B. Synovial hypertrophy only
C. Double contour sign, tophi and punctiform deposits in the synovium
D. Joint space widening

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Ultrasound findings of the double contour sign, tophi, punctiform deposits in the synovial membrane and hyperechoic spots in synovial fluid have good specificity (0.65 to 1.00) for gout. Ultrasound may assist when presentation is atypical or crystals cannot be demonstrated. Dual-energy CT is accurate but not widely available in Malaysia. A false-positive double contour sign may appear at the cartilage surface but disappears with a change in the probe angle.

**Teaching point:** Gout ultrasound: double contour sign, tophi, punctate synovial deposits.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.4b.

### Q7
**Topic:** Radiographic features

A 59-year-old man with 12 years of gout has a foot radiograph showing a punched-out erosion at the first MTP joint with an overhanging edge and a sclerotic rim, plus a soft-tissue mass containing calcification.

**Which statement about plain radiography in gout is correct?**

A. Radiographic change is an early feature, appearing within the first month
B. Overhanging edges with a sclerotic rim and calcified tophi are typical of established gout
C. Erosions of the DIP joints with a gull-wing appearance count as gouty erosions
D. Radiographs are the best way to detect early gout

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Radiographic changes take several years to appear, so radiography has limited value in early gout. Typical late features are bone erosions with overhanging edges and a sclerotic rim, bone proliferation, joint space narrowing and soft-tissue masses (tophi) that can calcify. In the ACR/EULAR definition, erosion excludes DIP joints and the gull-wing appearance (typical of erosive osteoarthritis).

**Teaching point:** Gout erosions: overhanging edge with sclerotic rim. X-rays are insensitive early.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.4a; Table 2 footnote.

### Q8
**Topic:** Handling synovial fluid

A synovial fluid sample from a swollen knee is collected on a Friday evening. The laboratory cannot examine it under polarised light until Monday morning.

**What is the correct way to handle the sample?**

A. Leave at room temperature; crystals are stable for 7 days
B. Add formalin to preserve crystals
C. Freeze at -20°C immediately
D. Refrigerate at 4°C if analysis will be delayed beyond 24-48 hours

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** MSU crystals remain stable in synovial fluid at room temperature for 24 to 48 hours, and the sample should be refrigerated at 4°C if analysis will be delayed. A weekend delay exceeds the room-temperature window. Freezing and formalin are not recommended in the CPG.

**Teaching point:** Analyse synovial fluid within 24-48 hours at room temperature, or refrigerate at 4°C.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.1.

### Q9
**Topic:** Hyperuricaemia is not diagnostic

A 50-year-old woman has a routine serum urate of 0.52 mmol/l. She has no joint symptoms, no tophi, and normal renal function. Her colleague has gout and takes allopurinol.

**Which approach fits the CPG?**

A. Diagnose gout and start allopurinol
B. Start febuxostat to protect the kidney
C. Do not diagnose gout on hyperuricaemia alone, and do not start urate-lowering therapy for prevention
D. Start colchicine prophylaxis

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Gout must not be diagnosed on hyperuricaemia alone, since not all hyperuricaemic people develop gout. There is insufficient evidence to recommend ULT in asymptomatic hyperuricaemia to prevent gout, CKD progression or cardiovascular events. In one RCT of CKD stage 3, gouty arthritis occurred in only 0.91% (febuxostat) versus 5.86% (placebo). The hyperuricaemia threshold is above 408 µmol/l (6.8 mg/dl).

**Teaching point:** Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia is not gout. Do not start ULT for prevention.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.3; Section 10.

## Section 2: Risk Factors, Natural History and Lifestyle

### Q10
**Topic:** Antihypertensive choice (long clinical stem)

A 58-year-old man with hypertension, BMI 31 kg/m2 and recurrent podagra (3 flares this year) takes hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg, bisoprolol 5 mg and amlodipine 5 mg. His BP is 148/92 mmHg. A physician wants to change the regimen to help his gout while still controlling BP.

Adapted from CPG Table 1 (medicines and gout risk):

| Raises gout risk | Lowers gout risk |
|---|---|
| Diuretics | Losartan |
| Non-losartan ARBs | Calcium channel blockers |
| ACE inhibitors | |
| β-blockers | |

**Which change best fits the CPG?**

A. Replace bisoprolol with valsartan
B. Replace amlodipine with perindopril
C. Add furosemide to hydrochlorothiazide
D. Replace hydrochlorothiazide with losartan and continue amlodipine

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Diuretics (loop, thiazide and thiazide-like, but not potassium-sparing), non-losartan ARBs, ACE inhibitors and β-blockers increase gout risk, whereas losartan and calcium channel blockers reduce it. The CPG says diuretics should be avoided or replaced with another antihypertensive in gout. Swapping the thiazide for losartan removes a risk factor and adds a protective drug, while amlodipine (a CCB) already helps. Valsartan is a non-losartan ARB, and perindopril and furosemide raise risk.

**Teaching point:** In gout with hypertension, prefer losartan or a CCB. Avoid thiazides and loop diuretics where possible.

**Reference:** CPG Section 3.1, Table 1; Recommendation 1.

### Q11
**Topic:** Low-dose aspirin

A 70-year-old man with gout and a previous myocardial infarction takes aspirin 100 mg daily. His flares have increased. His daughter asks whether the aspirin should be stopped.

**What does the CPG recommend?**

A. Stop aspirin, since it raises urate
B. Double the aspirin dose
C. Replace aspirin with a diuretic
D. Continue aspirin for its indication, monitor urate and adjust ULT to reach target

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Low-dose aspirin increases the risk of recurrent gout attacks, but the CPG recommends that aspirin taken for appropriate indications (such as a previous MI) should not be stopped, as alternatives are limited. Serum urate should be monitored and ULT adjusted to achieve target, which may help avoid flares. Concomitant allopurinol nullified the detrimental effect of aspirin in one study.

**Teaching point:** Keep indicated aspirin in gout. Optimise ULT to the urate target.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.1c.

### Q12
**Topic:** Dietary advice (long clinical stem)

A 49-year-old man with BMI 33 kg/m2 has had 4 flares in a year. He drinks 3 beers a night, takes sugar-sweetened soft drinks daily, eats red meat twice daily, and tried a cherry supplement. He eats oily fish rarely. He asks which dietary changes matter most.

**Which advice is most consistent with the CPG?**

A. Limit alcohol (all types), animal purines and high-fructose corn syrup; lose weight; fatty fish is acceptable
B. Take omega-3 capsules to prevent flares
C. Stop all vegetables because they contain purines
D. Drink beer but not spirits

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CPG advises weight loss, limiting purine-rich foods especially from animal sources (except omega-3 PUFA-rich fish), limiting all types of alcohol (beer, wine and liquor; risk rises significantly above 2 servings), and limiting high-fructose corn syrup. Dietary n-3-rich fish (2 or more servings in 48 hours) was linked to fewer flares, but omega-3 supplements were not. Purine-rich vegetables and soy did not increase risk, and cherry evidence needs more data.

**Teaching point:** Limit alcohol of any type, animal purines and fructose syrup. Fatty fish helps; supplements do not.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.1b; Recommendation 5.

### Q13
**Topic:** Natural history

A 44-year-old man has had an isolated attack of podagra that settled spontaneously over 8 days. He now feels completely well. His serum urate is 0.49 mmol/l. A colleague says that, because he is well, he does not have gout any more.

**Which term describes his current state?**

A. Chronic gouty arthritis
B. Intercritical gout
C. Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia
D. Tophaceous gout

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Intercritical gout is the asymptomatic period after or between gout flares, despite the persistence of MSU crystals. A gout flare typically peaks within 24 hours and resolves in 1 to 2 weeks. If hyperuricaemia persists, flares become more frequent, with polyarticular involvement and, on average about 10 years after the first symptoms, chronic gouty arthritis with or without tophi. Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia refers to the period before any flare.

**Teaching point:** The quiet interval between flares is intercritical gout, and crystals are still present.

**Reference:** CPG Section 4; Section 5.2.

### Q14
**Topic:** Early or atypical onset

A 27-year-old man with no obesity, no alcohol use and normal renal function presents with an acute first MTP monoarthritis and a high serum urate. He has no family history of gout.

**Which CPG recommendation applies?**

A. Treat as typical gout with no further work-up
B. Start febuxostat 120 mg
C. Refer to orthopaedics for aspiration
D. Consider rheumatology referral for an unclear or atypical presentation, as gout under age 30 without predisposing factors is unusual

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Gout in men under 30 without predisposing risk factors (and in premenopausal women) is an atypical presentation, and the CPG suggests rheumatology referral for such diagnostic uncertainty. Possible causes include enzymatic defects in purine metabolism (for example HPRT deficiency) or other genetic factors. High-dose febuxostat is not an initial approach.

**Teaching point:** Refer young men (<30) without risk factors and premenopausal women with suspected gout.

**Reference:** CPG Section 16.1; Recommendation 12.

## Section 3: Urate-lowering Therapy

### Q15
**Topic:** Indications for urate-lowering therapy

Four men have gout. Which should be started on urate-lowering therapy based on the established CPG indications?

**Which patient meets an established indication?**

A. A man with a single flare 3 years ago and no tophi
B. A man with asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (urate 0.50 mmol/l)
C. A man with 2 flares in the past 12 months
D. A man with one flare and mild obesity

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The established indications are recurrent flares (2 or more in 12 months), one or more tophi, or radiographic damage attributable to gout. ACR also conditionally supports ULT after a first flare in moderate to severe CKD (stage 3 or more), urate above 540 µmol/l or urolithiasis. A single remote flare and asymptomatic hyperuricaemia do not meet an indication.

**Teaching point:** ULT indications: 2 or more flares in 12 months, tophus, or gout damage on X-ray.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2a.

### Q16
**Topic:** Starting ULT during a flare

A 55-year-old man presents with his third flare in 8 months and a tophus on his ear. The flare is being treated with prednisolone 30 mg. He asks whether allopurinol should wait until the flare has resolved completely.

**What does the CPG say?**

A. ULT must wait at least 6 weeks after the flare
B. ULT should be started only after surgery
C. ULT is contraindicated while joints are inflamed
D. ULT can be started during a flare, provided the flare is appropriately treated

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Traditionally ULT was started after a flare resolved, but a meta-analysis of three RCTs showed that starting allopurinol during a flare did not significantly increase pain or prolong the flare. The CPG DG opines that ULT can be initiated during a flare as long as the flare is being appropriately managed. ULT should also be continued through flares.

**Teaching point:** ULT can start during a flare if the flare is treated. Never stop ULT because of a flare.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2a; Section 12.1a.

### Q17
**Topic:** Allopurinol starting dose with renal impairment

A 66-year-old woman with gout and CKD stage 4 (eGFR 24 ml/min/1.73 m2) is starting allopurinol.

Adapted from CPG Appendix 5 (allopurinol dose in renal impairment):

| eGFR (ml/min/1.73 m2) | Initial dose | Increment |
|---|---|---|
| >60 | 100 mg daily | +100 mg every 4 weeks |
| 30-60 | 50 mg daily | +50 mg every 4 weeks |
| <30 | 50 mg every other day | 50 mg daily after 4 weeks, then +50 mg every 4 weeks |

**What is the correct regimen?**

A. 100 mg daily, increased by 100 mg every 4 weeks
B. 50 mg daily, increased by 50 mg every 4 weeks
C. 50 mg every other day, then 50 mg daily after 4 weeks
D. 300 mg daily, as renal function does not alter the starting dose

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** At eGFR below 30, the initial dose is 50 mg every other day, increased to 50 mg daily after 4 weeks and then by 50 mg every 4 weeks as tolerated until the urate target is reached. The risk of allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome relates to starting dose (about 1.5 mg per unit eGFR minimised risk) and renal impairment. The maximum dose in CKD is determined by tolerability, not renal function (maximum 900 mg/day).

**Teaching point:** "Start low, go slow": 100 mg (eGFR >60), 50 mg (30-60), 50 mg alternate days (<30), titrating every 4 weeks.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 5; Section 12.2d.

### Q18
**Topic:** Allopurinol hypersensitivity (long clinical stem)

A 62-year-old Han Chinese man with CKD stage 3 is started on allopurinol 300 mg daily by a locum because "that's the usual dose". Three weeks later he develops a widespread rash, mucosal ulceration, facial swelling, fever and a rising ALT and eosinophil count.

**Which statement best explains the risk and the correct response?**

A. Switch to a higher dose of allopurinol once the rash fades
B. Reduce the dose by half and continue
C. This is a harmless rash; add an antihistamine
D. Risk factors included high starting dose, renal impairment and HLA-B*58:01; stop allopurinol immediately and seek urgent care

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (SCAR such as SJS, TEN or DRESS) is the most serious adverse effect. Its risk factors are a high starting dose, renal impairment and the HLA-B*58:01 allele (common in Han Chinese, Korean and Thai populations). About 90% of cases occur within the first 180 days, with a median of 30 days. Patients should be taught to watch for rash, itch, eye redness and oral or genital ulcers and to stop the drug promptly. Routine local HLA-B*58:01 screening was not found cost-effective in Malaysia.

**Teaching point:** Start allopurinol low (based on eGFR). If rash, ulcers or eye redness appear, stop at once.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2a; Section 15.3.

### Q19
**Topic:** HLA-B*58:01 screening

A 45-year-old Malay man with gout is about to start allopurinol. A student asks whether HLA-B*58:01 testing should be done first.

**What does the CPG say?**

A. Test every patient before starting allopurinol
B. Routine HLA-B*58:01 screening is not recommended locally, so start low and monitor
C. Test only women
D. Test only after the rash appears

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A study showed that HLA-B*58:01 testing before allopurinol was unlikely to be cost-effective in Malaysia, so the CPG DG does not recommend routine screening. Instead, use a low eGFR-based starting dose with gradual titration and educate patients on early cutaneous warning signs. Both renal impairment and the allele increase the risk of SCAR.

**Teaching point:** No routine HLA-B*58:01 screening locally. Reduce risk with a low start dose and slow titration.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2a.

### Q20
**Topic:** Allopurinol interactions

A 40-year-old renal transplant recipient on azathioprine develops tophaceous gout. His team considers allopurinol and febuxostat.

Adapted from CPG Appendix 5 (drug interactions):

| Drug | Interaction with azathioprine/mercaptopurine |
|---|---|
| Allopurinol | Reduces metabolism, so toxicity (bone marrow) rises |
| Febuxostat | Contraindicated with concomitant use |

**Which statement best matches the CPG?**

A. Febuxostat is the preferred choice because it has no interaction
B. Both are freely combinable with azathioprine
C. Febuxostat is contraindicated with azathioprine, and allopurinol needs azathioprine dose adjustment and close monitoring
D. Probenecid is contraindicated with azathioprine

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Febuxostat is contraindicated with azathioprine or mercaptopurine because increased plasma concentrations result in serious toxicity. Allopurinol reduces the metabolism of azathioprine and mercaptopurine and increases bone marrow toxicity, so dose adjustment and monitoring are needed. Other allopurinol interactions are with warfarin (prolongs half-life), ciclosporin (raises levels) and theophylline.

**Teaching point:** Azathioprine/mercaptopurine: febuxostat is contraindicated. Allopurinol needs dose adjustment and FBC monitoring.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 5.

### Q21
**Topic:** Febuxostat and cardiovascular disease (long clinical stem)

A 64-year-old man with gout, established coronary artery disease and a previous stent has had adverse reactions (non-severe rash) to allopurinol, which has since resolved. His urate is 0.55 mmol/l with frequent flares. His doctor considers febuxostat.

**Which statement best reflects the CPG?**

A. Febuxostat is first line and carries no cardiovascular warning
B. Febuxostat has a black-box warning for CV death in patients with established CV disease, so weigh risks and benefits and reserve it for those intolerant of allopurinol
C. Febuxostat is contraindicated in all gout patients
D. Febuxostat is safe at any dose in CKD stage 5

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In CARES, patients with gout and established CV disease on febuxostat had a higher rate of CV death than those on allopurinol, leading to an FDA black-box warning. FAST showed non-inferiority for major CV outcomes in patients with at least one CV risk factor. Febuxostat should be used only in those with inadequate response to a maximally titrated allopurinol dose, intolerance, or when allopurinol is not advisable, after weighing risks and benefits. Dose is limited to 40 mg once daily at CrCl 15-29 ml/min.

**Teaching point:** Febuxostat: second line, black-box warning for CV death in established CVD. Max 40 mg at CrCl 15-29.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2a; Appendix 5.

### Q22
**Topic:** Uricosuric safety (long clinical stem)

A 57-year-old man with gout has a history of recurrent renal stones (calcium oxalate) and an eGFR of 70 ml/min/1.73 m2. He cannot tolerate allopurinol (non-severe rash) and the urate remains 0.56 mmol/l. A friend recommends probenecid.

**What is the most appropriate response?**

A. Start probenecid 500 mg twice daily
B. Start benzbromarone at 200 mg
C. Uricosurics are contraindicated with urolithiasis, so consider febuxostat if suitable, after assessing CV risk
D. Add pegloticase

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Uricosuric agents (probenecid, benzbromarone) are contraindicated in patients with urolithiasis and are not recommended in severe renal impairment (probenecid should be avoided at CrCl below 30, benzbromarone at eGFR below 20). When allopurinol is not tolerated, febuxostat or uricosurics may be considered, so febuxostat is the option here after considering CV risk. Pegloticase is reserved for refractory chronic gouty arthritis with frequent flares or non-resolving tophi. Gout with urolithiasis should be assessed by a urologist.

**Teaching point:** Stones rule out uricosurics. Use a xanthine oxidase inhibitor and involve urology.

**Reference:** CPG Section 12.2a; Appendix 5; Section 16.2.

### Q23
**Topic:** Probenecid interactions and fluid advice

A 60-year-old man starts probenecid because allopurinol and febuxostat are contraindicated. He takes methotrexate, a sulphonylurea and an oral beta-lactam antibiotic.

**Which statement is most accurate?**

A. Probenecid has no interaction with his drugs
B. Probenecid can potentiate methotrexate toxicity, the sulphonylurea's hypoglycaemic effect and beta-lactam levels; maintain 2-3 litres of fluid daily
C. Probenecid lowers methotrexate levels
D. Probenecid needs fluid restriction

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Probenecid increases levels of beta-lactam antibiotics, methotrexate and several other drugs, and increases the hypoglycaemic effect of sulphonylureas. Aspirin reduces its uricosuric action. A fluid intake of 2-3 l/day is advised (unless on a fluid-restricted diet), and the drug should be avoided at CrCl below 30 ml/min. It is contraindicated in urolithiasis and blood dyscrasias.

**Teaching point:** Probenecid: interactions with methotrexate, sulphonylureas and beta-lactams. Drink 2-3 l/day.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 5.

### Q24
**Topic:** Serum urate targets

A 48-year-old man with tophaceous gout and chronic arthropathy has had a serum urate of 0.34 mmol/l for 6 months and still has frequent flares and large tophi. His doctor suggests continuing the same dose.

**Which target does the CPG support?**

A. Below 0.18 mmol/l for tophi
B. Below 0.45 mmol/l for all
C. Below 0.36 mmol/l for all; below 0.30 mmol/l in severe gout (tophi, chronic arthropathy, frequent flares); avoid prolonged levels below 0.18 mmol/l
D. No target is needed if symptoms improve

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Treat-to-target aims for serum urate below 360 µmol/l for all gout patients. A lower target below 300 µmol/l is recommended for severe gout (tophi, chronic arthropathy, frequent flares) to dissolve crystals faster. Prolonged urate below 180 µmol/l is not recommended, since urate may protect against neurodegenerative disease. He is at 340, which meets the general target but not the lower target for severe disease, so further titration to below 300 is reasonable.

**Teaching point:** Urate target <360 µmol/l; <300 µmol/l in severe (tophaceous) gout; avoid <180 µmol/l long term.

**Reference:** CPG Section 11; Recommendation 4.

### Q25
**Topic:** Failure to reach target

A 51-year-old man has taken allopurinol 300 mg daily for 4 months with good adherence. His urate is 0.48 mmol/l and he has had two flares. He has normal renal function and no adverse effects.

### Q27
**Topic:** Colchicine and clarithromycin (long clinical stem)

A 66-year-old man with gout takes colchicine 0.5 mg daily as prophylaxis since starting allopurinol. He develops a chest infection and is prescribed clarithromycin. Two days later he has a gout flare in his ankle.

**Which statement is correct about colchicine in this situation?**

A. Take 1 mg then 0.5 mg 1 hour later as usual, and continue prophylaxis
B. Clarithromycin is a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, so colchicine doses must be reduced and given cautiously, with a prophylaxis dose of 0.25 mg daily or every other day
C. Clarithromycin has no effect on colchicine
D. Double the colchicine dose to compensate

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Colchicine toxicity increases with CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein inhibitors (clarithromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, certain protease inhibitors, and also statins, fibrates, digoxin and ciclosporin, which raise myopathy risk). With a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, the flare treatment is 0.5 mg at first sign followed by 0.25 mg an hour later, and the prophylaxis dose is 0.25 mg daily or every other day. Alternatively, a non-colchicine flare treatment (NSAID or corticosteroid) may be chosen. Dose escalation to compensate would risk myelosuppression and neuromyotoxicity.

**Teaching point:** Strong CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors (clarithromycin, azoles) need much lower colchicine doses, or an alternative agent.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 5 (Colchicine dose modification).

### Q31
**Topic:** Duration and choice of prophylaxis

A 50-year-old man is starting allopurinol. He wants to know how long he needs flare prophylaxis and which method is preferred.

B. At least 3-6 months; preferred options are stepwise ULT dose increase and/or concomitant colchicine
C. Lifelong colchicine; canakinumab is preferred
D. No prophylaxis is needed if urate is below 0.5 mmol/l

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Starting ULT dissolves MSU deposits and disperses crystals, which increases flares. Prophylaxis should be used for at least 3-6 months. The CPG DG prefers a stepwise increase of ULT and/or colchicine. ### Q32

### Q34

A 67-year-old man with a large tophus on his right elbow has a discharging sinus and cellulitis, despite 6 months of ULT. A second patient, a 40-year-old with a small tophus on the ear, asks for it to be removed for cosmetic reasons after only 1 month of treatment.

**Explanation:** The treat-to-target strategy aims for a serum urate below 360 µmol/l (6 mg/dl) for all patients receiving ULT, with a lower target of below 300 µmol/l in severe disease. The 480 µmol/l figure was used to define uncontrolled gout in a cross-sectional study, 540 µmol/l is the urate level that, with CKD stage 3 or more or urolithiasis, favours starting ULT after a first flare, and below 180 µmol/l is not recommended long term.

**Teaching point:** Gout T2T: serum urate below 360 µmol/l. Below 300 in severe disease. Avoid prolonged levels below 180.

**Reference:** CPG Section 11; Recommendation 4; Section 17.
