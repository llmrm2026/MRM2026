**Source:** Ministry of Health Malaysia, *Clinical Practice Guidelines: Early Management of Head Injury in Adults* (MaHTAS), including its algorithms and appendices.
**Format:** 50 single-best-answer questions, 4 options (A-D) of similar length, MRCP Part 1 style. At least 20 have long multilayer stems. Several stems reproduce CPG algorithms or tables as text, with the algorithm or table cited.

## Section 1: Assessment, Triage and Airway

### Q1
**Topic:** GCS scoring and severity

A 29-year-old man is brought to the ED after a motorcycle crash. He opens his eyes only to pressure, says "oh god" (incomprehensible sounds) and withdraws from pain with flexion.

Adapted from CPG Appendix 3 (GCS):

| Component | Responses and scores |
|---|---|
| Eye | Spontaneous 4; to voice 3; to pain 2; none 1 |
| Verbal | Orientated 5; confused 4; words 3; sounds 2; none 1 |
| Motor | Obeys 6; localises 5; flexion withdrawal 4; abnormal flexion 3; extension 2; none 1 |

Severity: mild 13-15, moderate 9-12, severe 3-8.

**What is his GCS and severity?**

A. GCS 7 (E2 V2 M4), severe
B. GCS 8 (E2 V2 M4), severe
C. GCS 9 (E3 V2 M4), moderate
D. GCS 10 (E2 V3 M5), moderate

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Eye opening to pressure scores 2, incomprehensible sounds score 2, and flexion withdrawal scores 4, giving 2 + 2 + 4 = 8. A GCS of 3-8 is severe head injury, so he meets the threshold for securing the airway (GCS ≤8). Scores of 7 or 9 come from mis-scoring a component, and the last option reads localising and words.

**Teaching point:** GCS = Eye + Verbal + Motor. Mild 13-15, moderate 9-12, severe 3-8.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 3.

### Q2
**Topic:** Hypoxaemia in head injury

A 34-year-old woman with a head injury after a fall has a GCS of 14. SpO2 on room air is 88%. Her airway is patent and she has no chest injury.

**What is the most appropriate immediate action?**

A. Give supplemental oxygen and correct the hypoxaemia immediately
B. Observe for 15 minutes to see whether the saturation improves
C. Intubate immediately because SpO2 is below 90%
D. Hyperventilate to an ETCO2 of 25 mmHg

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Hypoxaemia (SpO2 below 90%) is the most important factor related to worse outcome in head injury and must be corrected immediately, with oxygen supplementation for all head injury patients. An airway is established for GCS ≤8, an inability to maintain the airway, or hypoxaemia not corrected by oxygen. Hyperventilation (ETCO2 below 35 mmHg) is avoided unless there are signs of cerebral herniation, and normal ETCO2 is 35-40 mmHg.

**Teaching point:** SpO2 <90% in head injury: give oxygen at once. Intubate if it is not corrected, GCS ≤8 or the airway is not maintained.

**Reference:** CPG Section 5.2; Recommendation 2.

### Q3
**Topic:** Airway indications and technique

A 41-year-old man with a GCS of 8 after an assault has blood in the oropharynx. A neck injury cannot be excluded.

**Which approach matches the CPG?**

A. In-line immobilisation during intubation, with a collar
B. Nasopharyngeal airway as the definitive choice
C. Avoid any airway until the CT scan is done
D. Intubate with the neck flexed, then apply a collar

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** An airway should be established in head injury with severe injury (GCS ≤8), inability to maintain the airway, or hypoxaemia not corrected by oxygen, by the most appropriate means (definitive such as endotracheal intubation, or non-definitive such as a laryngeal mask, depending on expertise). Manual in-line immobilisation should be performed during intubation, and a cervical collar applied if cervical injury is suspected. Isotonic crystalloid is the preferred fluid.

**Teaching point:** GCS ≤8: secure the airway with in-line immobilisation, and use a collar for suspected neck injury.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 2.

### Q4
**Topic:** Intoxication or head injury

A 32-year-old man is found drowsy outside a bar, smelling of alcohol, with a GCS of 12 and a scalp haematoma. A colleague says "he's just drunk, let him sleep it off".

**What is the CPG position?**

A. Alcohol explains any GCS above 10; observe at home
B. Exclude head injury before blaming alcohol
C. A raised blood alcohol excludes intracranial injury
D. Intoxicated patients are watched at home by a friend

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In patients with depressed conscious level, head injury needs to be ruled out before the diagnosis of intoxication is made. Alcohol intoxication and substance misuse are also factors for referral of mild head injury to hospital, and they preclude safe ED observation under the CPG criteria. He has a scalp haematoma and GCS of 12, so he needs assessment and a CT scan.

**Teaching point:** Do not attribute depressed consciousness to alcohol until head injury is excluded.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.2; Recommendations 3 and 7.

### Q5
**Topic:** Triage: physiological criteria (long clinical stem)

A paramedic assesses a 38-year-old man after a head injury from a fall. Measurements: GCS 14, respiratory rate 26/min, systolic BP 88 mmHg, heart rate 112/min, SpO2 94% on room air.

Adapted from CPG Algorithm 2, step 1 (red zone physiological criteria): airway obstruction; GCS <13; RR <10 or >30; SBP <90 mmHg; HR <60 or >110; SpO2 <90% on room air.

**What is the correct triage decision?**

A. Green zone: GCS is above 13
B. Yellow zone: only one criterion is met
C. Red zone: SBP <90 and HR >110 are physiological criteria
D. Red zone only if SpO2 is below 90%

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Step 1 of the triage algorithm checks vital signs and consciousness. SBP 88 mmHg (below 90) and heart rate 112/min (above 110) are both abnormal, so he is red zone regardless of GCS 14, respiratory rate 26 (within 10-30) or SpO2 94%. Any single red criterion is enough. In a head-injured patient hypotension must be investigated for another cause, because isolated head injury does not cause haemorrhagic shock.

**Teaching point:** Any one physiological abnormality (GCS <13, RR <10/>30, SBP <90, HR <60/>110, SpO2 <90%) puts the patient in the red zone.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 2; Recommendation 5; Section 7.1.

### Q6
**Topic:** Triage: older adult

A 72-year-old woman fell from standing height and hit her head. She is alert, GCS 15, on warfarin, with BP 112/70 mmHg and a pulse of 96/min. A paramedic notes the mechanism as "trivial".

**Which statement fits the CPG triage algorithm?**

A. Only a fall over 6 metres triggers escalation
B. Warfarin matters only if INR is above 5
C. A ground-level fall is a green-zone mechanism
D. Age ≥65 and anticoagulation raise the triage zone

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Step 4 of the algorithm assesses special patients: older adults (risk of injury and death rises from age 65, SBP below 110 may represent shock, and low-impact mechanisms such as ground-level falls can cause severe injury) and anticoagulation or bleeding disorders. These raise the zone. Falls over 6 metres (about two storeys) are a mechanism criterion for the high-energy step, so a low fall alone would not. She also meets referral criteria (age ≥65 and anticoagulant) and CT criteria.

**Teaching point:** Older and anticoagulated patients need escalation even with a trivial mechanism.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 2 (step 4); Recommendation 3.

### Q7
**Topic:** Referral from primary care (long clinical stem)

At a rural health clinic with no CT scanner, a doctor reviews five adults with mild head injury. All have a GCS of 15.

**Which patient should be referred to the nearest hospital according to the CPG criteria?**

A. A 24-year-old with a minor scalp bruise, no symptoms, and a reliable relative
B. A 28-year-old with no symptoms who lives with a reliable carer
C. A 67-year-old with a headache and no other features
D. A 31-year-old with no symptoms, normal examination, living with a spouse

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Factors for referral of mild head injury include GCS 15 but symptomatic (amnesia, headache, vomiting or restlessness), age ≥65, antiplatelet or anticoagulant treatment, GCS <15 or declining, alcohol or substance misuse, a focal temporal blow, social issues, and indication for head CT. The 67-year-old is over 65 and symptomatic, so he is referred. Young, asymptomatic patients with a reliable carer can be discharged with advice.

**Teaching point:** Refer mild head injury if age ≥65, symptomatic, on antithrombotics, intoxicated, GCS <15, temporal blow, social issues or CT indicated.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 3.

### Q8
**Topic:** Transport of a head-injured patient

A 45-year-old man with a GCS of 9 after a road traffic crash is to be transferred 90 minutes by road to a neurosurgical centre. The team prepares the ambulance.

**Which precautions does the CPG emphasise before and during transfer?**

A. Skip the checklist so that departure is quick
B. Hyperventilate and sedate so the patient is quiet
C. Avoid hypotension, hypoxia and CO2 changes; monitor and hand over
D. Withhold oxygen and monitor pulse only

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Hypotension, hypoxia, hypocarbia, hypercarbia and inadvertent cervical injury must be avoided before and during transfer. Vital signs are monitored continuously and effective communication is established between the transfer team and receiving hospital, using the transfer checklist (Appendix 9), with trained escorts, adequate oxygen (an additional hour or twice the journey time) and a referral letter with CT films.

**Teaching point:** Safe transfer: avoid secondary insults, monitor continuously, use a checklist and communicate.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 4; Appendix 9.

## Section 2: Observation and Disposition in the ED

### Q9
**Topic:** Head chart observations

A 36-year-old woman with a mild head injury is awaiting CT in the ED. The nurse asks how often she should record observations.

**Which monitoring does the CPG recommend?**

A. Vital signs are unnecessary in mild head injury
B. Observations only if the headache worsens
C. GCS every 6 hours with no pupil examination
D. Head chart with GCS, BP, pulse and pupils at least hourly

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** A head chart including serial GCS, BP, pulse rate and pupil size should be recorded at least hourly in the ED, including while waiting for CT or transfer, and monitored for signs of intracranial hypertension (decreased pupillary response, hypertension with bradycardia, posturing, abnormal breathing). Any deterioration should prompt immediate re-evaluation by the attending doctor.

**Teaching point:** Head chart (GCS, BP, pulse, pupils) at least hourly. Any deterioration means immediate review.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 6; Recommendation 9.

### Q10
**Topic:** Safe ED observation (long clinical stem)

Four patients with mild head injury, no CT indication and GCS 15, are being considered for observation in the ED for a minimum of six hours.

**Which patient can be safely observed in the ED?**

A. 30-year-old who is drunk with an isolated head injury
B. 58-year-old, no deficit, no anticoagulant, sober, no other injury
C. 40-year-old on warfarin with a normal examination
D. 66-year-old on aspirin 100 mg, no deficit

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Criteria for ED observation (minimum 6 hours): GCS 15 on arrival or 2 hours later, no neurological abnormality, age under 65, not on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, no coagulopathy, no multiple injuries, not intoxicated and not under the influence of psychotropic drugs. Patients with mild head injury aged ≥65 or on antithrombotics should be admitted directly to the ward irrespective of CT findings.

**Teaching point:** ED observation needs age <65, no antithrombotics, no intoxication, no other injuries and a normal examination.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 7.

### Q11
**Topic:** Observation frequency after deterioration

A 50-year-old man with a mild head injury has a GCS of 15 for 2 hours, then becomes drowsy with GCS 13. He is re-examined and awaits CT.

**What minimum frequency of observation applies from the time of deterioration until GCS 15 is achieved?**

A. Hourly for 12 hours, then 8-hourly
B. 4-hourly throughout
C. Half-hourly 2 h, hourly 4 h, 2-hourly 6 h, then 4-hourly
D. 15-minutely for 24 hours

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** After any deterioration beyond the initial 2 hours, the minimum observation schedule is half-hourly for the first 2 hours, hourly for the next 4 hours, 2-hourly for the next 6 hours, and 4-hourly thereafter until GCS 15 is achieved or no longer necessary. Routine hourly observations are otherwise adequate in stable patients.

**Teaching point:** After deterioration: half-hourly for 2 h, hourly for 4 h, 2-hourly for 6 h, then 4-hourly.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 9.

### Q12
**Topic:** Admission after ED observation

A 46-year-old man observed for 6 hours has GCS 15, and a normal CT, but vomited twice 3 hours ago. He lives alone in a remote area without transport.

**What should happen?**

A. Admit: vomiting ≥2 and a social problem
B. Discharge with a leaflet only
C. Observe at home with a neighbour
D. Discharge, as the CT is normal

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** After 6 hours of ED observation, patients are admitted if they have clinically significant CT abnormalities, GCS <15, worrying signs (vomiting ≥2 times, seizure, diffuse headache, amnesia, abnormal behaviour, neurological deficit), other injuries needing admission, or a social problem (no supervision by a responsible adult, transport issue, remote area). He has two features. Discharge is for low-risk patients with a reliable carer.

**Teaching point:** Admit after observation for CT abnormality, GCS <15, worrying symptoms, other injuries or social problems.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 10.

### Q13
**Topic:** Safe discharge without observation

A 27-year-old man has a minor blow to the head. He has GCS 15, no amnesia, vomiting, headache or loss of consciousness, a normal neurological examination, and his wife is at home with him.

**What does the CPG say about disposition?**

A. Needs a CT before discharge
B. Discharge without observation, with a reliable carer
C. Needs six hours in ED even if low risk
D. Admit all head injuries for 24 hours

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Patients with low-risk mild head injury (GCS 15, no amnesia, vomiting, diffuse headache or LOC, no neurological deficit) who live with a reliable caregiver can be discharged home safely without observation. It is neither feasible nor desirable to admit all mild head injury patients. Verbal and written discharge advice must be given, and carers should understand it.

**Teaching point:** Low-risk mild head injury plus a reliable carer: discharge without observation, with written advice.

**Reference:** CPG Section 6.3.5; Recommendation 11.

## Section 3: Imaging

### Q14
**Topic:** CT selection: immediate scan (long clinical stem)

Adults present to the ED after head injury.

Adapted from CPG Algorithm 3 (immediate CT risk factors): GCS <13 on initial assessment; GCS <15 at 2 hours; suspected open, closed or depressed skull fracture; any sign of basal skull fracture; post-traumatic seizure; focal neurological deficit; vomiting ≥2 since injury; on anticoagulant, antiplatelet or bleeding disorder with symptoms (headache, LOC, amnesia, vomiting).

**Which patient needs an immediate CT head?**

A. A 33-year-old with GCS 15 and a single episode of vomiting
B. A 39-year-old with GCS 15 and clear fluid from the right ear after a fall
C. A 55-year-old with GCS 15 and transient amnesia of 5 minutes only
D. A 45-year-old with GCS 15 and mild headache only

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Clear fluid from the ear after head trauma suggests CSF otorrhoea, a sign of basal skull fracture, which is an immediate-scan criterion. A single episode of vomiting (two or more required), brief amnesia without other factors, and mild headache alone do not meet the immediate criteria. Raccoon eyes, haemotympanum and Battle's sign are the other basal skull signs.

**Teaching point:** Immediate CT for GCS <13, GCS <15 at 2 hours, skull fracture or its signs, seizure, focal deficit, vomiting ≥2, and symptomatic anticoagulated patients.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 3.

### Q15
**Topic:** CT selection: urgent scan within 6-8 hours

A 68-year-old man tripped on a pavement and hit his head. He has GCS 15, no neurological deficit, no vomiting, and has not lost consciousness. He takes no anticoagulants.

**What imaging decision does Algorithm 3 suggest?**

A. Skull X-ray only
B. Immediate CT within 1 hour
C. No CT: GCS 15 with no symptoms
D. CT within 6-8 hours: age ≥65 is an urgent-scan factor

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Urgent CT risk factors (CT within 6-8 hours) include age ≥65, a history of bleeding or clotting disorder or anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, a dangerous mechanism (pedestrian or cyclist struck by a vehicle, ejection, fall from over 1 metre or 5 stairs), more than 30 minutes of retrograde amnesia, previous cranial surgery, and a history of epilepsy. He is 68, so he qualifies. Immediate CT (within 1 hour) is for the immediate list, and CT within 1-2 hours applies if risk factors arise during observation.

**Teaching point:** Age ≥65, antithrombotics, dangerous mechanism, >30 min retrograde amnesia, cranial surgery or epilepsy: CT within 6-8 hours.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 3.

### Q16
**Topic:** CT during observation

A 35-year-old woman with a mild head injury (GCS 15, no CT indication) is being observed in the ED. At 3 hours she vomits twice and becomes irritable.

**What is the next step?**

A. Discharge with advice, as GCS is still 15
B. Continue observation only, and re-examine in 6 hours
C. Skull X-ray and discharge
D. Head CT within 1-2 hours of the risk factor being identified

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** For risk factors that arise during observation (GCS <15, post-traumatic seizure, focal neurological deficit, vomiting ≥2, worsening diffuse headache, abnormal behaviour, amnesia) the CPG's algorithm advises head CT within 1-2 hours of the risk factor being identified. A provisional verbal radiology report should be available within 1 hour. Her airway and haemodynamics should be secure before the scan.

**Teaching point:** New risk factors during observation: CT within 1-2 hours.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 3.

### Q17
**Topic:** Canadian CT Head Rule

An ED doctor applies the Canadian CT Head Rule to a minor head injury patient. He asks about its limits.

Adapted from CPG Section 7.1:

| Risk | Factors |
|---|---|
| High (neurological intervention) | GCS <15 at 2 h; suspected open or depressed skull fracture; basal skull fracture signs; vomiting ≥2; age ≥65 |
| Medium (brain injury on CT) | Amnesia before impact >30 min; dangerous mechanism (pedestrian struck, ejection, fall >3 feet or 5 stairs) |

**In which patient is the rule NOT applicable?**

A. A patient on an anticoagulant
B. A patient with a GCS of 14 after witnessed loss of consciousness
C. A patient with definite amnesia and GCS 15
D. A patient with witnessed disorientation and GCS 13

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The CCTHR applies only to minor head injury (witnessed LOC, definite amnesia or witnessed disorientation with GCS 13-15). It is not applicable with seizure, penetrating skull injury, focal neurological deficit or anticoagulant use. Patients on anticoagulants or antiplatelets have positive CT in about 29% despite GCS 15, so head CT should be considered in them (Recommendation 12).

**Teaching point:** CCTHR does not apply with seizure, penetrating injury, focal deficit or anticoagulation. Scan anticoagulated patients.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.1; Recommendation 12.

### Q18
**Topic:** Polytrauma and head CT timing

A 30-year-old man is brought in after a road crash with a GCS of 11, BP 78/40 mmHg, a distended abdomen and a positive FAST scan. A colleague wants to send him straight to CT head.

**What does the CPG advise?**

A. Give mannitol to correct the hypotension
B. CT head first, because the GCS is low
C. Delay resuscitation until the CT head is reported
D. Treat the cause of hypotension first; closed head injury does not cause shock

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** In polytrauma, the primary cause of hypotension must be urgently sought and treated: patients with exsanguinating intra-abdominal injuries should be operated on and stabilised before head CT. Neurological examination is unreliable in hypotension, and closed head injury will not cause haemorrhagic shock (hypotension may occur with spinal cord injury or terminal medullary failure). The cardiopulmonary status should be stabilised before CT.

**Teaching point:** Hypotension in head injury is from another cause. Control bleeding first, then scan.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.1; Recommendation 12.

### Q19
**Topic:** Skull X-ray

A 25-year-old man with a GCS of 15 and a scalp laceration has no CT criteria. A scalp wound reveals no palpable depression.

**What does the CPG say about skull radiography?**

A. Skull X-ray replaces CT in mild head injury
B. Only if CT criteria are not met; CT is definitive
C. Skull X-ray is mandatory before CT
D. A normal skull X-ray excludes haemorrhage

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The definitive imaging for head injury is CT. A skull X-ray may be considered in mild head injury patients who do not fulfil CT criteria (Recommendation 13). A skull fracture is an important predictor of intracranial lesions, and in a GCS 15 patient external evidence of skull fracture is itself an indication for CT. Open fractures include penetrating injury, a fracture beneath a scalp laceration, or signs of skull base fracture.

**Teaching point:** CT is definitive. Skull X-ray only if CT criteria are not met, and a normal film does not exclude bleeding.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.2; Recommendation 13.

### Q20
**Topic:** Repeat CT (long clinical stem)

A 78-year-old woman on aspirin 75 mg had a mild head injury. Her initial CT was normal. She is neurologically unchanged on the ward. The registrar asks whether to repeat the CT.

**What does the CPG say?**

A. Repeat CT every 6 hours for a week
B. No further monitoring after a normal CT
C. No routine repeat; in ≥65s on aspirin, repeat at 12-24 h or watch 48 h
D. Routine repeat CT at 1 hour for all

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The CPG finds insufficient evidence to recommend routine repeat CT in mild head injury (intervention rates were higher when repeat CT was prompted by deterioration than when routine). However, in patients aged 65 or over on low-dose aspirin with an initial negative CT, routine repeat CT within 12-24 hours accurately identified secondary ICH; those not rescanned should have prolonged in-hospital observation of at least 48 hours. She is admitted anyway as she is over 65 and on an antiplatelet.

**Teaching point:** No routine repeat CT, except consider it at 12-24 h (or observe 48 h) in ≥65s on aspirin.

**Reference:** CPG Section 7.3.

### Q21
**Topic:** Cervical spine imaging (long clinical stem)

A 40-year-old woman is alert (GCS 15) after a fall from a ladder (2 metres) with neck pain and tingling in her left hand. She is stable.

Adapted from CPG Algorithm 4 (CT cervical spine within 1-2 hours if alert, stable, suspicion of cervical injury and any of): age ≥65; dangerous mechanism (fall >1 m or 5 stairs, axial load, MVC >100 km/h, rollover, ejection, motorised recreational vehicle, bicycle collision); focal peripheral neurological deficit or paraesthesia.

**What imaging does the algorithm suggest?**

A. CT cervical spine within 1-2 hours
B. Three-view cervical spine X-rays only, with no CT
C. No imaging, since she is alert
D. MRI brain only

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** She is alert and stable with suspected cervical injury, a fall from over 1 metre (dangerous mechanism) and paraesthesia (focal peripheral deficit). Either risk factor leads to CT cervical spine within 1-2 hours. Patients without such factors who can actively rotate the neck 45 degrees left and right may need no imaging, and three-view plain films apply to those not meeting CT criteria but not clearing clinically.

**Teaching point:** Alert patient with neck injury suspicion plus age ≥65, dangerous mechanism or paraesthesia: CT cervical spine within 1-2 hours.

**Reference:** CPG Algorithm 4; Recommendation 14.

### Q22
**Topic:** Cervical spine with CT head

A 52-year-old man with a GCS of 13 after a motor vehicle collision is going for CT head.

**What does the CPG recommend regarding his cervical spine?**

A. Cervical spine CT at the same setting as the head CT
B. Cervical spine X-ray after the head CT is reported
C. No cervical imaging, as the head CT is more important
D. Clinical clearance only, since he is conscious

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Head CT for a patient with a GCS below 15 should be accompanied by cervical spine CT in the same setting (Recommendation 14). A GCS below 13 or an intubated patient is also an indication for CT cervical spine within 1-2 hours. All patients with head injury are presumed to have cervical injury until excluded, and a collar is applied if injury is suspected.

**Teaching point:** GCS <15 and a head CT: do the CT cervical spine at the same sitting.

**Reference:** CPG Recommendation 14; Section 7.4.

## Section 4: Medication and Fluids

### Q23
**Topic:** Fluids

A 44-year-old man with a GCS of 10 and a head injury is hypotensive after a road crash. A junior doctor proposes 4% albumin because "it stays in the vessels".

**What does the CPG say about resuscitation fluid?**

A. Albumin is the preferred fluid
B. Hypotonic dextrose is preferred
C. Hyperosmolar colloids beat saline pre-hospital
D. Isotonic crystalloid; albumin raised mortality in severe TBI

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** Isotonic crystalloid is the preferred IV fluid in head injury (Recommendations 2 and 15). A systematic review found no evidence supporting hyperosmolar crystalloid or colloid over isotonic crystalloid for pre-hospital resuscitation, and an RCT showed albumin was associated with higher mortality and fewer favourable neurological outcomes than saline in severe TBI.

**Teaching point:** Use isotonic crystalloid in head injury. Albumin worsened outcomes in severe TBI.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.2; Recommendations 2 and 15.

### Q24
**Topic:** Osmotherapy and diuretics

A 35-year-old man with a severe head injury shows a dilated pupil and rising BP with bradycardia. The ED team wants to give mannitol. He is normotensive.

**Which statement matches the CPG?**

A. After specialist advice; never in hypotension
B. Diuretics are safe in hypotension
C. Give to every head-injured patient
D. Proven to cut mortality versus hypertonic saline

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** Mannitol, hypertonic saline or frusemide may be used to reduce intracranial pressure in head injury after consultation with a specialist, and diuretics should not be used in hypotensive patients. A Cochrane review found no significant difference in mortality between mannitol and pentobarbital or hypertonic saline, and ICP-directed treatment had a small advantage over treatment directed by neurological signs.

**Teaching point:** ICP-lowering agents: after specialist consultation, and never in hypotension.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.3; Recommendation 15.

### Q25
**Topic:** Analgesia and sedation

A 22-year-old man with a mild head injury has a painful scalp laceration and restlessness. A second patient, 40 years old with a severe head injury, is intubated and ventilated.

**Which approach fits the CPG?**

A. No analgesia, to avoid clouding the examination
B. Analgesia; short-acting titrated sedation only if agitated
C. Long-acting benzodiazepine infusion for the first patient
D. Analgesia for the intubated patient only

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Analgesia and sedation should be used in intubated and ventilated severe head injury. In mild to moderate injury, analgesia should control pain and a short-acting sedative may be offered in a titrated dose for agitation or restlessness. Oral paracetamol, NSAIDs and tramadol can be used in mild injury if there is no contraindication. Naloxone may be used for opioid reversal. Fentanyl or morphine boluses can raise ICP and lower MAP and CPP.

**Teaching point:** Treat pain in all head injury. Sedate only intubated severe cases, or short-acting titrated for agitation in mild-moderate cases.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.1; Recommendation 15.

### Q26
**Topic:** Seizure prophylaxis (long clinical stem)

A 28-year-old man has a moderate head injury (GCS 9) with a depressed skull fracture and a cortical contusion on CT. He has not had a seizure.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Start only after the first seizure
B. Prevents late seizures beyond 3 months
C. IV phenytoin within 8 hours (ideally 1 hour) with risk factors
D. Prophylaxis is never indicated in head injury

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Phenytoin should be given as prophylaxis against early post-traumatic seizure in head injury with risk factors (GCS <10, cortical contusions, depressed skull fracture, early intracranial haematoma, dural penetration, coma or post-traumatic amnesia over 24 hours, damage near the temporal sulcus). IV phenytoin within 8 hours (ideally the first hour) reduced early PTS in moderate to severe injury. Levetiracetam showed no clinically significant difference. Early PTS peaks in the first 48 hours.

**Teaching point:** Phenytoin prophylaxis (within 8 hours) for early PTS when risk factors exist. Levetiracetam is an alternative.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.4; Recommendation 15.

### Q27
**Topic:** Phenytoin dosing

A 70-kg man with a depressed skull fracture is prescribed IV phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis.

Adapted from CPG Appendix 11: phenytoin loading 15-20 mg/kg IV infusion over 30-60 minutes (not faster than 50 mg/min); maintenance IV 100 mg three times daily (or oral 300 mg at night) for a total of seven days. Levetiracetam: loading 20 mg/kg IV over 60 minutes; maintenance 1000 mg IV every 12 hours.

**Which regimen is correct?**

A. 2000 mg at 100 mg/min, then 50 mg daily
B. 300 mg over 60 minutes, then 1000 mg 12-hourly
C. 1000-1400 mg as a bolus over 2 minutes
D. 1000-1400 mg at ≤50 mg/min over 30+ min, then 100 mg TDS

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The phenytoin loading dose is 15-20 mg/kg (about 1050-1400 mg for 70 kg) as an IV infusion over 30-60 minutes at no more than 50 mg/min, to avoid hypotension, bradycardia and arrhythmia, then 100 mg IV three times daily (or 300 mg orally at night) for a total of seven days. Rapid injection is dangerous, and the 1000 mg every 12 hours regimen is levetiracetam.

**Teaching point:** Phenytoin load 15-20 mg/kg at ≤50 mg/min, then 100 mg TDS for 7 days. Levetiracetam 20 mg/kg then 1000 mg 12-hourly.

**Reference:** CPG Appendix 11.

## Section 5: Anticoagulants, Surgery and Extubation

### Q28
**Topic:** Reversal of anticoagulation (long clinical stem)

A 74-year-old man on warfarin for AF (INR 3.8) falls and hits his head. CT shows an acute subdural haematoma with mass effect. A second patient on apixaban has a traumatic intracerebral bleed.

**Which statement matches the CPG?**

A. Vitamin K reverses every anticoagulant
B. Stop and reverse at once (vitamin K for warfarin, FFP, PCC, rFVIIa)
C. Continue anticoagulation to prevent emboli
D. Reverse only after a repeat CT at 24 hours

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In intracranial bleeding, anticoagulants should be immediately stopped and reversed with vitamin K (only for warfarin), fresh frozen plasma, prothrombin complex concentrate or recombinant factor VIIa as indicated, with consultation among relevant specialties. Haemorrhagic expansion occurs within the first 24 hours and is an independent predictor of mortality, so reversal must be rapid. Pre-injury antiplatelet use also raises mortality in traumatic ICH.

**Teaching point:** Intracranial bleed on anticoagulation: stop and reverse immediately. Vitamin K applies to warfarin only.

**Reference:** CPG Section 9.1; Recommendation 16.

### Q29
**Topic:** Restarting warfarin

A 68-year-old man had a traumatic subdural haemorrhage on warfarin for a mechanical heart valve three weeks ago. He has now recovered. His physician asks about restarting warfarin.

**Which statement fits the CPG?**

A. Thromboembolism is not a concern
B. Never restart after any intracranial bleed
C. Individualise: general state, falls and bleeding risk
D. Restart on the day of the bleed

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Restarting warfarin after warfarin-associated ICH is a difficult decision. Recurrent ICH is uncommon after resumption, and the endpoints of thromboembolism and ICH did not differ significantly between restarted and non-restarted patients. The decision should consider the patient's general condition, risk of falls and systemic bleeding risk; patients without these risks may benefit from restarting. Predictors of haemorrhagic complications include younger age, traumatic cause, subdural haemorrhage and cardiac indications.

**Teaching point:** Restarting anticoagulation after ICH is individualised. Failure to resume risks thromboembolism.

**Reference:** CPG Section 9.2.

### Q31
**Topic:** Extubation criteria (long clinical stem)

A 31-year-old man was intubated in the ED for agitation and CT scanning after an alcohol-related head injury. The CT is normal. He is now calm. A.E. wants to extubate in the ED.

### Q35
**Topic:** Teleconsultation

A district hospital without a neurosurgeon uses teleradiology for a patient with mild head injury. Initially the consultant advised no transfer on clinical data, but after viewing the images he accepts the patient.

A. Clinical data alone are always enough
B. Telephone beats video and teleradiology
C. Cuts needless transfers; images plus data improve decisions
D. Teleconsultation always increases transfers

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Teleconsultation avoided unnecessary transfer in about a third (34%) of head injury patients and changed treatment at the referring hospital in 42%. ### Q37
**Topic:** Severity classification and decisions (long clinical stem)

A 47-year-old man arrives after a fall from a ladder. GCS 12 at 20 minutes post injury, improving to 14 at 2 hours. He has no seizure, vomiting, basal skull signs or focal deficit. He takes clopidogrel.

B. Moderate then mild; GCS <15 at 2 h and clopidogrel mean CT and admission

### Q38
**Topic:** Anatomic triage criteria

A paramedic assesses a 36-year-old man after a motorcycle crash. He has a GCS of 15, normal vital signs and a depressed skull fracture visible in the temporal region.

**How is he triaged?**

A. Green zone: GCS 15
B. Green zone: normal vital signs
C. Yellow zone only if the mechanism is a fall over 6 metres
D. Red zone: open or depressed skull fracture is an anatomical criterion

**Answer:** D

### Q39
**Topic:** Mechanism of injury

A 40-year-old cyclist is struck by a car at about 40 km/h and thrown 3 metres. He is alert (GCS 15) with normal vital signs and no obvious injury apart from a graze.

B. Green zone: normal vital signs and GCS

### Q43
**Topic:** Hypocarbia

A 33-year-old man with a severe head injury is ventilated after intubation. The anaesthetist sets a high minute ventilation and an ETCO2 of 28 mmHg "to reduce ICP". There are no signs of herniation.

**What does the CPG say?**

A. Aim for ETCO2 20-25 mmHg routinely
B. ETCO2 35-40 mmHg; hyperventilate only for herniation
C. ETCO2 is irrelevant to outcome

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Patients should be maintained with normal breathing rates, ETCO2 35-40 mmHg. ### Q44
**Topic:** Opioids and ICP

A 38-year-old man with a severe head injury is ventilated. The registrar gives fentanyl boluses for agitation. His ICP monitor shows a rise and his MAP falls.

**Which statement is consistent with the CPG evidence?**

A. Opioid boluses lower ICP in all patients
B. Barbiturates cut mortality in severe TBI
C. Opioid boluses raise ICP; propofol and etomidate improve ICP
D. Propofol and etomidate raise ICP

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** A systematic review found etomidate and propofol improved ICP and CPP in adults with severe TBI, whereas bolus or short-infusion opioids (fentanyl, morphine) significantly increased ICP and decreased MAP and CPP. No agent was clearly better for neurological outcome or mortality. Barbiturates gave no significant mortality or disability benefit and caused hypotension (more vasopressor use). Naloxone is safe in severe TBI.

**Teaching point:** Opioid boluses can raise ICP and lower CPP. Propofol and etomidate improved ICP and CPP.

**Reference:** CPG Section 8.1.

### Q50
**Topic:** Combined real-world management (long clinical stem)

A 76-year-old woman on warfarin (INR 2.6) for AF falls at home and strikes her forehead. She is alert (GCS 15), vomited once and has a headache. BP 148/82 mmHg, pulse 88/min irregular. The nearest hospital has a CT scanner.
